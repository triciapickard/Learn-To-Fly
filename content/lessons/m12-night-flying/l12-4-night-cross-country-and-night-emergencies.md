## The dark route and the lit route

Livermore to Watsonville is about 45 nm in a straight line, which by day is about half an hour's flying. At night the straight line crosses the Diablo Range, a black ridge of hills with no lights, no roads and no fields, and ends over Monterey Bay, the darkest area in the region. The same trip also has a lit route. Follow Interstate 680 and US 101 down the Santa Clara Valley, which is lit all the way through San Jose and Gilroy, then follow Highway 152 over Hecker Pass to Watsonville. It is a few minutes longer and it never leaves you more than a glide from a lit place to land. Most of night cross-country planning is choosing the lit route on purpose.

:::callout{type="real-world"}
In a real airplane the night cross-country is a dual flight with an instructor, over 100 nm, and it comes after the night patterns and before any solo night flying. Night emergencies are practiced at altitude with the instructor restoring power long before the ground matters. This lesson teaches the planning and the flows. Nothing makes a dark forced landing certain to be survivable, so the goal of night planning is to never need one.
:::

## Route choice: lit areas, airports and terrain

The night route is a different problem from the day route in [[l6-6-planning-a-cross-country-flight]]. By day you chose the shortest safe line and picked checkpoints along it. By night you choose the line that keeps these close:

| Keep close             | Why                                                                                                | On the KLVK–KWVI trip                                                    |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Lit areas**          | Lights are where you can see, navigate and, if the engine quits, see the ground before you hit it  | The I-680 and US 101 corridor through the Santa Clara Valley             |
| **Airports**           | A lit runway is the only guaranteed good forced-landing site at night                              | Hayward, San Jose, Reid-Hillview, South County (San Martin), Watsonville |
| **Low, known terrain** | Hills are invisible at night. You clear them by altitude and by never flying over the unknown ones | Avoid the Diablo Range direct. Cross Hecker Pass, not the ridges         |

Check the hours as well as the lights. Hayward's tower closes at 9 p.m. and Reid-Hillview's at 10 p.m., and after that their runway lights, like San Martin's and Watsonville's, are pilot-controlled on the CTAF.

Water counts as darkness. Monterey Bay, the salt ponds at the south end of San Francisco Bay and the reservoirs in the hills all look the same from the air at night, completely black. Plan your route so that a dark area is never the only thing within gliding distance.

:::callout{type="decision"}
Would you cross this dark area at this altitude? Your planned route from Gilroy to Watsonville crosses the southern end of the Santa Cruz Mountains over Hecker Pass. The pass itself is about 1,300 ft, Mount Madonna beside it rises to about 1,900 ft, the highway traffic marks the pass, and you are at 4,500 ft MSL. Now the variant: scattered clouds at 4,500 ft over the mountains. Descending to 3,500 ft to stay under them keeps you legal but leaves about 1,600 ft between you and the ridge in the dark. The good answers are "climb above the scattered layer if it stays scattered and I can see the lights through it", "fly south down 101 to Salinas and come up the coast", or "turn back to Gilroy". The bad answer is "it is only a few miles."
:::

## Checkpoints at night

Day checkpoints are shapes: a river bend, a lake, a railroad crossing a road. At night most of those vanish. Night checkpoints are lights with a pattern you can recognize.

| Good at night                                              | Why it works                                   | Poor at night                  | Why it fails                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| An airport rotating beacon                                 | Unique flash pattern, visible from a long way  | A river or creek               | Invisible unless the moon is on it |
| A lit highway, especially an interchange or a bend         | Continuous line of lights with a shape         | A railroad                     | Unlit                              |
| A city or town edge, where lights stop and darkness starts | Easy to see, and a town's shape is distinctive | A small town among larger ones | Blends into the glow               |
| A lit tower or a cluster of obstruction lights             | Red lights in a known place on the sectional   | A lake or reservoir            | Black, like everything else        |
| A large lit site: stadium, refinery, port                  | Bright and alone                               | Hills and ridgelines           | Shapes you cannot see              |

The sectional still works at night. A highway that is a checkpoint by day is a lit line by night, and towers are marked with their height. Use the G1000 to confirm what you find outside. Watch the MFD map show the checkpoint approaching, then look outside and find it. If the lights outside do not match the map, you are lost, and being lost at night is serious. The GPS method from [[l6-5-gps-navigation-with-the-g1000]] is your backup for every leg.

:::quiz{id="l12-4-q1" type="single"}
Which of these is the best checkpoint on a night cross-country?

- [ ] A bend in a river
- [ ] A railroad crossing a dirt road
- [x] A lit highway interchange next to an airport beacon
- [ ] A small hill between two towns

---

Lights with a recognizable pattern make night checkpoints. A highway interchange is a shaped line of lights, and an airport beacon is unmistakable. Rivers, railroads and hills are invisible after dark.
:::

## Altitude and terrain

Fly higher at night than you would by day. Extra height keeps terrain you cannot see below you, lets your glide reach more lights, and improves radio and GPS reception. To plan the altitude:

1. Read the **maximum elevation figure** (MEF) for every sectional quadrangle along the route. It is the highest thing in the whole quadrangle, obstacles included, so it can be far above the ground you cross. It tells you where the high ground is, and the terrain within a few miles of your course gives the number for the next step.
2. Add at least 2,000 ft over hills and mountains at night. That is a Learn to Fly planning minimum, not a regulation. The day habit is 1,000 ft, and night calls for more margin.
3. Round up to the correct VFR cruising altitude for your magnetic course: above 3,000 ft AGL, odd thousands plus 500 ft on easterly courses (000–179), even thousands plus 500 ft on westerly courses (180–359).

| Leg                                 | Magnetic course | Highest terrain near the route         | Night minimum | Planned cruise |
| ----------------------------------- | --------------- | -------------------------------------- | ------------- | -------------- |
| KLVK → Gilroy (I-680, US 101)       | ~150            | ~2,600 ft (Mission and Monument Peaks) | 4,600 ft      | 5,500 ft MSL   |
| Gilroy → KWVI (SR 152, Hecker Pass) | ~235            | ~1,900 ft (Mount Madonna)              | 3,900 ft      | 4,500 ft MSL   |
| KWVI → Gilroy (Hecker Pass)         | ~055            | ~1,900 ft                              | 3,900 ft      | 5,500 ft MSL   |
| Gilroy → KLVK (US 101, I-680)       | ~330            | ~2,600 ft                              | 4,600 ft      | 6,500 ft MSL   |

A road route bends, so the course in the table is the leg's overall direction. Where a leg changes direction by more than a few degrees, check the rule again for each straight segment.

Descend at the end only when the destination's lights are in sight and you know which lights they are. Many night accidents are an on-course descent, in the last 10 miles, into a hill the pilot never thought about.

## Fuel: the 45-minute night reserve

By day the rule is that you land with at least 30 minutes of fuel at normal cruise still in the tanks. **At night the required reserve is 45 minutes.** As with everything else in this module, you have fewer options at night, so you carry more margin. Diversions take longer when you can only divert to lit airports, and a forced landing from fuel exhaustion in the dark is one of the worst situations in general aviation.

Plan it the same way you did in [[l6-6-planning-a-cross-country-flight]] and [[l9-4-leaning-the-mixture-and-fuel-management]]: start and taxi, climb, cruise for each leg, then the reserve. At the simple planning figure of 10 GPH, 45 minutes is 7.5 gallons that you must still be carrying when you shut down at your destination. Many night pilots use a personal minimum of 1 hour. The Skyhawk carries {{aircraft.fuelCapacity}}, so for a 2-hour round trip to Watsonville fuel is never the limit. The point is to build the habit.

## The dark-area engine failure

The flow is the one from [[l5-4-engine-failure-and-forced-landings]]: airspeed, best field, checklist, declare, execute. At night the hard step is "best field", because you cannot see any. Instead, **glide toward lights**:

- Pitch and trim for {{vspeed.vg}} immediately.
- Turn toward the nearest airport if it is in range. The glide ring below shows what "in range" means. At {{aircraft.glideRatio}}, 4,500 ft MSL over the valley gives you roughly 6 nm.
- If no airport is reachable, turn toward lit, flat ground: a lit road, the edge of a town, a lit industrial lot. A lit road has wires and traffic, but you can see them, and you cannot see the ditch in the dark field next to it.
- **Avoid the black.** Dark areas are water, trees or hills. The darkest patch is never the answer. The AFH allows one exception: if you know the dark ground is flat and open, such as fields you flew over by day, an unlit field can be the better choice. Unknown dark ground never is.
- Run the restart items, set 7700, and tell someone where you are.
- Near the ground, turn on the landing light. The old joke is "if you don't like what you see, turn it off." The serious advice is to land as slowly as you can, wings level, into whatever you can see.

## Electrical failure at night

By day an alternator failure is an inconvenience. At night it takes away your panel lighting and the lights that let others see you once the battery runs down. The signs are a LOW VOLTS annunciation and the ammeter or load meter showing a discharge, with the voltage falling. The flow is short, and you should know it from memory:

:::callout{type="memory"}
**Alternator failure (night)**

1. Recognize: LOW VOLTS, discharge, falling voltage.
2. ALT circuit breaker and ALT switch: check, and attempt one reset.
3. Not restored: ALT off, and shed load now.
4. Keep: COM1, transponder, position lights, one G1000 display, the flashlight in your hand.
5. Drop: landing and taxi lights, strobes, the second display, anything else drawing power.
6. Tell ATC, and land as soon as practical at the nearest suitable **lit** airport. Turn the landing light on for the flare only.

:::

Shedding load buys time. A Skyhawk battery alone may give you about 30 minutes with the load trimmed, far less with everything on, and you want the radio and the transponder to be the last things to stop working. Get the flashlight out before the screens go, not after. [[l14-1-electrical-failures]] covers the full flow, the battery math and what the G1000 does as the voltage drops. This lesson covers the night-specific version. Get the flashlight out first, keep the lights that let others see you, and land at a lit airport.

## Practice and next steps

To carry forward:

1. Route over lights, between airports, above known terrain, and never descend into the dark on course.
2. The night reserve is 45 minutes, and a personal minimum of 1 hour costs little.
3. Engine failure: glide toward lights. Electrical failure: flashlight, shed load, nearest lit airport.
