## A dark bay and a bright valley

Livermore to Watsonville is about 45 nm in a straight line, which by day is a pleasant hour's flying. At night the straight line crosses the Diablo Range, a black ridge of hills with no lights, no roads and no fields, and ends over the darkest thing in the Bay Area: Monterey Bay. The same trip has a bright version. Follow Interstate 680 and US 101 down the Santa Clara Valley, a river of lights through San Jose and Gilroy, then follow Highway 152 over Hecker Pass to Watsonville. It is a few minutes longer and it never leaves you more than a glide from a lit place to land. Planning a night cross-country is mostly choosing the bright version on purpose.

:::callout{type="real-world"}
In a real airplane the night cross-country is a dual flight with an instructor, over 100 nm, and it comes after the night patterns and before any solo night flying. Night emergencies are practiced at altitude with the instructor restoring power long before the ground matters. This lesson teaches the planning and the flows; it does not make a dark forced landing survivable, and nothing does for certain. The point of night planning is to never need one.
:::

## Route choice: lit areas, airports and terrain

The night route is a different problem from the day route in [[l6-6-planning-a-cross-country-flight]]. By day you chose the shortest safe line and picked checkpoints along it. By night you choose the line that keeps three things close:

| Keep close             | Why                                                                                                | On the KLVK–KWVI trip                                                    |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Lit areas**          | Lights are where you can see, navigate and, if the engine quits, see the ground before you hit it  | The I-680 and US 101 corridor through the Santa Clara Valley             |
| **Airports**           | A lit runway is the only guaranteed good forced-landing site at night                              | Hayward, San Jose, Reid-Hillview, South County (San Martin), Watsonville |
| **Low, known terrain** | Hills are invisible at night; you clear them by altitude and by never flying over the unknown ones | Avoid the Diablo Range direct; cross Hecker Pass, not the ridges         |

Water counts as darkness. Monterey Bay, the salt ponds at the south end of San Francisco Bay and the reservoirs in the hills all look the same from the air at night: nothing. Plan your route so that "nothing" is never the only thing within gliding distance.

:::callout{type="decision"}
Would you cross this dark area at this altitude? Your planned route from Gilroy to Watsonville crosses the southern end of the Santa Cruz Mountains over Hecker Pass. The terrain rises to about 2,000 ft, the pass is lit by the highway, and you are at 4,500 ft MSL. Now the variant: scattered clouds at 4,500 ft over the mountains. Descending to 3,500 ft to stay under them keeps you legal but leaves 1,500 ft between you and the ridge in the dark. The good answers are "climb above the scattered layer if it stays scattered and I can see the lights through it", "fly south down 101 to Salinas and come up the coast", or "turn back to Gilroy". The bad answer is "it is only a few miles."
:::

:::callout{type="verify"}

:::

## Checkpoints at night

Day checkpoints are shapes: a river bend, a lake, a railroad crossing a road. At night most of those vanish. Night checkpoints are **lights with a pattern you can recognize**.

| Good at night                                              | Why it works                                    | Poor at night                  | Why it fails                       |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------ | ---------------------------------- |
| An airport rotating beacon                                 | Unique flash pattern, visible 20 nm or more     | A river or creek               | Invisible unless the moon is on it |
| A lit highway, especially an interchange or a bend         | Continuous line of lights with a shape          | A railroad                     | Unlit                              |
| A city or town edge, where lights stop and darkness starts | Easy to see; the shape of a town is distinctive | A small town among larger ones | Blends into the glow               |
| A lit tower or a cluster of obstruction lights             | Red lights in a known place on the sectional    | A lake or reservoir            | Black, like everything else        |
| A large lit site: stadium, refinery, port                  | Bright and alone                                | Hills and ridgelines           | Shapes you cannot see              |

Two tricks. First, **the sectional still works**: a highway that is a checkpoint by day is a lit line by night, and towers are marked with their height. Second, use the G1000 as a check, not a crutch. Watch the MFD map show the checkpoint approaching, then look outside and find it. If the lights outside do not match the map, you are lost, and at night lost is serious. The GPS method from [[l6-5-gps-navigation-with-the-g1000]] is your backup for every leg.

:::quiz{id="l12-4-q1" type="single"}
Which of these is the best checkpoint on a night cross-country?

- [ ] A bend in a river
- [ ] A railroad crossing a dirt road
- [x] A lit highway interchange next to an airport beacon
- [ ] A small hill between two towns

---

Lights with a recognizable pattern make night checkpoints. A highway interchange is a shaped line of lights, and an airport beacon is unmistakable. Rivers, railroads and hills are invisible after dark.
:::

## Altitude and terrain

Fly higher at night than you would by day. Height buys three things: terrain you cannot see stays below you, your glide reaches more lights, and radio and GPS reception improve. The planning method:

1. Read the **maximum elevation figure** (MEF) for every sectional quadrangle along the route.
2. Add at least 2,000 ft over hills and mountains at night (1,000 ft is the day habit; night deserves more).
3. Round up to the correct **VFR cruising altitude** for your course: above 3,000 ft AGL, odd thousands plus 500 ft on easterly courses (000–179), even thousands plus 500 ft on westerly courses (180–359).

| Leg                         | Course | Highest terrain nearby | Night minimum | Planned cruise |
| --------------------------- | ------ | ---------------------- | ------------- | -------------- |
| KLVK → Gilroy (valley)      | ~190   | ~2,000 ft (hills east) | 4,000 ft      | 4,500 ft MSL   |
| Gilroy → KWVI (Hecker Pass) | ~250   | ~2,000 ft              | 4,000 ft      | 4,500 ft MSL   |
| KWVI → KLVK (return)        | ~010   | ~2,000 ft              | 4,000 ft      | 5,500 ft MSL   |

Descend at the end only when the destination's lights are in sight and you know which lights they are. Many night accidents are an on-course descent into a hill that was never on the pilot's mind, in the last 10 miles.

## Fuel: the 45-minute night reserve

By day the rule is that you land with at least 30 minutes of fuel at normal cruise still in the tanks. **At night the required reserve is 45 minutes.** The reason is the same as everything else in this module: at night your options are fewer, so you carry more margin. Diversions take longer when you can only divert to lit airports, and a forced landing from fuel exhaustion in the dark is close to the worst situation general aviation offers.

Plan it the same way you did in [[l6-6-planning-a-cross-country-flight]] and [[l9-4-leaning-the-mixture-and-fuel-management]]: start and taxi, climb, cruise for each leg, then the reserve. At the simple planning figure of 10 GPH, 45 minutes is 7.5 gallons that you must still be carrying when you shut down at your destination. Many night pilots use a personal minimum of 1 hour. The Skyhawk carries {{aircraft.fuelCapacity}}, so for a 2-hour round trip to Watsonville fuel is never the constraint. The habit is.

Confirm the 10 GPH planning figure against `aircraft.yaml` ({{aircraft.fuelBurn}}) and the W20 nav-log night mode once it ships.

---

The night reserve is 45 minutes at normal cruise. 45 minutes at 10 GPH is 7.5 gallons. Most pilots round that up to 1 hour, 10 gallons, as a personal minimum.
:::

## The dark-area engine failure

The flow is the one from [[l5-4-engine-failure-and-forced-landings]]: airspeed, best field, checklist, declare, execute. At night the hard step is "best field", because you cannot see any. The rule becomes **glide toward lights**:

- Pitch and trim for {{vspeed.vg}} immediately.
- Turn toward the nearest airport if it is in range. The glide ring below shows what "in range" means: at {{aircraft.glideRatio}}, 4,500 ft MSL over the valley gives you roughly 6 nm.
- If no airport is reachable, turn toward lit, flat ground: a lit road, the edge of a town, a lit industrial lot. A lit road has wires and traffic, but you can see them, and you cannot see the ditch in the dark field next to it.
- **Avoid the black.** Dark areas are water, trees or hills. The darkest patch is never the answer.
- Run the restart items, set 7700, and tell someone where you are.
- Near the ground, turn on the landing light. The old joke is "if you don't like what you see, turn it off." The serious version: land as slowly as you can, wings level, into whatever you can see.

## Electrical failure at night

By day an alternator failure is an inconvenience. At night it takes away the thing that lets you see the panel and the thing that lets others see you, on a timer set by the battery. The signs are a **LOW VOLTS** annunciation and the ammeter or load meter showing a discharge, with the voltage falling. From there the flow is short and it belongs in your memory:

:::callout{type="memory"}
**Alternator failure (night)**

1. Recognize: LOW VOLTS, discharge, falling voltage.
2. ALT circuit breaker and ALT switch: check, and attempt one reset.
3. Not restored: ALT off, and shed load now.
4. Keep: COM1, transponder, position lights, one G1000 display, the flashlight in your hand.
5. Drop: landing and taxi lights, strobes, the second display, anything else drawing power.
6. Tell ATC, and land as soon as practical at the nearest suitable **lit** airport. Turn the landing light on for the flare only.

:::

Shedding load is about buying time. A Skyhawk battery alone may give you about 30 minutes with the load trimmed, far less with everything on, and you want the radio and the transponder to be the last things to die. Get the flashlight out before the screens go, not after. [[l14-1-electrical-failures]] covers the full flow, the battery math and what the G1000 does as the voltage drops; this lesson's job is the night-specific version: the flashlight first, the lights that keep you seen stay on, and the destination is lit.

## Practice and next steps

Three things to carry forward:

1. Route over lights, between airports, above known terrain, and never descend into the dark on course.
2. The night reserve is 45 minutes; a personal minimum of 1 hour is cheap.
3. Engine failure: glide toward lights. Electrical failure: flashlight, shed load, nearest lit airport.

**Practice.** This lesson feeds the `memory-items` deck with the night alternator-failure flow, and the `airspace-and-minimums` deck with the 45-minute reserve.
