## A confident wrong answer

A pilot without an instrument rating flies into cloud. The airplane enters a gentle bank. After a few seconds the bank feels like level flight. The nose drops and the airspeed rises; the pilot feels the pull of the descent and eases back, which tightens the turn. The altimeter unwinds faster. The pilot now feels, with absolute certainty, that the wings are level and the airplane is diving, and pulls harder.

This is the **graveyard spiral**, and it has killed a great many capable pilots. Nothing was wrong with the airplane. The pilot's balance system delivered a confident wrong answer, and the pilot believed it over the instruments. This lesson is about where those wrong answers come from.

:::callout{type="real-world"}
In a real airplane, instrument students experience disorientation on purpose: an instructor has you close your eyes under the view-limiting device while the airplane turns, or a rotating chair at a safety seminar makes your body insist it has stopped when it has not. The sim can teach the facts, but not the feeling, and the feeling is the point. A real pilot needs that training with an instructor before trusting the instruments becomes a reflex.
:::

## The inner ear

Your sense of balance lives in two sets of organs in each inner ear, and both were designed for walking, not flying.

| Organ               | Senses                          | How it is fooled in flight                                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Semicircular canals | Rotation (roll, pitch, yaw)     | Fluid catches up after about 15–20 s of steady turn, so the turn feels like level |
| Otolith organs      | Linear acceleration and gravity | Cannot tell "pushed back by acceleration" from "tilted nose up"                   |

The canals are three fluid-filled loops. When your head starts to rotate, the fluid lags and bends tiny hairs, and you feel the turn. In a steady turn the fluid catches up and the turn vanishes from your senses. Stop the turn and the fluid overshoots: now you feel a turn the other way that is not happening.

The otoliths are small weights on hairs. Gravity pulls them down; so does acceleration, in a different direction. Your brain adds the two and reports a single "down". Accelerate hard and "down" tilts backward, which feels exactly like pitching up.

On the ground, your eyes correct both every moment. In cloud, they have nothing to correct with, unless they are on the instruments.

## The illusions

| Illusion         | What you do                                    | What you feel                             | What you might wrongly do           |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| The leans        | Roll slowly into a bank, then level quickly    | Leaning the other way; level feels banked | Roll back into the original bank    |
| Coriolis         | Move your head sharply during a prolonged turn | Tumbling, rotation in a new axis          | Make a large, wrong control input   |
| Graveyard spiral | Hold a steady turn; the nose drops             | Level flight with a descent               | Pull back, tightening the spiral    |
| Somatogravic     | Accelerate quickly (takeoff, go-around)        | Nose pitching up                          | Push the nose down, into the ground |
| Inversion        | Level off abruptly from a climb                | Tumbling backward                         | Push forward, which makes it worse  |

The leans is the most common and the least dangerous: most instrument pilots feel it and simply fly the attitude indicator through it. The graveyard spiral and the somatogravic illusion are the killers. The somatogravic illusion is especially cruel right after takeoff at night or into cloud, when the airplane is accelerating, the horizon is gone and the ground is close.

:::quiz{id="l17-5-q1" type="single"}
You have been in a steady right turn in cloud for a minute and the airplane has started to descend. You feel level and diving. What is happening?

- [ ] The leans
- [x] A graveyard spiral
- [ ] The somatogravic illusion
- [ ] Inversion illusion

---

A prolonged turn stops being felt, so the descending turn feels like a wings-level dive. Pulling back tightens it. Level the wings on the attitude indicator first, then ease out, as in [[l17-3-unusual-attitude-recovery]].
:::

:::quiz{id="l17-5-q2" type="single"}
Departing into a low overcast at night, you add full power and accelerate. You feel the nose pitching up sharply, but the attitude indicator shows a normal climb. Which illusion is this, and what do you do?

- [ ] The leans; roll level
- [ ] Coriolis; hold your head still
- [x] Somatogravic; hold the climb attitude on the attitude indicator
- [ ] Inversion; add power

---

Acceleration tilts your sense of "down" backward, which feels like pitching up. Pilots who believe it push the nose down toward the ground. Hold the attitude the instrument shows.
:::

## Why the sim cannot make you feel them, and why that matters

A desktop sim does not move. Your inner ear sits in a chair that is not accelerating, so it reports "still", which is close enough to "level" that nothing fights your eyes. Every maneuver in this module feels easier than it will in an airplane. That gap matters most for the learner who later flies for real.

:::callout{type="safety"}
If you fly a real airplane, nothing in Learn-To-Fly's instrument course makes you safe in cloud. Your sim-trained scan will meet a body that insists, with total confidence, that the instruments are wrong. Only training with an instructor under a view-limiting device, and the rating that follows, prepares you for that argument. A VFR pilot who meets cloud makes the 180° turn from [[l11-6-the-go-no-go-decision-and-vfr-into-imc]] and does not test the question.
:::

What the sim can build is the habit that wins the argument: eyes on the attitude indicator, small inputs, trust the gauges. You can also reduce the odds:

- Avoid sudden head movements in cloud; move your eyes, not your head.
- Do not fly when tired, ill, dehydrated or hung over; all of these make the inner ear less reliable.
- Keep the scan going through every radio call and chart look.
- Use the autopilot when the workload rises, and keep monitoring it.
- Never trust a sensation over an instrument that is working.

:::quiz{id="l17-5-q3" type="multi"}
Which of these reduce your risk of spatial disorientation in IMC?

- [x] Keeping your head still and moving only your eyes
- [x] Flying the attitude indicator, not the feeling
- [ ] Closing your eyes briefly to reset your balance
- [x] Not flying when tired or unwell

---

Head movements trigger Coriolis, and your balance organs are less reliable when you are tired or sick. Closing your eyes removes the only input that is telling the truth.
:::

## Practice and next steps

Three things to carry forward:

1. The canals stop sensing a steady turn after about 15–20 seconds; the otoliths confuse acceleration with pitch.
2. The leans, Coriolis, graveyard spiral, somatogravic and inversion illusions all come from those two facts.
3. The only cure is to trust the instruments, and the only way to make that a reflex in a real airplane is real training.

**Practice.** The illusions table is the thing to re-read before any instrument session; the quizzes here are the drill.
