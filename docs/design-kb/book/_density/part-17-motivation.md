# Density test: Part XVII, Motivation and Gamification

Section chosen: "The structure of a reward decides its effect" (the reward-contingency section of the chapter "Rewards and the overjustification effect"), drafted from the notes for `deci1999meta`, `lepper1973undermining` and `hattie2007power`.

## Draft 1: terse (about 150 words)

Rewards do not have one effect. A meta-analysis of 128 experiments found that expected, tangible rewards reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation, while positive verbal feedback raised it [@deci1999meta]. Engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards all undermined free-choice behavior (d = -0.40, -0.36, -0.28). Positive feedback raised free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31). The effect is a property of the reward's structure. Lepper's nursery-school study shows the same: children who drew to win an expected award later chose drawing less than children who got the same award unexpectedly [@lepper1973undermining]. The debate with Cameron and Pierce means the effect should be read as moderate and conditional.

*In LTF:* do not pay for lesson completion; keep tiers informational and after the attempt.

## Draft 2: medium (about 400 words)

"Do rewards hurt motivation?" has no single answer, and the question is badly posed. Deci, Koestner and Ryan pooled 128 experiments and argued that the effect of a reward depends on what it is contingent on and on whether the person experiences it as controlling or as information about competence [@deci1999meta]. They sorted rewards by contingency: task-noncontingent (given regardless), engagement-contingent (for doing the task), completion-contingent (for finishing it) and performance-contingent (for reaching a standard). On interesting tasks, engagement-, completion- and performance-contingent rewards all reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28), as did tangible and expected rewards in general. Positive verbal feedback did the opposite, raising both free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31).

The founding experiment explains why contingency matters. Lepper, Greene and Nisbett showed preschoolers a "Good Player Award" and asked whether they wanted to draw to win it; a second group got the same award without warning; a third got none [@lepper1973undermining]. One to two weeks later the expected-award group spent 8.59 percent of free time drawing, against 16.73 and 18.09 percent for the others. The unexpected-award group received the identical object, so the harm comes from drawing in order to get it.

The finding is contested in degree. Cameron and Pierce argued the effect is small and narrow; Deci and colleagues replied that those analyses pooled dull and interesting tasks. The fair reading is a real, moderate effect concentrated in expected, tangible, contingent rewards for tasks people already like, and the d values come from short laboratory studies with children and students.

*In LTF:* a learner who arrives loving aviation is the case where undermining is most plausible. Never attach a currency to finishing a lesson; keep tiers as performance information delivered after the flight.

## Draft 3: full (about 850 words)

"Do rewards hurt motivation?" is a question with no single answer, and the reason is the most useful idea in this whole literature for a designer. Deci, Koestner and Ryan pooled 128 experiments, in a field where four earlier meta-analyses had disagreed, and argued that averaging all "rewards" together hides the variable that matters: the structure of the reward [@deci1999meta]. Cognitive evaluation theory predicts that a reward changes intrinsic motivation through two channels, how much it feels like control and how much it tells the person about their competence. Which channel dominates depends on what the reward is contingent on and how it is delivered.

The authors define four contingencies. Task-noncontingent rewards are given regardless of what the person does. Engagement-contingent rewards are given for doing the task at all. Completion-contingent rewards are given for finishing it. Performance-contingent rewards are given for reaching a standard or doing well. They predicted engagement-contingent rewards would be the most clearly controlling, because they say "you did this to get paid" with no information attached, and that performance-contingent rewards would depend on whether they carried competence information and how pressuring the climate was.

On interesting tasks (the primary analyses set dull ones aside, since undermining concerns activities people already find interesting), the results were consistent with that structure. Engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards all significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation, with d = -0.40, -0.36 and -0.28. All tangible rewards and all expected rewards undermined it as well. Engagement- and completion-contingent rewards also lowered self-reported interest, though less (d = -0.15 and -0.17). Positive verbal feedback went the other way, enhancing free-choice behavior (d = 0.33) and self-reported interest (d = 0.31). Tangible rewards were more harmful for children than for college students. The paper also cites two studies in which positive feedback given in a controlling style produced less intrinsic motivation than the same feedback given informationally, so even praise can be structured badly.

The founding experiment shows the mechanism at small scale. Lepper, Greene and Nisbett selected nursery-school children who already liked drawing with markers [@lepper1973undermining]. One group was shown a "Good Player Award" card and asked whether they wanted to draw to win it; one received the same card afterward without warning; one received nothing. Seven to 14 days later, observers blind to condition recorded free-choice time with the markers: 8.59 percent for the expected-award group, 16.73 percent for no award and 18.09 percent for the unexpected award. Pictures drawn under expected reward were also rated lower in quality (2.18 against 2.85 and 2.69 on a five-point scale) even though children drew a similar number. The unexpected-award group is the decisive control: same object, no harm. What hurt was doing the activity as a means to the award.

Two cautions keep this from becoming dogma. First, the literature was openly disputed. Cameron and Pierce (1994) and Eisenberger and Cameron (1996) concluded that rewards rarely harm intrinsic motivation; Deci and colleagues argued that those analyses collapsed across moderators and pooled dull with interesting tasks, and Lepper, Henderlong and Gingras sided broadly with the moderator view; the exchange continued after 1999. Second, the effect sizes are small to moderate and come from short laboratory sessions with children and students, so they are not predictions for an adult hobbyist. The defensible reading is that undermining is real under specific conditions (an already interesting activity, an expected tangible reward framed as payment) and absent or reversed for informational feedback and unexpected recognition. Hattie and Timperley add a framing that helps in practice: tangible rewards are contingencies, not feedback, because they carry almost no information about the task [@hattie2007power].

*In LTF:* the learner who opens `LessonPage` because they love aviation is exactly the person in whom undermining is most plausible. Never attach a currency, point total or unlock to finishing a lesson or opening a page. The Gold, Silver and Bronze tiers are performance-contingent, so their safety depends on keeping them informational: each tier is tied to a stated criterion in the brief, and the `ResultView` reports it per criterion after the flight, not as a prize promised in advance.

## Verdict

The terse draft keeps the headline numbers but drops the definitions of the four contingencies, the quality result, the dull-task exclusion, the controlling-praise finding and the reason the unexpected-award group matters. Without them a reader cannot apply the rule to a new mechanic, which is the point of the book. The medium draft keeps the mechanism and the caveat but loses the self-report effects, the child versus student difference and the controlling-feedback studies. The full draft keeps every finding and caveat in the notes, and its extra length is substance, not restatement. It does carry some risk of repeating the debate in every chapter.

**Chosen density: full**, with two disciplines. The reward debate and the "lab samples, not predictions" caveat are stated fully once, where they belong, and later chapters point back to that chapter by title instead of repeating them. Each section ends in an application to a named LTF surface, and the length comes from more findings and more product decisions, not from summary.
