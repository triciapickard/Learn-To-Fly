# Density test: Part XII, Information Visualization

Section tested: "Where the axis starts" (the zero-baseline question), from the chapter on graphical integrity. Sources in the notes: Correll, Bertini and Franconeri (2020); Franconeri et al. (2021); Wainer (1984); Tufte (1983); Cairo (2016); Few (2006).

## Draft A: terse (about 150 words)

Bars encode value by length, so a bar axis that starts above zero exaggerates differences. Correll, Bertini and Franconeri ran three small crowdsourced experiments: truncation had a large effect on perceived severity for bars and lines alike, and broken-axis or gradient cues did not reduce it [@correll2020truncating]. Value estimates were also worse at the most truncated baseline. Franconeri and colleagues report the zero rule as contested, since zero can make small effects illegible when data sit far from it [@franconeri2021science]. Wainer had already mocked "honesty requires that we start the scale at zero" as a way to hide change [@wainer1984display].

Rule: start bars at zero; let lines and dots start elsewhere only with units and a drawn reference band.

In LTF: `ProgressBar` already starts at zero. Any future score-history chart must too.

## Draft B: medium (about 400 words)

A bar encodes its value twice: by where its end falls and by how long it is. When the axis starts above zero, the end stays honest but the length lies, and length is what the eye compares. Correll, Bertini and Franconeri tested this in three crowdsourced experiments with 25 to 39 analyzed participants each [@correll2020truncating]. In the first, truncating the axis to start at 25 or 50 percent of the range had a large effect on how severe a change looked, and the effect was the same for bar and line charts. In the second, two common "honesty cues" (a broken axis and a bar that fades out at the bottom) did not reduce the exaggeration. In the third, people estimated the trend about as well whatever the baseline, but estimated single values worse at the 25 percent baseline. The bias, they conclude, is visual and survives correct reading.

They do not conclude that every chart must start at zero. Their argument is that the right axis depends on the size of effect the designer means to show. Franconeri and colleagues reach the same place: a non-zero axis can make viewers overestimate a difference, but a zero axis can make a meaningful effect invisible when data sit far from zero, and designers disagree [@franconeri2021science]. Tufte advised a baseline that shows the data rather than reaching down to zero for time series, and Cairo advises zero for bars but not for lines, as Correll and colleagues summarize them [@correll2020truncating]. Wainer, satirically, showed that "start at zero" can itself hide growth [@wainer1984display].

The workable rule is split by encoding. Bars start at zero, with no break marks. Lines and dot plots may use a tighter range when the meaningful variation is narrow, if the axis carries units and the meaningful band is drawn on the chart.

In LTF, `ProgressBar` and `ProgressRing` start at zero by construction (`clampPercent`). The risk is a future score-history view in the dashboard: a bar chart of attempt percentages starting at 50 would make a small gain look dramatic, and in a motivational product that inflates confidence. Plot from zero, or print the change as a number beside the picture.

## Draft C: full (about 850 words)

A bar encodes its value twice: by the position of its end against the axis and by its length from the baseline. When the axis starts above zero, the end position stays honest but the length does not, and length is what a viewer compares when two bars stand side by side. This is the oldest integrity complaint in the field. Tufte's first principle of graphical integrity asks that the physical representation of numbers be directly proportional to the quantities represented, and his Lie Factor measures how far a graphic departs from that [@tufte1983visual]. A truncated bar is a Lie Factor greater than one by design.

The experimental evidence is more recent and more interesting than the rule. Correll, Bertini and Franconeri ran three crowdsourced experiments [@correll2020truncating]. In the first, 39 analyzed participants rated the severity of changes shown in bar and line charts whose axes started at 0, 25 or 50 percent of the range. Truncation had a large effect on rated severity, with all three levels differing, and chart type made no significant difference: lines exaggerated as much as bars. In the second, 31 participants saw standard bars, broken-axis bars and bars whose bottoms faded out in a gradient. The two cues meant to warn viewers did not reduce perceived severity. In the third, 25 participants estimated values: error in estimating the trend did not change with truncation, but error in estimating individual values rose at the 25 percent baseline, and severity ratings still followed the truncation. The authors' conclusion is that the bias is visual. A viewer can read the numbers correctly and still feel the exaggeration.

The samples are small (25 to 39 people), the stimuli were context-free, only two warning cues were tried, and the measure was a severity rating, not a decision. No independent replication was found. The paper should be read as strong on one point (warning cues do not fix truncated bars) and suggestive on the rest.

The same authors refuse a simple binary of deceptive and truthful axes. They summarize the guidance landscape: Huff called non-zero axes "gee whiz" graphs; Brinton said a scale should normally include zero; Tufte advised, for time series, a baseline that shows the data rather than wasting space reaching to zero; Cairo advises zero for length-encoded bars but not for lines; and the Draco tool hard-codes the bar rule [@correll2020truncating]. Their advice is to consider the size of the meaningful effects and variation the chart is meant to communicate. Franconeri and colleagues, reviewing the wider literature, agree: failing to start at zero can cause viewers to overestimate differences, but a zero baseline can make effect sizes illegible when data sit far from zero, and designers disagree on whether the rule is absolute; one approach they cite spans 0.75 standard deviations either side of the mean [@franconeri2021science]. Wainer, earlier and with satire, showed the other edge: appealing to "honesty requires that we start the scale at zero" can be used to blow up a scale until real growth disappears [@wainer1984display]. Few gives the bar case its plainest example: a truncated axis that makes revenue look four times costs when it is under double [@few2006information].

The rule that survives all of this is split by encoding, not by a moral category. Where length carries the value (bars, filled areas, stacked columns, a progress fill), the axis starts at zero and no break mark is offered as an excuse. Where position alone carries the value (a line, a dot plot, a deviation gauge centered on a target), the axis may start elsewhere, on two conditions: the axis is labeled with its units and values, and the meaningful band (a target range, a tolerance, a previous best) is drawn on the chart so that the viewer can see how big the effect is relative to what matters.

In LTF, `ProgressBar` and `ProgressRing` start at zero by construction: `clampPercent` maps value over max to 0–100 and the fill is a width or a dash offset from the start. That is correct and should be kept. The exposure is in what has not been built. A score-history view on `DashboardPage` or in `HistoryTab` that plotted attempt percentages as bars from 50 would make a two-point gain look like a leap, and in a product whose result card already celebrates a tier with `tier-pop`, that exaggeration would feed confidence the flying has not earned. Plot such bars from zero, or use a dot strip on a labeled 0–100 axis with the learner's previous best drawn as a reference line, and print the change as a number ("up 4 points") beside it. A widget that shows deviation from a target (the kind of display a lesson might use to explain holding a speed) is centered on the target by design and is not zero-based; it must label its scale and draw the acceptable band.

## Verdict

Draft A keeps the conclusion but drops the sample sizes, the split between trend and value estimation, the limits of the study, Few's worked example, the 0.75 standard deviation approach, and the reasoning that makes the split rule defensible. A reviewer could not check it.

Draft B keeps the main findings and the split rule, but loses the study's limits (which decide the grade), the guidance landscape that shows where the consensus sits, and the specific LTF exposure (the `tier-pop` result card amplifying the bias).

Draft C keeps every finding, number and caveat in the notes for this topic, plus the practical rule and its LTF consequence, and it does not repeat itself: each paragraph adds a source or a condition. Its length comes from substance.

Chosen density: **full (Draft C)**, about 800 words per substantial section, with the discipline that each paragraph must add a finding, a number, a caveat or an application. Shorter sections where the notes are thin (a single craft source) stay shorter rather than padding.
