## Draft 1: terse (about 150 words)

Four decades of menu experiments agree that depth costs more than breadth. Miller found a U-shaped curve over 64 items, fastest at two levels of eight [@miller1981depth]. Kiger found time and errors rose with depth, with a 4-then-16 tree fastest [@kiger1984depth]. On the Web, Larson and Czerwinski found two levels of 16 or 32 beat three levels of eight: 36 and 46 seconds against 58, with lostness 0.63 in the deep tree [@larson1998web]. Zaphiris found depth, not links per page (2 to 16), drove time [@zaphiris2000depth]. Category quality matters as much as shape. "Seven plus or minus two" is not a menu rule [@miller1956magical].

For LTF: keep module then lesson as the only two levels. When three courses arrive, show them as headed groups on one `/learn` page, not as a third click.

Verdict on draft 1: keeps the direction and two numbers, but drops the trained-subject caveat, the identical-bottom-node confound, the preference and performance split, the 64 versus 512 item scales, and every condition under which the finding holds. A reader could not grade the rule from this.

## Draft 2: medium (about 400 words)

Menu experiments since 1981 point one way: each added level of hierarchy costs more than each added item on a page. Miller held 64 end items constant and varied depth from one to six levels; selection time formed a U, lowest at two levels of eight, and errors agreed [@miller1981depth]. Kiger dropped the flat condition and added asymmetric trees; the 4-then-16 tree was fastest and least error prone, not reliably different from 16-then-4 and 8-by-8, while participants preferred 8-by-8 [@kiger1984depth].

Larson and Czerwinski moved the question to the Web with 512 items in natural categories. Two-level structures beat three: 16x32 took 36 seconds, 32x16 46 seconds and 8x8x8 58 seconds, and lostness was 0.35, 0.49 and 0.63 [@larson1998web]. Preference ran the other way from speed (11 of 19 ranked 32x16 first). Zaphiris, with 17 people and 64 almanac items, found depth drove time while links per page over 2 to 16 did not [@zaphiris2000depth]. Landauer and Nachbar's touch-screen study fits the same pattern for ordered items [@landauer1985selection].

Limits: the early studies used trained participants and identical bottom nodes, which forced unnatural categories; Larson's study is one domain and 19 people; Zaphiris's is a class project. Snowberry and colleagues (reported in the notes on Miller) found grouping made the flat structure work, so category quality is part of the result. None of this supports "seven plus or minus two" items per menu [@miller1956magical].

For LTF the hierarchy is module then lesson. The content files already define three courses over 22 modules, so the risk is a third level. Keep `/learn` as one page with course headings.

Verdict on draft 2: keeps every number and the main caveats, but compresses the application to one paragraph and does not work through the decision (what exactly to do at 22 modules, how to test it, what to measure). It also loses the asymmetric-structure argument, which is the one most useful to LTF.

## Draft 3: full (about 850 words)

The oldest quantitative question in information architecture is how to arrange a fixed set of items: many choices on few levels, or few choices on many levels. Miller put it to the test with 64 end items held constant and depth varied from one level of 64 to six levels of two [@miller1981depth]. Goal acquisition time formed a U-shaped function, fastest at two levels of eight choices, and the error data agreed, so the fastest structure was also the most accurate. The flat 64-item screen and the six-level binary tree were both worse. Miller's own conclusion was moderate: minimize depth, but not at the cost of crowding the display.

Kiger repeated the design with five 64-item structures, dropping the flat screen and adding two asymmetric trees [@kiger1984depth]. Time and errors rose with depth. The fastest structure was a 4-item menu followed by a 16-item menu, followed closely by, and not reliably different from, 16-then-4 and 8-by-8. Participants, asked which they preferred, chose 8-by-8. Two lessons follow. The result is "two levels beat three or six," not "eight is best," because the winner on speed had a 16-item page. And people do not always prefer what makes them fastest.

The Web replications kept the direction. Larson and Czerwinski built three structures of 512 Encarta items with natural categories and good labels, and had 19 experienced users run 24 searches [@larson1998web]. The two-level 16x32 structure averaged 36 seconds (SD 16), 32x16 averaged 46 seconds (SD 26) and the three-level 8x8x8 averaged 58 seconds (SD 23); the deep structure was reliably slower than both two-level ones, which did not differ reliably from each other. Nine of the 14 five-minute time-outs came in 8x8x8. On Smith's lostness measure the deep tree scored 0.63 (very lost) against 0.35 for 16x32. Eleven of 19 ranked 32x16 best, though Likert ratings did not differ reliably. Memory span and visual scanning pre-tests did not predict time. Zaphiris, with 17 participants and five 64-item hierarchies of almanac facts, found task time rose with depth while the number of links per page, over 2 to 16, did not seem to matter; the 8x2 tree averaged 20.3 seconds against 36.0 for 2x6 [@zaphiris2000depth]. Landauer and Nachbar's touch-screen menus of ordered numbers and words fit a logarithmic time per screen, which favors broad, shallow trees when the items have an obvious order [@landauer1985selection].

The caveats are as important as the direction. Miller and Kiger held the bottom nodes identical across structures, which forced unnatural categories in some conditions and confounds category soundness with depth. Their participants studied the hierarchies and received feedback, so the results are best-case, trained performance, not a first visit. Snowberry, Parkinson and Sisson found that when the 64 items were grouped into coherent categories the flat structure did better than when the items were random, so the quality of the categories carries as much weight as the shape (reported in the notes on Miller). Larson and Czerwinski's study is one well-edited domain and 19 people; Zaphiris's is a class-sized replication with few errors to analyze. And the popular gloss that these studies prove "seven plus or minus two items per menu" is wrong twice: G. A. Miller's paper concerned memory span and absolute judgment, not visible lists, and the best Web structures had 16 and 32 items per page [@miller1956magical; @larson1998web].

For LTF the structure today is module, then lesson: `/learn` lists modules in `CurriculumMap`, each module opens to its lessons, and a lesson is two clicks from the curriculum. That is the shape the evidence favors. The pressure comes from `content/modules.yaml`, which already defines 22 modules in three courses (Course 1 is M0 to M8; Course 2 and Course 3 follow), even though the public beta shows only M0 and M1. The tempting design is a course page above the module page, which makes lessons three levels deep. The evidence says do the opposite: keep one `/learn` page, show the courses as visible headed groups on it (the asymmetric "few big groups, many items" pattern Kiger's fastest tree used), and let each module row expand in place as the `<details>` cards already do. Twenty-two modules on one page is within the 16 to 32 range Larson and Czerwinski found workable, provided the page can be scanned and the groups are clearly labeled.

Then test it, because the studies used trained users and artificial items. A tree test with beginners ("where would you learn to land in a crosswind?") measures success and lostness directly, and its results, not the menu literature, should settle the final grouping.

Verdict on draft 3: keeps every number, each caveat, the asymmetric-structure point, the preference split, the myth correction and a worked decision for LTF with a test. Nothing in it restates an earlier sentence. Its length comes from findings and application, not from filler.

## Verdict

Choose the full density (draft 3) for the Part. The terse draft loses the conditions that decide a rule's grade; the medium draft keeps the numbers but collapses the application into one undifferentiated paragraph, which is exactly where the Part has to earn its keep. The full draft is the right density when every paragraph does one of four jobs: a finding with its number, a caveat with its source, a mechanism, or a worked LTF decision. The writing rule for the Part: cut any sentence that repeats an earlier one, open sections with the finding rather than a preview, and reach the floor by adding sources from the notes and decisions for real LTF pages, never by summarizing.
