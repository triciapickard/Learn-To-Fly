# Density test: Part XVIII, Aesthetics, Emotion and Trust

Test section: the reversal of the aesthetic-usability effect (Tuch et al. 2012, with Sonderegger and Sauer 2010 as the contrasting result). Source notes: `research/19-aesthetics-emotion-trust/notes.md`, entries `tuch2012beautiful`, `sonderegger2010influence`, `tractinsky2000what`, `hassenzahl2010inference`.

## Draft A: terse (about 150 words)

Tuch and colleagues varied the beauty and the usability of an online clothing shop independently, with 80 participants in a 2 by 2 design [@tuch2012beautiful]. Usability was changed only through navigation labels, so the usable and unusable shops looked the same. Beauty did not change perceived usability, before or after use. Poor usability did lower perceived beauty after use. The authors read this as frustration spilling into the aesthetic judgment. The result conflicts with Sonderegger and Sauer, whose attractive phone simulation was rated more usable and was used faster [@sonderegger2010influence]. The difference is probably manipulation strength: Tuch had a large usability gap, Sonderegger none. For LTF, polish cannot rescue a frustrating flow, and Fly mode is where frustration is most likely. Fix the task first, then polish.

## Draft B: medium (about 400 words)

Tuch and colleagues ran the cleanest test of the "beautiful is usable" claim in this literature [@tuch2012beautiful]. Eighty participants used one of four versions of an online clothing shop in a 2 by 2 between-subjects design. Usability was manipulated only through the shop's information architecture (labels and category assignments), so the usable and unusable shops looked identical. Beauty was manipulated without touching any interaction element: 178 people rated 20 designs in a pretest and the pair furthest apart was chosen (d = .95). Participants did four browse tasks, each capped at five minutes, and rated the shop from a screenshot before use and again after.

Beauty did not move perceived usability before use (F(1,76) = .582, p = .448) or after. Usability, however, moved perceived beauty: the hard shop was judged less attractive after people had struggled with it. The authors propose that the frustration of poor usability colors the aesthetic judgment. They also note that their usability manipulation (f = 1.01 to 1.72) was much stronger than their aesthetics manipulation (f = .32 to .54), and that no earlier experiment had reported manipulation strengths at all.

Sonderegger and Sauer found the opposite direction with 60 adolescents and two phone simulations that differed only in looks [@sonderegger2010influence]. The attractive one was rated more usable (6.14 against 5.32) and was used faster (147.7 s against 198.5 s) with fewer errors. The two results fit together if the direction depends on which difference is larger: where interaction quality is equal, looks win; where interaction differs a lot, interaction wins and drags looks with it.

For LTF the practical reading is: polish is not a repair. A learner who cannot find the checklist, or who cannot parse a quiz, will downgrade the look of the product as well as its ease. `FlyModePage`, used beside the sim under load, is where that frustration is most likely, so its task quality comes first. In reviews, ask about appearance and ease separately, and ask after a task, not from a screenshot.

## Draft C: full (about 850 words)

The claim that beauty makes an interface seem usable rests on studies in which beauty was varied and usability was either held constant or varied only a little. Tuch and colleagues designed an experiment to vary both, independently and strongly, and to measure how strong each manipulation was [@tuch2012beautiful].

Eighty participants (42 female, mean age 25.7) each used one of four versions of an online clothing shop in a 2 by 2 between-subjects design. Usability was manipulated only through the shop's information architecture, its category labels and the assignment of items to categories, so the usable and unusable versions were pixel for pixel the same; the authors checked the navigation paths with a latent semantic analysis of information scent. Beauty was manipulated without changing any interaction element. In an online pretest, 178 people rated 20 design versions, and the pair with the largest difference in rated beauty was chosen (t(177) = 12.63, d = .95). In the lab, participants did four browse tasks to find target items, each capped at five minutes. They rated the shop from a screenshot before use and again after use, on several validated instruments: Lavie and Tractinsky's classical and expressive aesthetics scales, AttrakDiff-based hedonic scales and the System Usability Scale among them.

Two results matter. First, aesthetics did not affect perceived usability before use (F(1,76) = .582, p = .448), and it did not afterwards either. Second, usability affected perceived aesthetics after use: the shop that was hard to navigate was rated less beautiful once people had struggled with it, though it looked exactly like the easy one. The authors propose that the affective experience of use, specifically the frustration of failing at the tasks, mediates the effect. They then make a methodological point that reframes the earlier literature. Their usability manipulation was very strong (f = 1.01 to 1.72) and their aesthetics manipulation medium to large (f = .32 to .54), and none of the earlier experiments had reported its manipulation strengths as effect sizes. A finding that "beauty drives perceived usability" in a study whose usability manipulation was weak may simply reflect which manipulation was stronger.

The contrasting result comes from Sonderegger and Sauer [@sonderegger2010influence]. Sixty adolescents aged 14 to 17 used one of two mobile phone simulations that differed only in visual design. The attractive one was rated more attractive (5.3 against 3.15 on a 7-point scale), more usable (6.14 against 5.32 on a one-item scale, F(1,57) = 9.8), and it was used faster (147.7 s against 198.5 s) with fewer errors per trial (2.0 against 4.8). Here there was no usability gap at all, so looks were the only difference available to drive any judgment. Tractinsky and colleagues' earlier ATM study fits the same pattern: its usability manipulation (delays, buttons that failed on first press) was real but modest, and under high aesthetics post-use ratings of usability did not drop [@tractinsky2000what]. Hassenzahl and Monk's reanalysis of 25 correlations from 15 papers adds a mechanism: when people lack direct experience, beauty feeds a general judgment of goodness, and that general judgment colors usability ratings; direct experience then takes over [@hassenzahl2010inference].

Put together, the direction of the effect is conditional. Where interaction quality is equal or the user has little experience, looks lead. Where interaction quality differs a lot and the user has felt it, interaction leads and pulls the aesthetic judgment along. The limits are real: Tuch's study is one lab experiment with students and one product type; Sonderegger and Sauer's is one simulation with adolescents, who the authors note may weight appearance more than adults.

For LTF, this sets an order of work. A learner who cannot find the checklist, misreads a quiz, or loses their place in a challenge procedure will not only find the product harder; on this evidence they will find it uglier. `FlyModePage` is the surface where frustration is most likely, because it is used at a glance beside the simulator while the learner is busy flying, so its task quality (the `FlyPanel` step rows, the `Stopwatch`, the random-event alert) has to be right before any visual refinement is worth anything. The same applies to `QuizBlock` and the `DebriefForm` rubric. For design reviews it sets a method: rate appearance and ease separately, rate them after a task, not from a screenshot, and when comparing two variants, make sure they differ in only one of the two, or say which difference is larger.

## Verdict

Draft A keeps the direction of the findings but drops the manipulation-strength argument, which is the paper's most useful idea for LTF's own testing, and every number that lets a reader judge the size of the effects. Draft B keeps the key numbers and the reconciliation but loses the design detail (identical-looking usable and unusable shops, the pretest), the instruments, the sample descriptions that bound generalization, and the link to Tractinsky and to Hassenzahl and Monk that turns two studies into a model. Draft C keeps every finding, number, caveat and practical step, and each paragraph does new work; the only cut worth making is the opening framing sentence, which is already implied.

Chosen density: C, the full density, with framing sentences cut. Each chapter applies the evidence to named LTF components and ends with specific changes. The floor is reached by covering more sources and more LTF decisions, not by restating findings.
