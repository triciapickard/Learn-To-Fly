# Density test: Part IV, Typography on Screens

Test section: "Size is an angle, not a pixel count", drawn from the notes on Legge and Bigelow (2011), Rello, Pielot and Marcos (2016) and Wallace and colleagues (2022) in lane `03-screen-typography`.

## Draft A: terse (about 150 words)

Reading speed is flat across a "fluent range" of x-height angles from about 0.2 to 2 degrees and falls sharply below the critical print size of about 0.2 degrees [@legge2011print]. Angle is 57.3 times size over distance, so one CSS size gives different angles on a phone and on a monitor across a desk. Rello and colleagues found fixation durations fell as type grew from 10 to 18 points and flattened around 18 to 22, with comprehension worse at 10 and 12 points [@rello2016make]. Their points belong to a 1024 by 768 monitor at 60 cm, so the number does not transfer. Readers aged 35 and over slowed with age and were more sensitive to font [@wallace2022towards]. Rule: size body text so its x-height stays well above 0.2 degrees at the farthest expected viewing distance. In LTF, body text is 16 px; Fly mode should be sized for arm's length or more.

## Draft B: medium (about 400 words)

Legge and Bigelow define print size by x-height angle, because about 94.5% of running text is lowercase and most of its ink sits in the x-height zone [@legge2011print]. Reading is fastest across a fluent range from about 0.2 to 2 degrees. Below the critical print size (CPS), about 0.2 degrees with individual values from 0.15 to 0.3, speed falls sharply; above 1 to 3 degrees it slows again. Angle is 57.3 times physical size divided by distance. At 40 cm, 0.2 degrees is an x-height of 1.4 mm. Books and newspapers cluster at 0.23 to 0.24 degrees, just above CPS, and the authors' online-news sample put home-page body text at 0.19 degrees, below it. Their account of the floor is crowding within the visual span, not eye-movement limits, since one-word-at-a-time presentation shows the same curve.

Rello, Pielot and Marcos tested sizes up to 26 points with 104 readers and an eye tracker [@rello2016make]. Mean fixation duration fell from .255 s at 10 points to .208 s at 18 and .199 s at 22, then rose slightly to .204 s at 26. Comprehension was worse at 10 and 12 points than at 18. The sizes were set on a 17-inch, 1024 by 768 monitor at 60 cm, so "18 points" names a visual angle on that setup, not a CSS value today.

Wallace and colleagues found that reading slowed about 1.5 words per minute per year of age, and that font choice mattered for readers aged 35 and over [@wallace2022towards].

The rule: size text so its x-height clears 0.2 degrees with room to spare at the farthest distance the page will be read from. In LTF, the 16 px lesson body in Instrument Sans has an x-height of about 8 CSS px (the font's x-height is 0.51 em, measured this session). On a 24-inch 1080p monitor at 100% scaling and 70 cm, that is about 0.18 degrees by the formula above, at the CPS line. Fly mode text at 20 px read from a metre is lower still.

## Draft C: full (about 850 words)

Legge, a vision scientist, and Bigelow, a type designer, argue that the sizes people print at are set mostly by the visual system [@legge2011print]. They measure size by x-height, not point size, because point size is a body measure that letters do not fill, about 94.5% of running text is lowercase, and about 87% of the black area of lowercase letters lies in the x-height zone. The x-height fraction of body size varies widely between typefaces: Karow's measurement of 1,049 roman faces ran from 0.28 to 0.58 with a mean of 0.46. Two faces at the same nominal size can differ in effective size by a factor of two.

Size is then expressed as an angle at the eye: 57.3 times the physical x-height divided by the viewing distance. Reading speed is at its maximum across a fluent range of roughly 0.2 to 2 degrees, a factor of ten. Below the critical print size, about 0.2 degrees (individual estimates 0.15 to 0.3), speed falls sharply; above roughly 1 to 3 degrees it declines again. At the conventional 40 cm reading distance, 0.2 degrees is an x-height of 1.4 mm (4 points) and 2 degrees is 14 mm. Print converged just above the floor: newspaper text averaged 0.23 degrees and fiction 0.24, within a range of 0.20 to 0.28. Screens did not do better. In the authors' sample of online news (132 ppi, 40 cm), home-page body text averaged 0.19 degrees, below CPS, and article text 0.21. The mechanism they favor is the visual span, limited by crowding: below CPS, the spacing between letters falls inside the crowding zone and the number of letters taken in per fixation shrinks. Eye movements are not the limit, because rapid serial presentation of one word at a time shows the same curve. Widening the spacing did not enlarge the span.

The evidence is strong: a long line of psychophysics with consistent values across methods. Its limits are that the fluent range is defined for normal vision (low-vision readers have a CPS that is a multiple of their acuity limit), that the 40 cm distance is a convention real devices break, and that the clustering of print sizes in the fluent range is correlational.

Rello, Pielot and Marcos supply the screen-reading evidence above the floor [@rello2016make]. With 104 participants (93 analyzed for fixations), six Wikipedia passages in Arial, six sizes from 10 to 26 points and a Tobii eye tracker at 60 cm on a 17-inch 1024 by 768 monitor, mean fixation duration fell from .255 s at 10 points through .241, .224 and .208 to .199 s at 22 points, and was .204 s at 26 (F(5, 445) = 66.825, p < .001). Fixations at 10 to 14 points were longer than at every larger size. Comprehension was worse at 10 and 12 points than at 18, and readers rated 18 points most readable. The authors recommend 18 points or more for text-heavy pages. The caveats matter as much as the result: one typeface, one language, the first paragraphs only, and a fixed column, so larger type also meant fewer characters per line, a confound Dyson warns about [@dyson2004physical]. Above all, the points were points on that monitor at that distance, and the same CSS value on a high-density laptop or a phone subtends a different angle.

Age sharpens the case for margin above the floor. In Wallace and colleagues' crowd-sourced study of 352 readers, speed fell about 1.5 words per minute per year of age across the sample, steepening to about 2.6 for readers aged 35 and over, the group in which the choice of font also mattered [@wallace2022towards]. Adult beginners at a home simulator are in that group.

The practical rule follows: specify body size by the angle it must deliver at the farthest expected viewing distance, and keep the x-height well above 0.2 degrees, aiming toward 0.3 degrees so that readers whose individual CPS sits at the top of the reported range are still fluent.

The LTF arithmetic, computed this session from the font file and the formula above: Instrument Sans has an x-height of 0.51 em, so the 16 px lesson body has an 8.2 CSS px x-height. On a 24-inch 1920 by 1080 monitor at 100% scaling (a pixel pitch of about 0.28 mm) that is 2.3 mm. At 40 cm it subtends 0.32 degrees, at 60 cm 0.22, at 70 cm 0.18 and at a metre 0.13. A learner whose lesson sits on a monitor beside the sim, pushed back to make room for a yoke, is reading at or below the critical print size. Fly mode's `text-xl` step text (20 px, an x-height of 10.2 px) is 0.16 degrees at a metre. These are estimates under stated assumptions, not measurements of real setups, and device scaling changes them, but every plausible desk setup puts LTF's body text near the floor rather than comfortably above it.

## Verdict

Draft A keeps the headline numbers but loses the definition of x-height fraction, the online-news finding that screens already sit below CPS, the crowding mechanism, the low-vision limit, Rello's confound with line length and the size-by-size fixation curve. It states a rule without the reasoning a reviewer needs to apply it to a new surface. Draft B keeps the main numbers and the transfer caveat but drops Karow's range (which is what makes "compare faces by x-height" concrete), the strength-of-evidence paragraph and the age slope beyond one figure. Draft C keeps every finding, caveat and number in the notes and adds the worked LTF computation, which is the part of the section that changes a decision. Its only excess is a sentence or two of connective prose.

Chosen density: full (Draft C), trimmed of connective sentences, about 700 to 900 words for each substantial section. Chapters will carry two to four such sections plus rules, so each chapter lands between about 1,400 and 2,000 words, reaching the floor through more findings and more worked LTF decisions, not restatement.
