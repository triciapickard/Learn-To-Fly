# Density test: Part XXI, Design Systems, Process and Craft

Test section: "Show several designs, never one" (from the chapter on exploring before refining),
drafted from the notes for tohidi2006getting, dow2010parallel and buxton2007sketching.

## Draft 1: terse (about 150 words)

People who test one design are polite about it. Tohidi, Buxton, Baecker and Sellen (2006) gave
48 students paper prototypes of a climate controller, either one design alone or three side by
side. Every design was rated lower among alternatives, two of the three differences were
significant, and positive comments fell by roughly half. Showing three designs did not yield
more suggestions for fixes: users find problems, they do not design solutions. Dow and
colleagues (2010) found that designers who made banner ads in parallel, with critique on a set,
produced ads with more clicks in a real campaign, more varied designs and larger gains in
self-efficacy than designers who iterated one at a time. Both are small studies. For LTF: test
two or three different versions of a lesson opener or Fly mode screen together, ask learners for
problems, not fixes, and sketch several widget directions before building one.

## Draft 2: medium (about 400 words)

A lone design draws faint praise. Tohidi, Buxton, Baecker and Sellen (2006) tested hand-drawn
paper prototypes of a touch-screen house climate controller with 48 University of Toronto
students. Twelve saw all three designs (Circular, Tabular, Linear, deliberately different in
style but identical in function); twelve each saw only one. Every design scored lower when seen
among alternatives: Circular 9.08 alone against 8.13 in the group (p = 0.004), Linear 7.92
against 6.89 (p = 0.014), and Tabular 8.83 against 8.39, which missed significance (p = 0.064).
Positive comments fell sharply for all three designs, and negative comments rose significantly
only for the weakest, Linear. The number of substantial suggestions did not change. The authors'
conclusion is that testing "is a means to identify problems, not provide solutions".

Dow and colleagues (2010) moved the question from testers to designers. Thirty-three
participants each made five prototype banner ads and a final ad, with equal critique. Serial
designers received critique after each prototype; Parallel designers made three, received
critique on the set, made two more, then finished. The final ads ran in a real campaign for 15
days. Parallel ads drew more clicks in the first five days (44 against 26 on about equal
impressions, p < 0.05), were rated higher by professionals and were more diverse, and only the
Parallel group gained significantly in self-efficacy. Nearly half the Serial participants
reacted badly to critique; no Parallel participant did.

Both studies are small, single-domain and student-based, and the Parallel condition also
differed in timing of feedback, so they support a practice rather than an effect size. They
agree with Buxton's craft argument that the right design is found by exploring several before
refining one.

For LTF: when a new Fly mode layout, lesson opener or widget is tested, show two or three
genuinely different versions in one session, ask learners what is wrong and which they would
use, and leave the fixes to the designer. Treat a single prototype's ratings as an upper bound.

## Draft 3: full (about 800 words)

A lone design draws faint praise, and the evidence for that comes from a careful small
experiment. Tohidi, Buxton, Baecker and Sellen (2006) built three hand-drawn paper prototypes of
a touch-screen house climate control system on laminated 5 by 8 inch index cards, with a person
playing the computer. The three were deliberately different in style (Circular, with round dials
and analog clocks; Tabular, with drop-down lists; Linear, with horizontal timelines and sliders),
built by the same team to similar fidelity, with identical functions. Forty-eight students and
recent graduates (26 female, 22 male) were randomly assigned: twelve saw all three designs, and
twelve each saw only one. Each performed four tasks while thinking aloud, then rated the design
on ten-point scales for ease of use per task, seven design dimensions and overall usability, and
was interviewed.

Every design scored lower when seen among alternatives. Circular fell from 9.08 alone to 8.13 in
the group (p = 0.004); Linear from 7.92 to 6.89 (p = 0.014); Tabular from 8.83 to 8.39, which
did not reach significance (p = 0.064). The team coded 337 statements from 24 of the 48 tapes,
with two judges agreeing at a reported 85 percent. Positive comments fell for every design
(Circular 6.42 to 2.67, Tabular 4.75 to 2.17, Linear 5.83 to 2.33). Negative comments rose
significantly only for Linear, the lowest rated, with 70 in the Multiple condition against 11.5
for Linear alone. Substantial suggestions for improvement did not differ between conditions,
which the authors summarize as testing "is a means to identify problems, not provide solutions".
They also hedge that without further study the validity of the comments is unknown.

Dow, Glassco, Kass, Schwarz, Schwartz and Klemmer (2010) asked the same question of designers
rather than testers. Thirty-three participants, about half with prior graphic or ad design
experience, designed 160 by 600 pixel web banner ads for a magazine client. Each made five
prototypes and a final ad and received descriptive critique on every prototype, with equal time.
Serial participants received critique after each prototype. Parallel participants made three,
received critique on all three, made two more and received critique again. The 33 final ads ran
in a real campaign for 15 days and drew 501 clicks on 1,180,320 impressions. The two best ads
were Parallel. In the first five days, when impressions were about equal, Parallel ads drew 44
clicks on about 79,800 impressions against 26 on about 79,658 (chi-square 4.60, p < 0.05).
Professional raters scored Parallel ads higher and judged Parallel sets more diverse.
Self-efficacy on a 20-point scale rose on average from 10.85 to 12.12, significantly only for
the Parallel group. Nearly half the Serial participants described negative reactions to
critique; none of the Parallel participants did.

The limits are real. Both samples are small and student-heavy; the Tabular difference missed
significance; Dow's Parallel condition also received its first feedback later and had an
explicit second round, so it is an explore-then-refine hybrid rather than pure parallelism; and
click performance barely predicted rated quality (an R-squared of 0.032 appears in the paper),
so "better" depends on the yardstick. The two studies come from different groups, which gives the
shared conclusion more weight than either alone, but neither is a meta-analysis.

The craft literature reaches the same place by another road. Buxton separates getting the right
design (exploring with many cheap sketches) from getting the design right (refining a chosen
direction), and his chapter title "Sketches are not Prototypes" marks the difference. Kocienda's
account of Apple's demo culture describes the same loop of making alternatives, showing them and
keeping what survives, though as memoir it is one company's view.

For LTF the practice is concrete. A new Fly mode layout, a lesson opener or a widget such as the
checklist runner is tested in two or three genuinely different versions in the same session,
built at equal rough fidelity so that polish does not decide. Learners are asked what is wrong,
where they hesitated and which they would use beside the sim, not how to fix it. Ratings from a
single version are treated as an upper bound, and the decision rests on observed hesitations and
failures. Designers sketch at least three directions for any new widget before coding one, and
critique is given on the set.

## Verdict

The terse draft keeps the direction of both findings but drops the numbers a reviewer needs to
judge strength (the non-significant Tabular result, the click counts), the confound in Dow's
design and the reason the studies agree. The full draft keeps everything but spends words on
procedure (index cards, coding agreement, gender split) that does not change any LTF decision.
The medium draft keeps every number that bears on strength, both caveats and a concrete LTF
practice. The Part is written at medium density, rising toward full where a boundary condition
or a confound changes what LTF should do (for example Dow's timing confound, which decides how
LTF schedules critique).
