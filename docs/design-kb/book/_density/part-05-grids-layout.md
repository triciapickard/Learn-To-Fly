# Density test: Part V, Grids, Layout and Space

Section chosen: "What the whitespace experiments show" (the evidence on margins, leading and surrounding information), drafted from the lane 04 notes on `chaparro2004reading`, `mcmullin2002effects` and `ling2007influence`, with the related `dyson2004physical` notes from lane 02.

## Draft 1: terse (about 150 words)

Whitespace experiments are few and modest. Chaparro and colleagues varied margins and leading in online text: margins improved reading speed and comprehension over a no-margin version and were preferred, while leading changed preference but not measured performance [@chaparro2004reading]. A 2005 follow-up found an enhanced layout raised satisfaction without changing speed or comprehension. McMullin and colleagues found better comprehension when text was surrounded by whitespace than by meaningless information, and no effect of line length [@mcmullin2002effects]. Ling and van Schaik found that greater line spacing improved accuracy and speed in visual search, and that left-aligned text beat justified text that participants preferred [@ling2007influence]. The pattern: margins and separation from clutter help comprehension; finer polish mostly moves preference; preference and performance often disagree. For LTF, give lesson text real margins, keep chrome away from the reading column, and test layouts with quiz items, not opinion.

## Draft 2: medium (about 400 words)

The case for whitespace is mostly made by designers, and the experimental record is thin. Three small studies carry most of it, and none was read in full for this book, so sample sizes, exact margin widths and effect sizes are not known here.

Chaparro, Baker, Shaikh, Hull and Brady built four layouts by crossing two kinds of space around online text: the margins surrounding the text block and the leading between lines [@chaparro2004reading]. Margins improved both reading speed and comprehension compared with a version with no margins, and readers preferred text with margins. Leading did not measurably change reading performance, though it shaped preference. A 2005 follow-up from the same group compared text with and without an enhanced layout (headers, indentation, figure placement): speed and comprehension did not change, but readers were more satisfied and found the enhanced version less fatiguing.

McMullin, Varnhagen, Heng and Apedoe tested undergraduates on web text and found that comprehension was better when text was surrounded by whitespace than when it was surrounded by meaningless information; line length had no effect in the range they tested [@mcmullin2002effects]. Their comparison is closer to a clutter manipulation than a pure whitespace one.

Ling and van Schaik studied visual search rather than reading: greater line spacing improved accuracy and reduced reaction times, and left-aligned text outperformed justified text even though participants preferred justified [@ling2007influence]. The apparent conflict with the Chaparro leading result is probably a task difference (search versus reading), not a contradiction.

Read together, the studies support three claims. Structural space around a text block, and distance from irrelevant material, help comprehension. Finer layout polish changes satisfaction more than performance. And what people say they prefer is not a reliable guide to what helps them. They do not support "more whitespace is always better," and nothing here was tested with learners following a procedure beside a simulator.

For LTF: keep a real side margin on phones and let margins grow on larger screens; keep the `SignupBanner`, progress and promotional prompts out of the paragraph's neighborhood; choose line height for legibility and consistency rather than as a comprehension lever, except in scanned lists where generous spacing helps search; and when comparing layout variants, measure with the same quiz items rather than asking which looks nicer.

## Draft 3: full (about 850 words)

The claim that whitespace helps reading is one of the most repeated in design, and one of the least tested. The experimental record for screen text is a handful of small studies from the early 2000s, published partly in trade bulletins, and the lane notes record that none of their full texts could be read. What follows reports their abstracts and is careful about what those abstracts allow.

Chaparro, Baker, Shaikh, Hull and Brady manipulated two kinds of space around online text, the margins that surround the text block and the leading between its lines, giving four layouts [@chaparro2004reading]. They measured reading speed, comprehension and preference. Margins improved both reading speed and comprehension compared with a version with no margins, and readers generally preferred text with margins. Leading did not measurably change reading performance, but it did shape preference. The sample size, the particular margin widths and leading values, the passages and the statistics are not available from the record that could be read, so no effect size can be stated.

A 2005 follow-up by Chaparro, Shaikh and Baker asked whether a poor layout hurts performance. It compared text with and without an enhanced layout (headers, indentation and figure placement). Reading speed and comprehension were not affected, but participants were more satisfied with the enhanced layout and found it less fatiguing. That follow-up is described in the same lane notes but has no separate bibliography entry, so it is cited here through the 2004 study's notes.

McMullin, Varnhagen, Heng and Apedoe asked undergraduate students to read web text and answer comprehension questions while two features varied: line length, and what surrounded the text [@mcmullin2002effects]. Comprehension was better when the text was surrounded by whitespace than when it was surrounded by meaningless information. Line length had no effect in the range tested, and that range could not be confirmed. The tested contrast is whitespace against clutter, not whitespace against less whitespace, so it says more about removing competing material than about the virtue of emptiness. The null result for line length is a limit of one small sample and one range; it does not show that line length never matters, and the legibility literature reviewed by Dyson reaches a more qualified view [@dyson2004physical].

Ling and van Schaik studied a different task, visual search of web pages [@ling2007influence]. Greater line spacing improved accuracy and reduced reaction times. Left-aligned text outperformed justified text, although participants preferred justified text. The same authors' 2006 study on font and line length showed the same split, with longer lines helping scanning and shorter lines rated better. This appears to conflict with Chaparro's finding that leading did not change reading performance, but the tasks differ (search versus continuous reading) and the stimuli cannot be compared, so the fair conclusion is that line spacing matters more when people scan for a target than when they read through.

There is also an older claim on the other side. Bernard, Chaparro and Thomasson framed their 2000 question against a view attributed to Jared Spool's team that sites with more whitespace did worse at helping users find information. A second-hand description says Bernard found no difference in search time but a preference for enough space to separate information units; that description could not be confirmed against the original, so it is reported only as context.

Read together, the studies support three claims and reject one. Structural space around a block of text, and distance from irrelevant material, help comprehension. Finer polish (indents, header styling, modest leading changes) mostly moves satisfaction and fatigue rather than measured speed or understanding. Preference and performance disagree often enough that preference alone cannot settle a layout question. The rejected claim is that more whitespace is always better: the evidence supports margins and grouping, not unlimited emptiness, and it was gathered on casual readers and students, not on learners working through a procedure with a simulator open on another screen.

For LTF the application is specific. The lesson column already has the 16 px phone gutter from `PageContainer` and the `px-4` on `LessonPage`; that is the minimum, and margins should grow with the viewport rather than the column growing to fill it. The `SignupBanner`, the resume bar and any future progress or streak prompt are the "surrounding information" in McMullin's sense when they sit beside or between paragraphs, so they belong at the edges of the reading flow. Line height should be chosen for legibility and consistency and left alone as a comprehension lever, except in lists that learners scan (checklists, the speeds table, the challenge step list), where Ling's result favors generous spacing. And when two lesson layouts are compared, the comparison should use the same quiz items for each, as McMullin did, instead of a vote on which looks better.

## Verdict

The terse draft keeps the headline findings but loses the caveats that decide how hard each rule can be graded: that no full text was read, that McMullin's contrast is whitespace against clutter, that the leading conflict is a task difference, and that the null line-length result is range-bound. A reader could turn it into "more whitespace helps," which is the claim the evidence rejects.

The medium draft keeps every finding, every caveat and the LTF application, at about half the length of the full draft. The full draft adds three things: the 2005 follow-up's citation status, the Bernard and Spool context, and the Dyson cross-reference. The first two are useful once but would be padding if repeated; the third belongs in the chapter on measure.

Chosen density: **medium, rising to full where a study's limits or a worked LTF decision need the room.** Each source gets its findings, its numbers if the notes give them, and its caveats in one pass; context that does not change a rule is cut; length is spent on more findings, more specific application to named LTF components and worked decisions, not on restating results.
