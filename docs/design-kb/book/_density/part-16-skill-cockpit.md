## Draft 3: full (about 850 words)

Knowledge of results (KR) is information about the outcome of a movement: where the ball landed, how far the airplane drifted from the target altitude. For most of the twentieth century the working assumption was that more KR, given sooner, meant faster learning. Salmoni, Schmidt and Walter argued that this belief rested on a confound [@salmoni1984knowledge]. Experiments had measured performance while KR was still being given, and that measure mixes two different effects: a temporary effect of KR on performance, which vanishes when KR is withdrawn, and a relatively permanent effect on learning, which shows on a later test without KR. Once the two are separated, the picture changes. The guidance hypothesis that grew from the review says KR guides the learner toward the correct response, which helps during practice, but if it is given too often the learner comes to depend on it and stops processing intrinsic feedback, the feel and sight of their own movement. The review's practical conclusion, as later authors relay it, is that a reduced relative frequency of KR (the proportion of trials on which it is given) slows acquisition while KR is present but supports better retention. The full review was not read for this book; its definitions and conclusion come from later papers that describe and cite it.

Schmidt and Bjork describe the experiment that made the idea concrete [@schmidt1992new]. In Schmidt, Young, Swinnen and Shapiro's study, feedback came after every trial or as a summary after 5 or 15 trials. Errors during acquisition grew as the summary got longer. On the no-feedback retention tests at 10 minutes and at 2 days, the 15-trial group did best. A 50 percent faded condition, in which feedback was gradually withdrawn, also beat feedback on every trial at retention, and the differences grew as the retention interval grew. Schmidt and Bjork add a warning that applies to any product team: trainers rarely see learners on the delayed tests that are the real goal, and learners prefer the arrangements that feel smooth, as in the keyboard study where the schedules subjects preferred produced the least learning.

The limit is the part that matters for teaching a beginner to fly. Wulf and Shea reviewed the motor-learning evidence and concluded that principles derived from simple laboratory tasks, with one degree of freedom and little practice, do not reliably generalize to complex skills [@wulf2002principles]. For the ski simulator task, 100 percent concurrent feedback proved most effective for learning. The optimal summary length was shorter for more complex tasks: in a simulated batting task a summary of 5 trials beat summaries of 1 or 15. Switching the focus of feedback every trial was worse than keeping one focus for a block. Their explanation is a load account: when a task already loads the learner heavily, conditions that reduce load help, and when a task is light, conditions that add load help. Later research on the guidance hypothesis has been mixed, and neither it nor the competing feedback-usefulness hypothesis has been confirmed consistently.

The resulting rule is conditional rather than a slogan. For a complete beginner on a complex maneuver such as a landing, frequent and specific feedback in the first sessions is right. As attempts become consistent, feedback should thin: a summary across several attempts instead of a verdict after each one, and fewer prompts during the attempt. Learning is then checked without help and after a delay, because a smooth guided attempt shows only that the guidance works. No specific number of attempts or percentage of feedback has been shown optimal for this task, so the product should not claim one.

In LTF the pieces are partly in place. `ResultView` gives criterion-by-criterion feedback after each debrief, and `reviewLink` sends the learner to the lesson section behind any criterion scored below the second level. That suits early modules. What is missing is the second half of the schedule. `HistoryTab` shows a table of attempts but not a summary across them, so the learner cannot see a pattern in a criterion over five attempts. `FlyPanel` always shows every step with a checkbox, so the learner never flies a challenge they have already passed without the page in front of them. Build a per-criterion summary in `HistoryTab`, and a "fly without steps" option in `FlyModePage` offered once a challenge has been passed.

## Verdict

The terse draft keeps the headline numbers but loses three things the notes insist on: that the 1984 review was not read in full, that later replication is mixed, and the load explanation that says when the rule flips. Without those the rule reads as more confident than the evidence. The full draft keeps every finding, number and caveat, including the keyboard-preference warning and the feedback-focus result, and its LTF paragraph names what exists and what to build. Its only excess is the opening example sentence and a few restating clauses. The medium draft drops the feedback-focus result, the learner-preference warning and most of the product detail.

Chosen density: the full draft, edited tight (about 700 to 800 words for a section of this weight). Each source gets its claim, its numbers, its evidence type and its limit once; the application names components and says keep, change or build. No paragraph restates the previous one. Shorter sections use the same discipline at their natural length.
