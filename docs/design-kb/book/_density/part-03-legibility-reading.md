## Draft 2: medium (about 400 words)

Line length has a broad comfortable middle, and both extremes cost something different. In print, Tinker's very large samples (1,760 readers for 10-point type alone) found 10-point type set solid equally legible from 17 to 27 picas, and with 2 points of leading from 14 to 31 picas [@tinker1963legibility]. Very short lines needed more and longer fixations. A 43-pica line raised the frequency of regressions by 56.7 per cent compared with a 19-pica line, which Tinker put down to trouble finding the start of the next line. Huey, earlier and with far less control, preferred 60 to 80 mm columns [@huey1908psychology]; his figures are history, since they do not control type size.

Dyson's review of screen studies makes the key methodological point: characters per line, physical length, type size and margins were often changed together, and characters per line is the variable that matters, because saccade length is consistent in characters, not degrees [@dyson2004physical]. With that lens, most screen studies found longer lines read as fast as or faster than shorter ones. Dyson and Kipping's 48 readers were fastest at 100 characters per line, partly because they scrolled less, yet they rated 55 characters easiest. Dyson and Haselgrove found better comprehension at 55 than at 100. Bernard and colleagues found no speed difference across about 45, 76 and 132 characters, and adults found the short line easiest to concentrate on [@bernard2003effects]. Dyson states that no upper limit on characters per line has been identified.

The trade-off is mechanical. Short lines reduce the information taken in per fixation and add return sweeps; long lines make the return sweep harder to land. Between, performance is flat and preference picks the middle.

## Draft 3: full (about 800 words)

Line length has a broad comfortable middle, and the two extremes fail for different reasons. The strongest print evidence is Tinker's. His line-width and leading studies used samples rarely matched since (1,760 readers for 10-point type, 1,900 for 11-point, 1,800 for 12-point, 2,000 each for 9 and 8 points, 1,960 for 6) and measured speed of reading meaningful text with comprehension checked [@tinker1963legibility]. Ten-point type set solid was equally legible from 17 to 27 picas, and with 2 points of leading from 14 to 31 picas. Very short lines needed more and longer fixations. At the other end, a 43-pica line raised regression frequency by 56.7 per cent against a 19-pica line, which Tinker explained as difficulty finding the start of the next line. He turned the results into "safety zones" per size: for 10-point, 14-pica lines with 1 to 4 points of leading, or 19-pica lines with 2 to 4.

Huey had reached the same direction half a century earlier with much weaker methods. He reported a consensus for shorter lines with a maximum of 90 mm and preferred 60 to 80 mm himself; in his own recordings a 60.5 mm column was read at 3.63 words per fixation, while columns of 98 to 121 mm needed about one fixation per two words [@huey1908psychology]. His figures are in millimeters with no control for type size, so they show the direction, not a value.

Screens changed the question. Dyson's critical review argues that characters per line, physical length, type size and margins were often confounded (a 15 cm line holds about 90 characters of 12-point Times and about 100 of 10-point), and that characters per line is the right variable, because saccade length is consistent in characters rather than visual angle [@dyson2004physical]. Read that way, the screen studies mostly favor longer lines for speed. Dyson and Kipping had 48 readers read 800-word passages at six lengths from 25 to 100 characters per line; the longest line was read faster than the shortest, partly because there was less scrolling, but readers rated 55 characters easiest. Dyson and Haselgrove, with a more demanding comprehension test, found 55 characters gave better comprehension than 100. Bernard and colleagues, with adults and children reading at about 45, 76 and 132 characters, found no difference in reading time or efficiency, while adults found the short line easiest to concentrate on and none chose the full width as a favorite [@bernard2003effects]. Their null result may reflect the extreme 132-character line. Dyson states plainly that no upper limit on characters per line has been identified, and the studies are small (18 to 48 participants) and run on 1980s and 1990s screens.

The mechanism explains both ends. Short lines cut the characters available per fixation and multiply return sweeps; long lines lengthen the return sweep and raise the chance of landing on the wrong line, which is where Tinker's extra regressions come from. In the middle, speed is flat, and preference and comprehension both lean toward moderate lengths. That is why a 45 to 75 character measure is defensible: not as a speed optimum, which the data do not show, but as the length readers prefer, that supported comprehension in one study, and that print tradition agrees on.

Two cautions follow. First, preference and performance diverge, so asking learners which width feels better answers a different question from which width they read better. Second, because size and characters per line are coupled, enlarging the type on a fixed column shortens the line; any size change must be made with the measure in characters.

*In LTF:* the lesson `article` is capped at `max-w-[800px]`, which the dossier estimates at roughly 90 to 100 characters of 16 px Instrument Sans (not measured), while `Prose` already uses `max-w-measure` (the `container-measure` token, 68ch) at `body-lg`. Put `MarkdownContent` paragraphs and lists on `max-w-measure` while letting tables, widgets and images use the full 800 px column, so that the measure stays in characters whatever the learner's text size. Leave Fly mode's `max-w-3xl` at `text-xl` alone for now: a narrow line is low risk there, and adults in Bernard's study found short lines easiest to concentrate on.

## Verdict

The terse draft loses the sample sizes, the mechanism, the caveat that 45 to 75 is a preference and comprehension judgment rather than a speed finding, the confound argument and Huey's caveat. A reader could not defend the rule from it. The medium draft keeps the main numbers and the confound argument but drops Tinker's safety zones, Huey's fixation counts, the scrolling explanation's limits and the two cautions, which are the practical part. The full draft keeps every finding, number and caveat the notes give and adds the application, with little restatement; its only redundancy is the closing restatement of the mechanism, which can be shortened.

Chosen density: full, at about 700 to 900 words per substantial section, with the mechanism stated once and no closing summary. Sections that rest on a single thin source are written shorter rather than padded.
