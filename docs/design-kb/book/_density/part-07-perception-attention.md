## Draft 2: medium (about 400 words)

Palmer proposed common region as a grouping principle that cannot be reduced to proximity, closure or similarity: elements lying inside the same bounded region tend to be seen as a group [@palmer1992common]. His demonstrations were built like Wertheimer's dot rows. Proximity or similarity favored one grouping, then closed outlines were drawn around a different grouping, and the outlines won. Three further findings from the abstract matter for design. The effect depends on perceived depth: if the outline is not seen as a surface or background behind the elements, it weakens, which places common region after some depth processing. When regions nest, the smallest enclosing region dominates. And regions can follow a hierarchical embedding scheme, so groups sit inside larger groups.

The principle has held up. Wagemans and colleagues list common region among the established newer principles and report that response-time methods, such as the repetition discrimination task of Palmer and Beck, give it objective support [@wagemans2012century]. Later studies reported in secondary summaries found common region dominating shape similarity, but those originals were not read for this book.

Common region is what designers call a card, a panel or a box. It is cheap and strong, and both properties cut both ways. Because it can override proximity, an element placed inside the wrong region will be read as part of that region. Because it depends on the region being seen as a surface, a boundary that barely separates from the page weakens the cue.

In LTF, the `Card` component and its many hand-inlined copies (`rounded-lg border border-line bg-surface p-4 shadow-1`) are the main common-region device. ## Draft 3: full (about 850 words)

Palmer's 1992 paper proposed that elements lying inside the same bounded region of space tend to be grouped, and argued that this is a genuinely new principle, not a disguised case of proximity, closure or any known factor [@palmer1992common]. The method followed Wertheimer: rows or arrays of elements arranged so that proximity or similarity favors one grouping, then closed outlines drawn so that a different grouping falls inside shared regions. The outlines won. Common region could overcome proximity and similarity, which at the time were the two strongest named factors.

The abstract adds three findings that are easy to skip and important for interface work. First, grouping by common region depends on perceived depth relations. If the enclosing outline is not seen as a surface or background behind the elements, the effect weakens. Palmer used this to argue that common region operates after at least some depth perception, later than image-level factors such as proximity. Second, when regions are nested, the smallest enclosing background area dominates: an element inside a small box inside a large box groups first with the small box's contents. Third, the principle follows a hierarchical embedding scheme, so groups can sit inside larger groups without contradiction.

Palmer also sorted grouping cues into intrinsic ones (properties of the elements, such as their spacing and color) and extrinsic ones (properties of the context, such as an enclosing region). That classification is attributed to Palmer in later work found by web search; it is not in the 1992 abstract, so treat it as a useful vocabulary rather than a finding.

The status of the principle is good. Wagemans and colleagues list common region among the established newer principles of grouping, alongside connectedness, and report that response-time measures give it objective support [@wagemans2012century]. The main objective method is the repetition discrimination task of Beck and Palmer and Palmer and Beck, in which people report whether a row contains a repeated shape; the grouping effect is the response-time difference between repeats that fall inside one group and repeats that straddle a group boundary. Secondary summaries report that common region dominated shape similarity in a later study and that cooperating principles group more strongly than either alone. Those originals were not read for this book, so they support the direction of the claim, not any size.

The caution comes from Palmer's own argument. Because common region needs the boundary to be read as a surface, it is not a purely image-level effect, and it can fail quietly. A hairline with little contrast, or a fill that blends into the page, may not be read as a separating surface, and the grouping it was meant to create weakens. The same depth logic appears in Palmer and Rock's account of connectedness: connected regions perceived to lie in separate depth planes fail to form one unit [@palmer1994rethinking].

Common region is the perceptual name for what designers call a card, a panel, a tray or a boxed group. It is cheap, it is strong, and both properties have costs. Because it can override proximity and similarity, an element placed inside the wrong region will be read as part of that region even when spacing and style say otherwise. Because it depends on the region reading as a surface, its strength varies with theme, display and viewing distance.

In LTF the `Card` component, and the many hand-inlined copies of its classes (`rounded-lg border border-line bg-surface p-4 shadow-1`) on lesson, challenge, dashboard and reference pages, are the main common-region device. Computed this session from `tokens.css`, the Day `surface` (#ffffff) on `canvas` (#f7f5f3) is about 1.09:1, and the `line` border (#dfdedc) on `surface` about 1.34:1. In Night, `surface` (#1f1e1c) on `canvas` (#141412) is about 1.11:1, and the region is drawn by a 1 px ring in `line` (#393836) at about 1.42:1 against the surface. The fill difference contributes very little, so the region is carried by a faint hairline and, in Day, a soft shadow. Those numbers are not a failure under any standard cited here, because card outlines are decoration rather than controls; they are a reason to test whether the cards read as surfaces in a quick glance, especially in fly mode on a second screen at arm's length.

Three working consequences follow. Draw region boundaries around meaning, not around convenient chunks of markup: a widget, its caption and its "Describe this diagram" disclosure belong in one `WidgetFrame`; an unrelated paragraph does not. Check nesting deliberately: a `Badge` inside a `Card` inside a tinted `Callout` will group with the innermost region first, so the innermost box must be the right one. And do not let every element have its own box; when everything is enclosed, enclosure stops distinguishing anything, which the chapters on search and clutter make precise.

## Verdict

The terse draft keeps the headline claim and one application but drops the depth caveat's source, the nesting rule's practical meaning, the evidence status (how the principle was confirmed and which follow-ups were not read) and the Night theme numbers. A reviewer could not grade a rule from it.

The medium draft keeps every finding, the evidence status and the core application, and is the best ratio of substance to length. It loses the intrinsic and extrinsic vocabulary, the link to connectedness and depth planes, the method detail that explains why the support counts as objective, and the "do not box everything" consequence.

The full draft keeps all of that, and nothing in it restates an earlier sentence. Its extra length is method detail, caveats and worked application, which is what the brief asks for.

Chosen density: the full draft's level of detail, written with the medium draft's economy. Each section states the finding, the method in a sentence or two, the numbers the notes support, the replication status and what was not read, then applies it to named LTF components. No recap paragraphs, no restated claims, no openers about what a section will do.
