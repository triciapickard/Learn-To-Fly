# Lane 22: Design philosophy (research notes)

## rittel1973dilemmas
Rittel, H. W. J., and Webber, M. M. (1973). Dilemmas in a general theory of planning. Policy Sciences 4(2), 155-169. DOI 10.1007/BF01405730.

### Core claims
Rittel and Webber argue that the problems of social policy and planning cannot be solved with the methods that made science and engineering successful, because those methods were built for what the authors call "tame" or "benign" problems. Planning problems are "wicked" instead. The authors say they use the word in a sense akin to "malignant" (in contrast to "benign"), "vicious" or "tricky", and they stress that they do not mean to personify the problems or imply malicious intent. The paper's boldest claim is that the classical paradigm of science and engineering is "not applicable to the problems of open societal systems", and that professionals were "misled" into assuming they could solve social problems the way scientists solve theirs. The authors add that social problems "are never solved", they are "only re-solved, over and over again". A second claim concerns method. The "first generation" systems approach, with its phases of understand, gather information, analyze, synthesize and work out a solution, is judged inadequate. They propose a "second generation" model of planning as an argumentative process in which an image of the problem and of the solution "emerges gradually among the participants, as a product of incessant judgment, subjected to critical argument". (All quotations read in the scanned article text, pp. 160-162, see the Confirmed line.)

### Specifics
The tame examples given are solving a mathematical equation, an organic chemist analyzing a compound's structure, and a chess player seeking checkmate in five moves: for each, the mission is clear and it is clear whether the problem has been solved. Wicked problems lack both traits, and include nearly all public policy issues such as freeway location, tax rates, school curricula and crime. The authors list "at least ten" distinguishing properties, each headed in the paper as follows. (1) There is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem; "the formulation of a wicked problem is the problem", because problem understanding and problem resolution are concomitant. (2) Wicked problems have no stopping rule; the planner stops for reasons external to the problem such as time, money or patience, and says "that's good enough". (3) Solutions are not true-or-false, but good-or-bad. (4) There is no immediate and no ultimate test of a solution, since solutions generate waves of consequences over an extended, virtually unbounded period. (5) Every solution is a "one-shot operation" because there is no opportunity to learn by trial and error; every attempt counts significantly. (6) There is no enumerable or exhaustively describable set of potential solutions or permissible operations. (7) Every wicked problem is essentially unique. (8) Every wicked problem can be considered a symptom of another problem. (9) A discrepancy can be explained in numerous ways, and the choice of explanation determines the nature of the resolution; the analyst's "world view" is the strongest determining factor. (10) The planner has no right to be wrong, contrasted with Popper's view that scientific solutions are hypotheses offered for refutation. The text on property 8 also warns about incrementalism: attacking a problem at too low a level may make things worse, and "marginal improvement does not guarantee overall improvement". The authors also say each tame problem can be stated exhaustively, whereas for wicked problems, "in order to describe a wicked problem in sufficient detail, one has to develop an exhaustive inventory of all conceivable solutions ahead of time". The evidence type is a conceptual essay with examples. There is no empirical sample.

### Replication and criticism
This is argument, not experiment, so "replication" means uptake and critique. The idea spread from planning into design through Buchanan (see buchanan1992wicked), who credits Rittel's work at the Ulm school and notes that the ten-item list was extended gradually after a 1972 working paper, and who also observes that Rittel never answered why design problems are wicked, so that the theory "has remained only a description of the social reality of designing". A 50th-anniversary article in PMC (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8918592) reports that descriptions of properties 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 as widely circulated are exact quotes, while descriptions of the others are paraphrases or edited quotes. This matters: many popular lists ("wicked problems have no stopping rule, etc.") reword the original, and Buchanan's own list of ten (pp. 15-16 of his essay) is condensed and reordered relative to the 1973 headings. A practical caution is that the label is now applied loosely to any difficult project. Rittel and Webber meant social-policy problems with plural stakeholders and irreversible public consequences, not every hard design task. A lesson on instrument scanning is not wicked in their sense; it is a tame problem with a fairly definite test.

### Implications for LTF
First, use the distinction to sort LTF decisions. Questions with a checkable answer (does the lesson state the right number for a published limit, does a widget render the correct pitch scale) are tame and should be closed by tests, sources and review. Questions about what a beginner needs first, how much to explain, or how to trade motivation against rigor are wicked: they have no stopping rule, so LTF should write down when it stops ("good enough for v1, revisit with learner data") rather than pretend to finish. Second, property 5 and 10 argue for caution about irreversible choices: slugs and IDs never change once published, so name them carefully and ship small. Third, property 9 warns against single-cause explanations of learner drop-off; a dashboard that shows one metric will invite a "world view" reading. Pair metrics with learner interviews. Fourth, the second-generation idea supports shipping a lesson draft early to a few real beginners and arguing from their reactions, instead of specifying everything before building. Finally, do not use "wicked" as an excuse: flight facts remain tame and must be sourced.

## buchanan1992wicked
Buchanan, R. (1992). Wicked problems in design thinking. Design Issues 8(2), 5-21. DOI 10.2307/1511637.

### Core claims
Buchanan argues that design has moved through four stages in the twentieth century: from a trade activity, to a segmented profession, to a field for technical research, and now to "a new liberal art of technological culture". Design resists reduction to any one science because, unlike physics or chemistry, it has no subject matter of its own. His central diagnosis, taken from Rittel's wicked-problems idea, is that design problems are "indeterminate" and "wicked" because "design has no special subject matter of its own apart from what a designer conceives it to be". The subject matter of design is "potentially universal in scope", and the designer must "discover or invent a particular subject out of the problems and issues of specific circumstances". This contrasts with sciences that study the principles and laws of existing subject matters. The second claim is a method for invention, the "doctrine of placements": designers do not work from fixed categories but move a problem between flexible placements, and that movement is a source of new ideas.

### Specifics
Buchanan lists four broad areas in which design is practiced, each described with its professional home. First, symbolic and visual communications (typography, advertising, book and magazine production, scientific illustration, extending to photography, film, television and computer display). Second, material objects (clothing, domestic objects, tools, instruments, machines, vehicles). Third, activities and organized services (logistics, sequences and schedules, strategic planning and decision making). Fourth, complex systems or environments for living, working, playing and learning (systems engineering, architecture, urban planning). He says these are not mere categories but "places of invention shared by all designers", interconnected with "no priority given to any single one", and he names the four as signs, things, actions and thoughts, which "not only interconnected, they interpenetrate and merge in contemporary design thinking with surprising consequences for innovation". Placements differ from categories: categories "have fixed meanings that are accepted within the framework of a theory", whereas placements "have boundaries to shape and constrain meaning, but are not rigidly fixed", and applying one to a specific situation "can generate a new perception of that situation and, hence, a new possibility to be tested". An example in the essay: managers of a retail chain found that customers struggled to find merchandise, and larger signs did not help; a consultant observed shoppers and concluded they look for familiar, representative products, so display strategy placed those products prominently. Buchanan calls this a double repositioning, from signs to action, then from action to signs. He also summarizes the linear model of design (problem definition as analysis, problem solution as synthesis) and reports Rittel's ten properties in a condensed form, noting that "indeterminacy" is different from "undetermined". Evidence type: a conceptual essay with historical examples, Dewey-inspired.

### Replication and criticism
The essay's reach is evident from its own subject: it reports that the wicked-problems approach was one of the central themes the participants of a 1974 design theory conference kept returning to, and it carried Rittel's planning idea into design. Buchanan himself flags limits: he notes that Simon's positivist reading of design can be "reasonably" disputed, and that placement language can harden into categories in the hands of disciples. A secondary-source confusion is the name of the fourth area: one web summary we searched labeled it differently from the essay. The essay's own wording is signs, things, actions and thoughts for the sequence, and "complex systems or environments" for the fourth area, so cite that. Critics of the wicked-problems framing, including Buchanan's own remark, note that it describes rather than explains. It offers no empirical test and is opinion informed by practice.

### Implications for LTF
LTF can borrow the four-area map as an audit: any lesson sits in several areas at once. A checklist widget is a thing (an artifact), a sign (it communicates), and part of an action (flying the checklist) and system (cockpit workflow). When a design problem stalls, reposition it. The retail-sign example translates directly: if beginners miss a panel label, bigger labels may fail, while redesigning the page around what they look for first (the familiar gauge shape) may work. Because design has no fixed subject matter, LTF should keep its design decisions tied to its particular subject, the Cessna 172 learner in a sim, rather than copying general web patterns. The "no special subject matter" point also justifies an explicit decision log: each LTF choice is a discovered particular, not a derivation from rules.

## simon1996sciences
Simon, H. A. (1996). The Sciences of the Artificial (3rd ed.). Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. (First edition 1969.)

### Core claims
Simon proposes that there is a coherent body of knowledge about "artificial" things, meaning things made by human intention to fit goals, and that its core is the process of design. An artifact, he writes, is "a meeting point, an interface in today's terms, between an inner environment, the substance and organization of the artifact itself, and an outer environment, the surroundings in which it operates". If the inner environment is appropriate to the outer one, the artifact serves its purpose. The famous ant-on-the-beach passage makes the companion point that the apparent complexity of behavior may be "a complexity in the surface of the beach, not a complexity in the ant". His chapter "The Science of Design: Creating the Artificial" gives the widest definition of design in the literature: "Everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones". By that definition the intellectual activity that produces material artifacts is "no different fundamentally" from prescribing remedies for a patient, devising a sales plan or a social welfare policy. Design is "the core of all professional training" and "the principal mark that distinguishes the professions from the sciences". He then argues that the professional schools abandoned design for the natural sciences in the mid-twentieth century because design was thought "intellectually soft, intuitive, informal, and cookbooky", and calls for "a body of intellectually tough, analytic, partly formalizable, partly empirical, teachable doctrine about the design process". (Quotations read in the third edition text, see the Confirmed line.)

### Specifics
Four named ideas matter most for design philosophy. First, satisficing: because "real-world optimization, with or without computers, is impossible, the real economic actor is in fact a satisficer, a person who accepts 'good enough' alternatives, not because less is preferred to more but because there is no choice". Design searches usually aim at a satisfactory design rather than an optimum. Second, the generator-test cycle: design can be viewed as the generation of alternatives and then testing against "a whole array of requirements and constraints"; there can be "a whole nested series of such cycles", the generators "implicitly define the decomposition of the design problem", and the tests make sure important indirect consequences are noticed. Third, process as a determinant of style: because designers satisfice, the sequence and the division of labor between generators and tests can shape the final design, so "what we ordinarily call style may stem just as much from these decisions about the design process" as from the goals. His example is an architect designing a house from the outside in versus the inside out. Simon adds that variety within satisfactory constraints may be desirable partly because it lets us "attach value to the search as well as its outcome", and he suggests that city planning could be a creative activity many community members take part in. Fourth, hierarchy and near-decomposability, in "The Architecture of Complexity": the parable of the watchmakers Hora and Tempus. Hora builds watches from stable subassemblies of about ten elements each; Tempus builds each watch in one sequence of about a thousand parts. With the book's example probability of interruption of 0.01 per added part, a straightforward calculation puts Tempus's average assembly time at about four thousand times Hora's. Complex systems that evolve do so faster if they are hierarchical, with stable intermediate forms. Evidence type: theoretical argument with worked models and illustrations, written by a Nobel laureate in economics, not an experiment.

### Replication and criticism
The book is foundational and also a long-standing target. Buchanan (1992) cites Simon's line that the proper study of mankind is the science of design, calls Simon's view "positivist and empiricist", says one may reasonably disagree with aspects of it, and adds in a footnote that a careful reading would show why designers feel somewhat disenchanted with the book while it remains "exceptionally useful work" (read in the JSTOR copy of Buchanan's essay, footnote 12). Donald Schon attacked Simon's picture of design as problem-solving, calling it technical rationality; a secondary account we searched (jmkorhonen.github.io/notes/theory-design-as-problem-solving) reports this and also reports a commentator who says the critique caricatures Simon, since Simon's own Nobel-recognized point was that people cannot be perfectly rational. Roozenburg and Dorst, as summarized in that account, judged Schon's alternative not a radical departure and empirically weak, so the dispute is unresolved. Rittel and Webber's wicked problems (see rittel1973dilemmas) attack the "first generation" phases model that Simon's framework can be read to support, though Simon explicitly treats ill-structured problems and satisficing. Satisficing itself is accepted in behavioral economics as a description of limited search; the claim that it matters, which Simon notes economists like Friedman dismissed, is where the debate lay.

### Implications for LTF
Simon's broad definition licenses LTF to treat a lesson sequence as a design in the same sense as a widget: a course of action meant to change a learner from "cannot" to "can". The inner/outer environment frame suits an app used next to a sim: the page (inner) must fit the learner's attention, lighting, screen and hands (outer), so a fly mode is judged by how well it fits a pilot with a hand on a controller, not by its internal cleverness. Satisficing is a permission and a discipline: for non-critical choices pick the first option that meets written criteria and move on, and keep optimization for safety-relevant content. The generator-test cycle suggests an explicit loop for lesson design: generate two or three layouts of a checklist page, test each against a fixed set of requirements (readable at arm's length, works in both themes, source cited), then record why one was chosen. Hora and Tempus is an argument for LTF's module structure: small, stable, independently verified lesson units (each with its own source and verification status) survive interruption better than a single long dependency chain, and a failed verification should not unravel a whole course.
