# Lane 22: Design philosophy (research notes)

## rittel1973dilemmas
Rittel, H. W. J., and Webber, M. M. (1973). Dilemmas in a general theory of planning. Policy Sciences 4(2), 155-169. DOI 10.1007/BF01405730.

### Core claims
Rittel and Webber argue that the problems of social policy and planning cannot be solved with the methods that made science and engineering successful, because those methods were built for what the authors call "tame" or "benign" problems. Planning problems are "wicked" instead. The paper's boldest claim is that the classical paradigm of science and engineering is "not applicable to the problems of open societal systems", and that professionals were "misled" into assuming they could solve social problems the way scientists solve theirs. They propose a "second generation" model of planning as an argumentative process in which an image of the problem and of the solution "emerges gradually among the participants, as a product of incessant judgment, subjected to critical argument". (All quotations read in the scanned article text, pp. 160-162, see the Confirmed line.)

### Specifics
The tame examples given are solving a mathematical equation, an organic chemist analyzing a compound's structure, and a chess player seeking checkmate in five moves: for each, the mission is clear and it is clear whether the problem has been solved. Wicked problems lack both traits, and include nearly all public policy issues such as freeway location, tax rates, school curricula and crime. The authors list "at least ten" distinguishing properties, each headed in the paper as follows. (1) There is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem; "the formulation of a wicked problem is the problem", because problem understanding and problem resolution are concomitant. (2) Wicked problems have no stopping rule; the planner stops for reasons external to the problem such as time, money or patience, and says "that's good enough". (3) Solutions are not true-or-false, but good-or-bad. (4) There is no immediate and no ultimate test of a solution, since solutions generate waves of consequences over an extended, virtually unbounded period. (5) Every solution is a "one-shot operation" because there is no opportunity to learn by trial and error; every attempt counts significantly. (6) There is no enumerable or exhaustively describable set of potential solutions or permissible operations. (7) Every wicked problem is essentially unique. (8) Every wicked problem can be considered a symptom of another problem. (9) A discrepancy can be explained in numerous ways, and the choice of explanation determines the nature of the resolution; the analyst's "world view" is the strongest determining factor. (10) The planner has no right to be wrong, contrasted with Popper's view that scientific solutions are hypotheses offered for refutation. There is no empirical sample.

### Replication and criticism
This is argument, not experiment, so "replication" means uptake and critique. The idea spread from planning into design through Buchanan (see buchanan1992wicked), who credits Rittel's work at the Ulm school and notes that the ten-item list was extended gradually after a 1972 working paper, and who also observes that Rittel never answered why design problems are wicked, so that the theory "has remained only a description of the social reality of designing". A 50th-anniversary article in PMC (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8918592) reports that descriptions of properties 1, 2, 3, 4 and 7 as widely circulated are exact quotes, while descriptions of the others are paraphrases or edited quotes. A lesson on instrument scanning is not wicked in their sense; it is a tame problem with a fairly definite test.

### Implications for LTF
First, use the distinction to sort LTF decisions. Questions with a checkable answer (does the lesson state the right number for a published limit, does a widget render the correct pitch scale) are tame and should be closed by tests, sources and review. Questions about what a beginner needs first, how much to explain, or how to trade motivation against rigor are wicked: they have no stopping rule, so LTF should write down when it stops ("good enough for v1, revisit with learner data") rather than pretend to finish. Third, property 9 warns against single-cause explanations of learner drop-off; a dashboard that shows one metric will invite a "world view" reading. Finally, do not use "wicked" as an excuse: flight facts remain tame and must be sourced.

## buchanan1992wicked
Buchanan, R. (1992). Wicked problems in design thinking. Design Issues 8(2), 5-21. DOI 10.2307/1511637.

### Core claims
Buchanan argues that design has moved through four stages in the twentieth century: from a trade activity, to a segmented profession, to a field for technical research, and now to "a new liberal art of technological culture". Design resists reduction to any one science because, unlike physics or chemistry, it has no subject matter of its own. His central diagnosis, taken from Rittel's wicked-problems idea, is that design problems are "indeterminate" and "wicked" because "design has no special subject matter of its own apart from what a designer conceives it to be". The subject matter of design is "potentially universal in scope", and the designer must "discover or invent a particular subject out of the problems and issues of specific circumstances". The second claim is a method for invention, the "doctrine of placements": designers do not work from fixed categories but move a problem between flexible placements, and that movement is a source of new ideas.

### Specifics
Buchanan lists four broad areas in which design is practiced, each described with its professional home. First, symbolic and visual communications (typography, advertising, book and magazine production, scientific illustration, extending to photography, film, television and computer display). Second, material objects (clothing, domestic objects, tools, instruments, machines, vehicles). Third, activities and organized services (logistics, sequences and schedules, strategic planning and decision making). Fourth, complex systems or environments for living, working, playing and learning (systems engineering, architecture, urban planning). He says these are not mere categories but "places of invention shared by all designers", interconnected with "no priority given to any single one", and he names the four as signs, things, actions and thoughts, which "not only interconnected, they interpenetrate and merge in contemporary design thinking with surprising consequences for innovation". Placements differ from categories: categories "have fixed meanings that are accepted within the framework of a theory", whereas placements "have boundaries to shape and constrain meaning, but are not rigidly fixed", and applying one to a specific situation "can generate a new perception of that situation and, hence, a new possibility to be tested". An example in the essay: managers of a retail chain found that customers struggled to find merchandise, and larger signs did not help; a consultant observed shoppers and concluded they look for familiar, representative products, so display strategy placed those products prominently. Buchanan calls this a double repositioning, from signs to action, then from action to signs. He also summarizes the linear model of design (problem definition as analysis, problem solution as synthesis) and reports Rittel's ten properties in a condensed form, noting that "indeterminacy" is different from "undetermined". Evidence type: a conceptual essay with historical examples, Dewey-inspired.

### Replication and criticism
The essay's reach is evident from its own subject: it reports that the wicked-problems approach was one of the central themes the participants of a 1974 design theory conference kept returning to, and it carried Rittel's planning idea into design. Buchanan himself flags limits: he notes that Simon's positivist reading of design can be "reasonably" disputed, and that placement language can harden into categories in the hands of disciples. The essay's own wording is signs, things, actions and thoughts for the sequence, and "complex systems or environments" for the fourth area, so cite that. Critics of the wicked-problems framing, including Buchanan's own remark, note that it describes rather than explains. It offers no empirical test and is opinion informed by practice.

### Implications for LTF
LTF can borrow the four-area map as an audit: any lesson sits in several areas at once. A checklist widget is a thing (an artifact), a sign (it communicates), and part of an action (flying the checklist) and system (cockpit workflow). When a design problem stalls, reposition it. The retail-sign example translates directly: if beginners miss a panel label, bigger labels may fail, while redesigning the page around what they look for first (the familiar gauge shape) may work. Because design has no fixed subject matter, LTF should keep its design decisions tied to its particular subject, the Cessna 172 learner in a sim, rather than copying general web patterns. The "no special subject matter" point also justifies an explicit decision log: each LTF choice is a discovered particular, not a derivation from rules.

## simon1996sciences
Simon, H. A. (1996). The Sciences of the Artificial (3rd ed.). Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. (First edition 1969.)

### Core claims
Simon proposes that there is a coherent body of knowledge about "artificial" things, meaning things made by human intention to fit goals, and that its core is the process of design. An artifact, he writes, is "a meeting point, an interface in today's terms, between an inner environment, the substance and organization of the artifact itself, and an outer environment, the surroundings in which it operates". His chapter "The Science of Design: Creating the Artificial" gives the widest definition of design in the literature: "Everyone designs who devises courses of action aimed at changing existing situations into preferred ones". Design is "the core of all professional training" and "the principal mark that distinguishes the professions from the sciences". (Quotations read in the third edition text, see the Confirmed line.)

### Specifics
Four named ideas matter most for design philosophy. First, satisficing: because "real-world optimization, with or without computers, is impossible, the real economic actor is in fact a satisficer, a person who accepts 'good enough' alternatives, not because less is preferred to more but because there is no choice". Second, the generator-test cycle: design can be viewed as the generation of alternatives and then testing against "a whole array of requirements and constraints"; there can be "a whole nested series of such cycles", the generators "implicitly define the decomposition of the design problem", and the tests make sure important indirect consequences are noticed. Third, process as a determinant of style: because designers satisfice, the sequence and the division of labor between generators and tests can shape the final design, so "what we ordinarily call style may stem just as much from these decisions about the design process" as from the goals. Fourth, hierarchy and near-decomposability, in "The Architecture of Complexity": the parable of the watchmakers Hora and Tempus. Hora builds watches from stable subassemblies of about ten elements each; Tempus builds each watch in one sequence of about a thousand parts. Evidence type: theoretical argument with worked models and illustrations, written by a Nobel laureate in economics, not an experiment.

### Replication and criticism
The book is foundational and also a long-standing target. Buchanan (1992) cites Simon's line that the proper study of mankind is the science of design, calls Simon's view "positivist and empiricist", says one may reasonably disagree with aspects of it, and calls the book "exceptionally useful work" (Buchanan, footnote 12). Donald Schon attacked Simon's picture of design as problem-solving, calling it technical rationality; a secondary account we searched (jmkorhonen.github.io/notes/theory-design-as-problem-solving) reports this and also reports a commentator who says the critique caricatures Simon, since Simon's own Nobel-recognized point was that people cannot be perfectly rational. Rittel and Webber's wicked problems (see rittel1973dilemmas) attack the "first generation" phases model that Simon's framework can be read to support, though Simon explicitly treats ill-structured problems and satisficing. On satisficing, Simon himself notes that economists such as Milton Friedman argued the gap between satisfactory and best is of no great importance, while he believes it matters a great deal.

### Implications for LTF
Simon's broad definition licenses LTF to treat a lesson sequence as a design in the same sense as a widget: a course of action meant to change a learner from "cannot" to "can". The inner/outer environment frame suits an app used next to a sim: the page (inner) must fit the learner's attention, screen and hands (outer), so a fly mode is judged by fit, not by internal cleverness. Satisficing is a permission and a discipline: for non-critical choices pick the first option that meets written criteria and move on. The generator-test cycle suggests an explicit loop for lesson design: generate two or three layouts of a checklist page, test each against a fixed set of requirements (readable at arm's length, works in both themes, source cited), then record why one was chosen. Hora and Tempus is an argument for LTF's module structure: small, stable, independently verified lesson units (each with its own source and verification status) survive interruption better than a single long dependency chain, and a failed verification should not unravel a whole course.

## alexander1964notes
Alexander, C. (1964). Notes on the Synthesis of Form. Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press. (Based on his 1962 Harvard doctoral dissertation; paperback edition 1971.)

### Core claims
Alexander treats a design problem as the task of making a form fit its context. Because "good fit" is hard to describe, he proposes working from failure: it is "the easiest thing in the world to name the specific kinds of misfit which prevent good fit". His examples include a kitchen hard to clean, no place to park, the eye-level grill that spits fat, and the front door you cannot find. "Because they are expressed in negative form they are specific, and tangible enough to talk about." Design is therefore the minimization of misfits, and a good form is one with none. The second claim is structural: the misfit variables interact, so one cannot adjust them one at a time; the designer should decompose the problem into subsystems whose members interact densely with one another and weakly with the rest, and design a diagram for each. The third claim is methodological and later repudiated by the author: that a formal mathematical procedure could find that decomposition. (Quotations read in Gabriel's essay, which reproduces them; see the Confirmed line.)

### Specifics
Alexander defines a misfit variable as binary: 0 where the condition fits and 1 where it does not (Gabriel's description). A link between two variables means that changing how one is treated can affect the other. His worked example is the redesign of an agricultural village of about 600 people in India. The problem has 141 requirements, stated positively but each derived from a potential misfit, with about 1,400 interactions among them. He proposed a numerical goodness measure for a partition and a search for the best partition; Gabriel describes this as generate-and-test, and says Alexander wrote computer programs for it (the HIDECS programs, documented only in internal reports). Evidence type: a theoretical monograph with one worked, computed case. It is not an empirical test of whether designers using the method produce better buildings.

### Replication and criticism
The most useful recent source is Richard Gabriel's 2023 essay in the Programming journal ("Notes on Notes on the Synthesis of Form"), in which he tried to replicate Alexander's computed decomposition. He reports confusing clerical-type errors in the raw data in the book's appendix and an odd mathematical treatment, and his "attempted replication also failed" (from the abstract); he then traced five different programs Alexander wrote and describes the chain of thought from cybernetics to later work. So the formal result is not reproducible from the book alone. Alexander himself repudiated the method: his 1971 preface to the paperback says that once the book was written he found it "quite unnecessary to use such a complicated and formal way of getting at the independent diagrams", and in 1971 he was quoted, speaking of the design methods field that "Notes" partly spawned: "I would say forget it, forget the whole thing" (both quoted in Gabriel). Software engineers adopted the ideas of cohesion and coupling from the book, per Gabriel's abstract, and the later Pattern Language line takes Alexander in the opposite direction, toward judged "good fit" and feeling. The cautionary reading is that the misfit idea is robust and the formalization is not, and that selecting which misfit variables to list cannot itself be formalized (as a secondary summary we searched also notes).

### Implications for LTF
The misfit method is the one part of Alexander that LTF can use directly. Instead of defining a good lesson page positively ("clear, engaging"), list concrete misfits between page and learner: the learner cannot find the next step, the key number is hidden below the fold on a phone propped beside the sim, the checklist text is too small at arm's length, a term is used before it is defined, dark mode washes out a gauge. The interaction point also applies: fixing one misfit (bigger text) can create another (the instrument panel no longer fits the viewport), so test fixes together. Alexander's own retreat is the caution: do not pursue a formula for the perfect page layout. Use misfits to find problems, and use judgment and learner observation to choose fixes.

## papanek1972design
Papanek, V. (1972). Design for the Real World: Human Ecology and Social Change. New York: Pantheon Books. (First American edition per Open Library; first published 1971, second edition 1985. The text read here is an Internet Archive scan whose edition is not stated in the text.)

### Core claims
Papanek's polemic holds that designers carry moral responsibility for what they make and that industrial design, as practiced around 1970, was mostly serving fashion, profit and waste instead of human need. The book opens: "There are professions more harmful than industrial design, but only a very few of them." He calls advertising design "probably the phoniest field" and industrial design "a close second", and charges designers with creating unsafe automobiles, "permanent garbage" and polluting processes. His positive program is that design should serve real needs of the poor, the sick, the disabled and the developing world, that designers should work in teams with other disciplines, and that "aesthetic value is an inherent part of function". The book rejects the old argument over whether to make a thing functional or pleasing: "Do you want it to look good, or to work?" he calls "barricades erected between what are really just two of the many aspects of function".

### Specifics
The central tool is the "function complex", a six-part checklist that Papanek says can be used to evaluate any design. Method: the interaction of tools, processes and materials, where "an honest use of materials, never making the material seem that which it is not, is good method". Use: "Does it work?" (his examples: a vitamin bottle should dispense pills singly, an ink bottle should not tip over). Need: genuine economic, psychological, spiritual, technological and intellectual needs, as against manipulated "wants" inculcated by fad and fashion. Telesis: "the deliberate, purposeful utilisation of the processes of nature and society to obtain particular goals" (he cites a 1961 American College Dictionary), meaning a design must reflect the conditions of its time and fit the socio-economic order in which it operates. Association: inherited psychological conditioning that predisposes people for or against a form, as with a television set deciding whether to carry the associations of furniture or of technical equipment. Aesthetics: a tool for shaping form and color into things that move and please us, which he says should be demystified instead of treated as private mystery. The evidence is polemic and anecdote with many case histories (the automobile as "a perversion of design for use"). No experiments or surveys are reported for the six-part model.

### Replication and criticism
The book was widely read and mixed in reception. In a 2012 reappraisal in Metropolis (metropolismag.com/viewpoints/rereading-design-for-the-real-world/), Christopher Hawthorne reports that the 1985 edition claimed to be "the most widely read book on design in the world", places it alongside the reform critiques of its decade, and says the attention to ecological and social responsibility, the ethics of working in poor regions, and the critique of design education still hold, while its analog worldview and underdeveloped political tactics have aged badly. The six-function model is craft theory, not tested science, and several parts overlap (association and aesthetics, need and use). The tone is hyperbolic: some claims, such as his car-safety fatality figures, are stated without sources in the text read here and must not be quoted as established statistics.

### Implications for LTF
Use the function complex as a pre-publication review lens for every lesson, widget and challenge, translated into LTF terms. Method: is the page honest about what it is made of (a diagram labeled as a simplified model, not passed off as the real panel)? Use: does it work for the learner at the sim, such as a checklist that can be followed one-handed? Need: is the page serving what a beginner needs to learn, not what flatters the app (streaks and badges are "wants" unless they serve learning)? Telesis: does it fit the current context, namely a learner flying MSFS 2024 with a keyboard, yoke or controller, not an aircraft cockpit? Association: do the visuals call up the right associations (a trustworthy flight-training feel, not a video game, not a corporate dashboard)? Aesthetics: treat it as part of function, so no separate "make it pretty" pass. The ethical frame applies to the beginner audience's real risk: LTF teaches a skill whose real-world version carries safety consequences, so unsourced aviation facts are the design analog of Papanek's unsafe automobile.

## maeda2006laws
Maeda, J. (2006). The Laws of Simplicity. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. (Simplicity: Design, Technology, Business, Life series.)

### Core claims
Maeda, a designer and computer scientist at MIT, offers simplicity as an organizing value for design, technology, business and life, in a short book built from ten "laws" and three "keys". The summarizing claim, the tenth law, is "Simplicity is about subtracting the obvious, and adding the meaningful" (as quoted at fs.blog/the-laws-of-simplicity/). The book is deliberately small. Rob Tannen's critique notes it is capped at exactly 100 pages and ten principles. It is a work of craft opinion and aphorism, not research: there is no experiment, survey or measure of simplicity in it.

### Specifics
The laws, with Maeda's one-line statements as reproduced by a reader's summary (fs.blog): 1 Reduce: "The simplest way to achieve simplicity is through thoughtful reduction." 2 Organize: "Organization makes a system of many appear fewer." 3 Time: "Savings in time feel like simplicity." 4 Learn: "Knowledge makes everything simpler." 5 Differences: "Simplicity and complexity need each other." 6 Context: "What lies in the periphery of simplicity is definitely not peripheral." 7 Emotion: "More emotions are better than less." 8 Trust: "In simplicity we trust." 9 Failure: "Some things can never be made simple." 10 The One: the subtract-the-obvious line above. The three keys for the technology domain are Away ("More appears like less by simply moving it far, far away"), Open ("Openness simplifies complexity") and Power ("Use less, gain more"). The Failure law includes the line (noted by the Monash reviewer) "the final flaw: too many laws", an acknowledgment that the book's own structure contradicts its subject.

### Replication and criticism
There is nothing to replicate, but the book has drawn sustained critique, from reviewers and practitioners. Alan Dorin's 2006 review (users.monash.edu/~aland/reviews/maeda.rev.html) finds the book overcomplicated for a work on simplicity, with too many laws, keys and acronyms and unclear links between them. Dorin argues Reduce and Organize overlap, Time could be folded into Reduce, and disputes Maeda's Learn-law claim that giving students a seemingly insurmountable challenge is the best motivator: in his experience it motivates only some, while many need large tasks broken into sub-goals. He welcomes Emotion as a counterpoint to "less is more" and notes under Trust that simplifying can remove needed features, so a thing should be "simple but not too simple". Rob Tannen's essay at Boxes and Arrows (boxesandarrows.com/simplicity-the-distribution-of-complexity/) calls the book "an artifact of forced simplicity, with form taking precedent over utility", asks whether ten laws is a finding or a convenient number, and argues that simplicity and complexity are not opposites: making something simpler often relocates complexity (a manual transmission versus an automatic), simplicity has mechanical, perceptual and cognitive attributes, and BMW's iDrive single rotary controller leaned on mechanical simplicity and became confusing. Donald Norman's Living with Complexity (MIT Press, 2011), as summarized in search results, argues that complexity is not the problem, bad design is, and treats simplicity as a state of mind and complexity as a state of the world. The evidence base is therefore expert opinion with credible internal and external critique.

### Implications for LTF
Maeda's "subtract the obvious, add the meaningful" is a useful editing rule for lesson pages, but only with the critiques applied. For a beginner, the Reduce law must not remove the cues that make a skill learnable: Dorin's note that learners need large tasks broken into sub-goals fits LTF's step-by-step lessons and argues against the "insurmountable challenge" framing. Tannen's relocation idea suits fly mode: if the page hides detail, the complexity must go somewhere (a linked "why", a tooltip, a prior lesson), and LTF should decide that placement deliberately and test it. The Time law and SHE (shrink, hide, embody) point to practical wait handling: loading states, long sim setup steps and slow scenery loads should be shortened, hidden behind useful reading, or made tangible with progress. The Trust law, read with Dorin, is a warning: do not simplify a checklist or limit to the point of dropping a required item. Treat the book as vocabulary and provocation, not as evidence, and cite Tannen's and Norman's counterpoints when someone proposes "make it simpler" without saying what moves where.

## venturi1966complexity
Venturi, R. (1966). Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture. New York: Museum of Modern Art. (Introduction by Vincent Scully; 135 pages per the publisher listing quoted in a search summary.)

### Core claims
Venturi's book is subtitled by commentators "a gentle manifesto for a nonstraightforward architecture". It argues against the orthodox Modern movement's insistence on purity, clarity and simplicity, and for an architecture of richness and ambiguity that reflects, in a phrase quoted by Charles Jencks, "the richness and ambiguity of modern experience". Using historical examples, Venturi proposes that multilayered, paradoxical principles can counter the banality of orthodox Modernism. The best-known line answers Mies van der Rohe's "Less is more" with "Less is a bore". The book's central concept, according to Jencks's fiftieth-anniversary assessment in Architectural Record, is "The Obligation Toward the Difficult Whole": unity should arise from multiplicity, which takes effort and skill, and not from the suppression of difference.

### Specifics
Per the Jencks article, the argument is organized as a series of precepts, including "Both-And in Architecture" (set against modernism's exclusive "either-or", also stressed in a Dwell summary) and "The Double-Functioning Element" (a single element that does two jobs at once). Venturi applies the ideas through close readings of Mannerist, Baroque and vernacular buildings, and he illustrates them with his own Vanna Venturi House (1964), about which, Jencks reports, he admitted that "Some of its elements are good on one level and bad on another." A related catchphrase, "More is not less", appears alongside "Less is more" and "Less is a bore" in a summary of the book (architectuul and architizer discussions). Venturi later softened the slogan: a 1986 New York Review of Books piece, as quoted in a search summary, has him saying there is "nothing I want to take back, except maybe the term 'Less is a bore'", and adding that Mies is "one of the great masters of this century". The book analyzes visual and formal qualities in individual elements.

### Replication and criticism
Reception was strong: MoMA published it, Vincent Scully introduced it, and Jencks compares it in influence to Jane Jacobs's The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961), since both challenged Modernist orthodoxy in favor of complexity. Venturi became a father figure for 1970s Postmodern architects, and in 2001, Jencks reports, he disclaimed Postmodernism, likely out of disappointment with followers. Criticisms collected by Jencks: the book emphasizes visual and formal qualities and neglects the productive, technical and communicative aspects that Le Corbusier's Vers une Architecture covered; it analyzes individual elements closely but does not apply the same scrutiny to the "difficult whole"; its final section shows less confidence than the rest; it risks creating a new exclusionary taste; and, as formal analysis, it does not address the sociological or scientific ideas behind complexity theory. Importantly for design generally, the book argues for the aesthetic value of complexity from the observer's richness of experience; it contains no measurement of how occupants or users respond, so it cannot settle whether complex environments serve users better.

### Implications for LTF
Venturi is the strongest counterweight to Maeda (see maeda2006laws) and to minimalist rules in this lane, and LTF needs both. For a flight-training page the question is where richness helps the learner and where it harms. A cockpit is a "difficult whole": a real panel has many instruments that must be seen together, so an LTF widget that strips the panel to one gauge may teach isolated skills but fail to teach scanning the whole. The both-and idea suggests letting an element do two jobs when both are real (a heading that is also a navigation anchor, a gauge illustration that is also a quiz target), but double-functioning must not blur the distinction between a safety-critical instruction and decoration. Because Venturi's argument is formal and aesthetic, not tested on users, LTF should treat it as a license to allow depth and texture in lesson pages, not as evidence that complexity improves learning; check any complexity with beginner testing. Use "obligation toward the difficult whole" as a requirement on the dashboard: if it shows many parts of progress, it must still read as one coherent picture.

## krippendorff2006semantic
Krippendorff, K. (2006). The Semantic Turn: A New Foundation for Design. Boca Raton: CRC Press / Taylor & Francis.

### Core claims
Krippendorff, a communication scholar, cybernetician and designer, argues that design has turned from asking how an artifact should function to asking what it means to the people who deal with it. The foundational axiom, drawn from Wittgenstein's view of meaning as use and quoted in a search summary, is that people do not act on the physical qualities of things but on what those things mean to them. From this he derives "human-centered design", set against "technology-centered design", which addresses the technical workings of artifacts in terms ordinary users do not understand. Per the Wikipedia article, he says "form follows function" no longer holds for intelligent artifacts except the simplest tools: a car must function, but designers also weigh driving experience, ease of operation, feelings of safety and social meaning. The book grew out of "product semantics" (Krippendorff and Butter, 1984-1989) and defines design as bringing forth what would not arise naturally: realizable artifacts that must make sense to their stakeholders.

### Specifics
A fetched account of the book's structure (Wikipedia, "The Semantic Turn"; a secondary account, cross-checked against Routledge and Taylor & Francis listings in search results) lists these ideas. Meaning: attributing meaning precedes action and action reshapes meaning; meanings are constructed and depend on context, culture and person. Designers therefore need "second-order understanding", an understanding of others' understanding, gained by observing actions, in dialogue and by involving stakeholders in the process. Artifacts in use: meanings become "anticipated usabilities"; any artifact is an interface; ideal interfaces are self-evident and intrinsically motivating; drawing on Heidegger, use has three stages (recognition, exploration, unproblematic reliance); designers can build several "semantic layers" for users with different competence, as in photocopiers with separate layers for copying, clearing jams, servicing and factory repair. Artifacts in language: names, narratives and adjectives shape an artifact's fate, so designers should make interfaces "narratable"; the book proposes measuring an artifact's "character" with semantic differential scales, free association, story analysis and pairwise comparison. The closing section builds on Simon (see simon1996sciences): natural science studies what exists and design what should be, so a science for design must support change agency, handle meaning as well as causation, work in concrete detail with stakeholders and favor innovation over prediction. The earlier Design Issues essay (1989, DOI 10.2307/1511512) states the slogan that design is making sense of things; I confirmed its record on Crossref but did not read it.

### Replication and criticism
This is a theoretical foundation with proposed methods, not an empirical program, so there is no replication. Its reach is documented: the Wikipedia article says the semantics of artifacts is taught at many design programs and has influenced ergonomics, marketing and cognitive engineering, and lists reviewers from design theory, interaction design and cybernetics. The same article records, with a citation-needed flag, that advocates of more critical design approaches have called the approach overly simplistic, without specifics, so that claim is weak. Treat the methods as proposals, not validated procedures.

### Implications for LTF
For beginners the meaning of an interface is part of the product. The three stages of use (recognition, exploration, unproblematic reliance) map onto learning the LTF interface itself: the first visit needs recognition cues, repeated visits need low-friction exploration, and fly mode should reach unproblematic reliance fast, so it should look and behave consistently with lessons. Semantic layers are a pattern for content depth: a primary layer (what to do), a second layer (why), a third layer (reference and source), each with its own competence level, as the copier example does. Second-order understanding means interviewing beginners about what they think each widget does, using free association or short story prompts to catch mismatches before launch. The ecology idea warns that LTF lives inside an ecosystem (MSFS 2024, community add-ons, other courses and forums) so its terms should match the names learners see there, and any LTF-specific vocabulary (such as a branded name for a drill) should be tested for meaning.

## hume1757standard
Hume, D. (1757). Of the Standard of Taste. In Four Dissertations. (Text read here: the Hume Archives edition hosted at users.rowan.edu, based on the 1875 Green and Grose edition of Hume's Writings.)

### Core claims
Hume starts from a puzzle: taste varies enormously, and the common saying is that beauty is a matter of private sentiment. He grants the premise. "Beauty is no quality in things themselves: It exists merely in the mind which contemplates them; and each mind perceives a different beauty." He then argues that this does not leave us with no standard at all. Some judgments of taste are better than others, because certain qualities in objects "are fitted by nature to produce those particular feelings", and because human faculties can be impaired or improved. He writes that "the general principles of taste are uniform in human nature", and that where men vary in judgment some "defect or perversion in the faculties" can usually be found, "from prejudice, from want of practice, or want of delicacy". Where the difference lies in blameless variations of temperament, or of the manners and opinions of an age and country, there is room for different preferences, and no ranking is possible.

### Specifics
The positive proposal is the "true judge". Hume's formula, as quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia entry and found in the full text, is "Strong sense, united to delicate sentiment, improved by practice, perfected by comparison, and cleared of all prejudice, can alone entitle critics to this valuable character; and the joint verdict of such, wherever they are to be found, is the true standard of taste and beauty." This gives five qualities: strong sense, delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison and freedom from prejudice. Delicacy is illustrated with the story from Don Quixote of Sancho's two kinsmen, who judged a hogshead of wine: one found a taste of leather, the other a taste of iron, and they were ridiculed until the cask was emptied and an old key on a leathern thong was found at the bottom. Hume uses the story to argue that "general rules of beauty" are drawn from "established models, and from the observation of what pleases or displeases", and that we can test a claim of delicacy against them. He also admits the practical difficulty: "But where are such critics to be found? By what marks are they to be known?" and answers that these are "questions of fact, not of sentiment". A passage cited in the Stanford entry on utility and beauty says that a house ill-contrived for its owner displeases, and a building that looks unstable is ugly even when it is solid, since apparent tendencies, not actual utility, affect the mind. The evidence is philosophical argument with anecdote.

### Replication and criticism
There is nothing to replicate in the experimental sense, but there is a long critical literature, summarized in the Stanford Encyclopedia's "Hume's Aesthetics" (plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2020/entries/hume-aesthetics/). The best-known objection is circularity: the best art is what the true critics endorse, yet the true critics are identified by endorsing the best art; Brown (1938) and Noxon (1961) saw this, and Kivy (1967) recast it as a regress. The encyclopedia's own view is that Hume is not a relativist: not every sentiment is equally good.

### Implications for LTF
Hume gives LTF a defensible way to talk about "good design" without either dogma or relativism. Preferences about visual style within a legible, accessible space (which blue, which corner radius) are Hume's blameless variations and should not be argued at length. Departures that stem from "want of practice, or want of delicacy" are different: a page that a beginner finds unreadable should be fixed even if the team likes it. The five qualities become a review checklist for who gets to judge LTF design: informed (strong sense), practiced (has watched beginners use it), comparing (has seen alternatives, including other flight courses), unprejudiced (not simply defending their own work) and sensitive to small defects (Sancho's kinsmen: someone must notice the leather taste, such as a mislabeled axis or a flicker). The key on a thong is the lesson that fine perception is vindicated by facts: when a reviewer says a screen feels wrong, find the fact (contrast ratio, tap target, reading order).

## ruskin1849seven
Ruskin, J. (1849). The Seven Lamps of Architecture. London: Smith, Elder, and Co. (First edition, with illustrations drawn and etched by the author; read in the Internet Archive scan of that edition.)

### Core claims
Ruskin argues that architecture is a moral and expressive art governed by seven principles, which he calls lamps: Sacrifice, Truth, Power, Beauty, Life, Memory and Obedience. He begins with a definition that separates architecture from building: "Architecture is the art which so disposes and adorns the edifices raised by man for whatsoever uses, that the sight of them contribute to his mental health, power, and pleasure." A building does not "become architecture merely by the stability of what it erects", so a ship made swift or a church made comfortable is building, and architecture is the further art that "impresses on its form certain characters venerable or beautiful, but otherwise unnecessary". The introduction states the guiding maxim, from an artist friend: "Know what you have to do, and do it", with the observation that failure usually comes from "a confused understanding of the thing actually to be done" more than from lack of means.

### Specifics
The seven chapters, per the contents page of the 1849 edition, are Sacrifice, Truth, Power, Beauty, Life, Memory and Obedience, with notes. The Lamp of Truth lists "architectural deceits" under three heads: (1) "the suggestion of a mode of structure or support, other than the true one"; (2) "the painting of surfaces to represent some other material than that of which they actually consist", or the deceptive representation of sculptured ornament; (3) "the use of cast or machine-made ornaments of any kind". A quoted line sums up the ethic: "we can command an honest architecture: the meagreness of poverty may be pardoned, the sternness of utility respected; but what is there but scorn for the meanness of deception?" The Lamp of Life holds that hand-work can be told from machine-work and that "to those who love Architecture, the life and accent of the hand are everything"; he compares a design cut by a machine to verses "jangled by rote" and says imperfect handling that shows care is "above all price". On ornament he declares: "Ornament cannot be overcharged if it be good, and is always overcharged when it is bad." The Lamp of Beauty argues that the colors of architecture should be those of natural stones, and that a building should be treated as one organized creature, colored as nature colors one thing (a shell, a flower), not as it colors groups of things. The evidence is a craft and moral treatise with sketched examples from buildings he had studied; it offers no experiments.

### Replication and criticism
Ruskin's influence is well documented, his rules are not. Britannica's account of Ruskin (via search) says the Lamp of Memory shaped Morris's respect for the original fabric of old buildings, which fed later conservation practice, and that his writing on imperfection in Gothic art (The Nature of Gothic, in The Stones of Venice, not this book) underlies both his critique of industrial capitalism and the Arts and Crafts movement. Critiques: one architecture-history site (buffaloah.com, via search) says Ruskin was too attached to ornament and too hostile to machines and standardized construction to be counted a forerunner of functionalism; the Wikipedia article reports that Ruskin himself later dismissed the essay as a "wretched rant" and that the historian George Hersey called it a "moral gloss" on the earlier theorist Alison. I did not confirm those two reports in a primary source. 

### Implications for LTF
Ruskin's "deceits" translate well into an honesty test for LTF visuals and copy. Structural deceit: a widget that implies it is the real Cessna panel when it is a simplified schematic. Surface deceit: skeuomorphic chrome that suggests a physical instrument the learner cannot touch, or a fake sim screenshot. LTF can adopt a rule: every visual element must be what it appears to be, and simplified models must be labeled as models. His point that failure comes from a confused understanding of the thing to be done is a plain method for lesson writing: state the one thing the learner must be able to do after the page, then check each element serves it. Do not adopt his hostility to machine production, since LTF's accuracy depends on generated, tokenized values rendered consistently.

## loos1910ornament
Loos, A. (1910). Ornament and Crime. Lecture given in Vienna on 21 January 1910 (the text is often dated 1908). Text read here: an English translation (translator not named on the page) hosted by the University of Washington Vienna documents collection.

### Core claims
Loos argues that the progress of culture means removing ornament from objects of daily use, and that making new ornament in a modern age is a waste and a symptom of backwardness. The essay opens with a developmental analogy: the human embryo goes through the phases of animal life, a child passes through the changes of mankind's history, and "the child is amoral. So is the Papuan, to us." From this base he claims that the Papuan tattoos everything he can reach without being a criminal, while "the modern man who tattoos himself is a criminal or a degenerate". The core economic claim is that ornament is "wasted labour and hence wasted health", and now also "wasted material", so that it adds up to wasted capital. If there were no ornament, he says, a man would have to work only four hours instead of eight, since "half the work done at present is still for ornamentation" (all quotations read in the translation hosted at depts.washington.edu/vienna/documents/Loos/Loos_Ornament.htm). A third claim is that the absence of ornament is a mark of advanced humanity: "Lack of ornament is a sign of spiritual strength" in this translation, and the greatness of the age lies in "its inability to produce a new form of decoration".

### Specifics
The argument is a lecture, not a treatise, and it moves through several lines. The producer argument is that ornament harms workers: he says the producers of ornament "must work twenty hours to earn the wages a modern worker gets in eight", and that "the lack of ornament means shorter working hours and consequently higher wages" (a claim stated without evidence in the text). The evidence is polemical assertion with anecdote, including an unsourced claim that eighty per cent of prisoners in some prisons are tattooed. Its ethnological and criminological premises are nineteenth-century opinions, not findings I could confirm.

### Replication and criticism
There is no empirical test, and the premises have not survived. The analogy that treats children, non-European peoples and criminals as earlier stages of one human development is a colonial and racial hierarchy, not science, and a modern reader should not repeat it. Canales and Herscher, "Criminal Skins: Tattoos and Modern Architecture in the Work of Adolf Loos," Architectural History 48 (2005), 235-256, DOI 10.1017/S0066622X00003798, argue from the abstract page we fetched that the essay's force came largely from nineteenth-century "criminal anthropology", that it was read only within the inter-war avant-gardes (Le Corbusier promoted Loos in the 1920s), and that it helped move architecture away from historicism and anticipated "less is more" and International Style functionalism. A dating note matters for citations: a search summary of the Wikipedia article says the lecture was first given in 1910, that the original manuscript reads 1910, and that an editor changed the year to 1908 in a 1931 reprint; the translation we read still contains the "living in 1908" line. Venturi's "Less is a bore" (see venturi1966complexity) and Papanek's insistence that aesthetics is part of function (see papanek1972design) can be read as rejoinders, and Ruskin (see ruskin1849seven) is the opposite pole on ornament.

### Implications for LTF
Loos's useful point is economic: decoration costs production and reading time, and it dates. For LTF it justifies a default of plain, durable surfaces for the lesson body and fly mode, where every element competes for attention at arm's length. The essay's point that ornament is no longer an expression of the culture that uses it also bears on maintenance: trend-driven styling will need redoing, and plain surfaces are easier to keep consistent across light and dark themes. Loos's "lack of ornament" slogan has been carried into minimalism that strips the cues beginners rely on, and the argument is rhetoric with a dismissive and prejudiced base, so cite it as history and polemic, not as evidence. A safe use: quote the ideas of wasted labor and obsolescence, and avoid the tattoo and Papuan analogy altogether in any LTF writing.

## sullivan1896tall
Sullivan, L. H. (1896). The tall office building artistically considered. Lippincott's Monthly Magazine, March 1896, 403-409. Philadelphia: J. B. Lippincott Company.

### Core claims
Sullivan sets out to design a new building type, the steel-framed tall office building, as "a problem to be solved". He argues that "it is of the very essence of every problem that it contains and suggests its own solution", which he calls natural law, and that the designer should proceed from general to special, from coarser to finer considerations. He lists the practical conditions: a below-ground story for engines and plant, a ground floor of stores or banks needing large area and light, a pile of identical offices tier upon tier ("an office being similar to a cell in a honey-comb"), and an attic where pipes and machinery complete the "circulatory system". The essay's famous formulation is that "form ever follows function, and this is the law. Where function does not change, form does not change." He calls it "the pervading law of all things organic and inorganic, of all things physical and metaphysical, of all things human and all things superhuman", and uses the eagle in flight, the apple blossom and the work-horse as examples (quotations checked in the scan of the magazine issue, see the Confirmed line).

### Specifics
Note the wording: Sullivan wrote "form ever follows function", and the later short slogan "form follows function" is a compression. The essay does not argue for plain utilitarian design. It adds a second requirement, introduced with "We must now heed the imperative voice of emotion": the building's chief characteristic is that it is lofty, so "it must be tall, every inch of it tall", "every inch a proud and soaring thing, rising in sheer exultation that from bottom to top it is a unit without a single dissenting line". The designer must be "no coward, no denier, no bookworm, no dilettante." Sullivan rejects the common theories that the tall building should be a classical column (base, shaft and capital), a mystical trinity, or a logical statement, and argues instead that the design must express the actual divisions of use. The text is an essay of craft theory by a practicing architect, resting on analogy with natural forms and with no experiments or user evidence.

### Replication and criticism
The slogan became a battle cry of Modernist architects after the 1930s, as a search summary of Wikipedia's "Form follows function" article reports, and Wright extended it into "form and function are one". Jonathan Ochshorn's paper "Sullivan's Eagle: Form and Function Artistically Considered" (ACSA 108th Annual Meeting Proceedings, 2020, DOI 10.35483/ACSA.AM.108.83; abstract read) is the sharpest modern critique: the relation is often true for building elements, but Sullivan's defense rests on biological and inorganic analogies that do not hold up, and his writings use a few functional considerations to support a preferred form while overlooking many others. In Ochshorn's reading the formulation is a justification of a formal preference, not a neutral analysis. Venturi (see venturi1966complexity) argued the other side, that a building's richness comes from contradictory functions.

### Implications for LTF
Sullivan's method is a good first move for any new LTF component: write out the practical conditions before drawing. For an instrument widget the conditions might be: needs to show current value, needs to show target range, must be legible at 1 m on a laptop beside the sim, must work in both themes. The "voice of emotion" paragraph is the useful correction to naive functionalism: a flight-training app has a purpose beyond information transfer, to make a nervous beginner feel capable, so tone and feel are part of function, as Papanek also says (see papanek1972design). The unchanging-function, unchanging-form rule gives a consistency principle: elements with the same function (every "current step" marker, every warning callout, every value from the aircraft data file) get the same form everywhere, and different functions get distinguishable forms. Treat "form follows function" as a heuristic with the caveat that LTF's function statements must be written, argued and tested; a function claimed after the fact is Ochshorn's justification of preference.

The magazine's volume number was not visible in the OCR, so it is left blank.

## rams2026ten
Rams, D. (undated web page, read 2026). Ten principles for good design. Vitsoe. Principles formulated in the late 1970s per the page; other sources date them between 1975 and 1985. A related book is Less but Better / Weniger, aber besser (Hamburg: Jo Klatt Design + Design, 1994 per Open Library), which I did not read.

### Core claims
Dieter Rams, the industrial designer long associated with Braun, asked himself in the late 1970s whether his own design was good design, because he was concerned about "an impenetrable confusion of forms, colours and noises" (Vitsoe's account of the origin). The ten principles are his answer, and the last gives them a motto: good design is "as little design as possible", summarized as "Less, but better", with the added lines "Back to purity, back to simplicity" and "Simplicity is the key to excellence". The principles are a statement of values by a craftsman-designer, offered as a set of theses and not as a method, and this is the document most often quoted in digital product design.

### Specifics
The ten statements on the Vitsoe page are, in order: Good design is innovative (innovation develops alongside technology and is never an end in itself). Good design makes a product useful (a product must meet functional, psychological and aesthetic criteria, and design emphasizes usefulness and removes anything that detracts from it). Good design is aesthetic (aesthetic quality is integral to usefulness, because everyday products affect our well-being; only well-executed objects can be beautiful). Good design makes a product understandable (it clarifies the product's structure and ideally makes it self-explanatory). Good design is unobtrusive (products that fulfill a purpose are like tools, not decorative objects or art, so their design should be neutral and restrained, leaving room for the user's self-expression). Good design is honest (it does not overstate innovation, power or value, and does not make promises it cannot keep). Good design is long-lasting (it avoids being fashionable and therefore never appears antiquated, lasting for years even in a throwaway society). Good design is thorough down to the last detail (nothing is arbitrary or left to chance; care and accuracy are respect for the user). Good design is environmentally friendly (it conserves resources and minimizes physical and visual pollution through the product's life cycle). Good design is as little design as possible (concentrate on the essential aspects and avoid burdening products with non-essentials).

### Replication and criticism
First, dating and authorship are loose: sources give 1973, "late 1970s" and "between 1975 and 1985", so cite the Vitsoe formulation and avoid a specific year unless you have the book. The Jansen article reports Rams saying they are "theses, mind you, not rules!" and wanting younger generations to develop them further; the same article asks to what extent the theses transfer to architecture "1:1", warns that unobtrusiveness can slide into monotony, and argues that classical modernism was more expressive than "soulless" post-war modernism. A three-part UX Planet series (not fetched, summarized in search results) concludes the principles are better understood as ten principles for good industrial design of homeware and are subjective. No empirical study of the principles' effect on user outcomes was found by me; they are expert opinion tied to a specific body of work (consumer electronics and furniture with a long product life), so any claim that following them improves digital learning products is unsupported.

### Implications for LTF
The ten principles fit a flight-training app unusually well, if applied as a review checklist and not as a proof. Understandable: a widget should explain itself, so a pitch-attitude diagram needs labels where a beginner looks, not a legend elsewhere. Unobtrusive: the lesson body and fly mode are tools used next to the sim, so they should be neutral and restrained and leave the learner's attention for the sim. Honest: LTF should not overstate what a sim lesson does (it does not replace instruction), should never label a simplified model as the real aircraft panel, and should show verification status (the project's "verify" callouts) rather than hide it. Thorough to the last detail: a wrong digit in a limit or a checklist item is the most disrespectful kind of carelessness, so detail checking (token use, sources) is part of design.

## pye1978nature
Pye, D. (1978). The Nature and Aesthetics of Design. London: Barrie and Jenkins. (A revision of The Nature of Design, 1964; 160 pages in later printings.)

### Core claims
Pye, a woodworker and professor of furniture design, wrote the 1964 book The Nature of Design and revised and retitled it in 1978. His central move is to reject "form follows function" as "fuzzy and unhelpful", in the words of the one detailed review we could read (Jon Jagger, Overload, accu.org/journals/overload/11/54/jagger_366/). According to that review, Pye argues in chapter 5 that many techniques together impose "hardly any limitations on the shape" of a design, so form does not follow function, and he urges dropping the idea of form in favor of the result: every device produces a "concrete, measurable, objective result", which he considers the only sound basis for a theory of design. A second claim is that design is a matter of conflicting requirements: chapter 8, per the review, argues that requirements always conflict, so every design is arbitrary to a degree, and there is no perfect design, "only a practical balance". A third is that economy and style are matters of choice, and that economy, not physics, sets the most limits.

### Specifics
The review reports the following chapter content, which I treat as secondhand. Chapter 2 distinguishes invention from design: "invention is the process of discovering a principle whereas design is the process of applying that principle". Chapter 3 lists six requirements that limit the designer's freedom to different degrees; the review names component strength, access for maintenance and acceptable appearance, and a teaching slide deck found in search lists them as arrangement, geometric relation of components, strength, access, acceptable cost and acceptable appearance (I could not open the deck; the list is therefore unconfirmed in detail). Chapter 5 contrasts the "workmanship of certainty" (making where risks are minimal, even by hand) with the "workmanship of risk", where "the quality of the result is not pre-determined, but depends on the judgment, dexterity and care the maker exercises". The method is craft knowledge reasoned into a theory; it has no experiments, and my access is to a review, not the book.

### Replication and criticism
The book is a theory essay, so the relevant checks are reception and fit. Introductions to later editions described, in a summary we found in search results, Pye's "brilliant refutation" of "form follows function" and functionalism; that is an admiring reception, not independent confirmation. Jagger, a software writer, finds the ideas strongly relevant to software (size, names, refactoring, workmanship), and notes limits: physical constraints such as parts being unable to occupy the same place apply much less to software, and chapters 4 and 17 have few software parallels. Sullivan's line (see sullivan1896tall) and Ochshorn's later critique of it make the same objection from the other side: function underdetermines form. Pye's claim that every design is arbitrary to a degree can be misused to excuse sloppy decisions, and his workmanship-of-risk idea is about physical craft where the maker's skill is exercised on each piece, so applying it to software or lessons is an analogy. Treat the whole as expert opinion by an experienced maker.

### Implications for LTF
Pye gives LTF a vocabulary for tradeoffs. A lesson page is a balance of conflicting requirements: accuracy (needs detail), brevity (needs omission), visual clarity, authoring cost and build size. The six-requirements idea suggests writing the requirements that bound each component up front, including "acceptable appearance" as a requirement with its own test (it must look trustworthy and pass legibility checks) but not as the driver. The workmanship-of-certainty/risk distinction maps to LTF's content pipeline: tokens, schemas and validation are certainty (the check is automatic), while lesson prose and widget feel are risk (they depend on the author's judgment), so spend human review time on the latter and automate the former. "Small things matter" and "a good surface" support a detail pass: a misaligned label repeated across a hundred lesson pages is multiplied. Pye's toothache line supports a limit on ambition: LTF mostly removes discomforts (confusion, error, anxiety) and should not promise to create passion for flying.

## jones1856grammar
Jones, O. (1856). The Grammar of Ornament. London: Day and Son. (Illustrated by examples from various styles of ornament, with 100 and twelve plates; read in an Internet Archive scan of the 1856 edition, preface dated December 15, 1856.)

### Core claims
Jones's book is a pattern book and a theory at once. It presents plates of ornament from many cultures and periods, preceded by thirty-seven numbered propositions that he offers as general principles in the arrangement of form and color. Proposition 36 says so directly: "The principles discoverable in the works of the past belong to us; not so the results. It is taking the end for the means." Proposition 37 adds that "no improvement can take place in the Art of the present generation until all classes, Artists, Manufacturers, and the Public, are better educated in Art, and the existence of general principles is more fully recognised." The book is craft theory built from a visual survey and not an experiment, and it is a nineteenth-century attempt at the kind of rule set that Alexander and Rams later wrote in their own ways.

### Specifics
The propositions fall into general principles, form, and color. Examples verbatim from the scan: (1) "The Decorative Arts arise from, and should properly be attendant upon, Architecture." (3) all works of the decorative arts "should possess fitness, proportion, harmony, the result of all which is repose." (4) "True beauty results from that repose which the mind feels when the eye, the intellect, and the affections, are satisfied from the absence of any want." (5) "Construction should be decorated. Decoration should never be purposely constructed." (6) beauty of form is produced by lines "growing out one from the other in gradual undulations", with "nothing could be removed and leave the design equally good or better". (7) general forms first, then subdivision by general lines, then ornament that "may again be subdivided and enriched for closer inspection". (8) "All ornament should be based upon a geometrical construction." (9) proportion: the whole and each part "should be a multiple of some simple unit", and the most beautiful proportions "will be those which it will be most difficult for the eye to detect". (13) flowers or natural objects should not be used as ornament, but "conventional representations founded upon them". Colour propositions run from 14 to 34: color assists form and distinguishes parts; primaries go on small surfaces and secondaries and tertiaries on larger masses; proposition 18 gives balancing proportions credited to Field's "chromatic equivalents"; and propositions 24 to 28 follow Chevreul's simultaneous contrast, for example "Colours on white grounds appear darker; on black grounds, lighter". 

### Replication and criticism
Reception is mixed and mostly historical. Search results report that the book has never gone out of print, that Jones paid for the chromolithographed printing himself with Day and Son, and that its plates are historical records of real ornaments, not radical designs. Reviews and re-readings report limits: a 2023 review (The Art Newspaper, of Olivia Horsfall Turner's monograph) calls the compendium colorful and at times "bewildering", and says the prose of the chapter on "Ornament of Savage Tribes" is hard to read today; a 2019 re-reading by Priya Khanchandani and Sam Jacob drew out colonizing aspects of Jones's point of view. The color propositions rest on mid-nineteenth-century color theory (Field and Chevreul) and I did not verify them against modern color science, so do not quote the proportions as rules; the color lane of this knowledge base covers that science. I found no direct critique of the Grammar by Ruskin.

### Implications for LTF
Jones is useful as an example of how to write design rules and where they overreach. His better propositions are structural and transfer: proposition 7 (general forms first, then subdivision, then detail "for closer inspection") is a layout principle for lesson pages and instrument widgets, with a clear page-level structure, a mid-level grouping, and fine detail for those who lean in. Proposition 28 (colors should never impinge on each other) and the simultaneous contrast propositions anticipate real effects that matter for dark and light themes, where adjacent colors alter each other's apparent tone, and each LTF theme should be checked in context, not by swatch. Avoid reading his proportion and color formulas as validated science, and avoid his taxonomy of "savage" ornament in any LTF writing.
