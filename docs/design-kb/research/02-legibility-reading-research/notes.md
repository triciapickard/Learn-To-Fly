## Javal1879

**Core claims.** Louis Emile Javal, a Paris ophthalmologist, published a series of articles in the Annales d'oculistique in 1878 and 1879 under the title "Essai sur la physiologie de la lecture". His central finding overturned a natural assumption. Introspection suggests that the eyes glide smoothly along a line of print. Javal showed that they do not. They move in quick jumps and rest between jumps, and the reading happens during the rests. The jumps were later called saccades (French for a jerk or check, as in a rider's reining in a horse). Javal also showed that a line of ordinary book text is covered in only a few stops, so each stop takes in several letters or a whole short word, not a single character. This was the first empirical blow against the idea that reading is serial letter-by-letter decoding. Because the work came from an eye doctor, it was practical in aim: Javal wanted to know what makes print easy on the eye, and he later wrote on typography, lighting and the hygiene of reading.

**Specifics.** The method was low-tech. Javal and his colleague Lamare could not photograph or record eye position. Lamare listened to the eyelid of the reader through a small acoustic device and heard clicks when the eye moved; Javal also observed the eye directly with the help of mirrors. The counts were therefore rough: a few fixations per line, with the stops sometimes described as irregular in spacing and a return sweep at the end of the line. Exact fixation durations and saccade amplitudes came later, from Erdmann and Dodge (1898) who used photographic methods, and from Huey and Buswell. The modern reference values (about a quarter of a second per fixation, saccades of about 7 to 9 characters in silent reading) belong to Rayner (1998) and should not be attributed to Javal. Javal is also associated in the secondary literature with the claim that the upper half of a printed line is more informative than the lower half, but I could not trace that attribution to a specific page this session, so treat it as not confirmed. Javal's name is also attached to the "Javal rule" in ophthalmology (an estimate of astigmatism), which is unrelated to this lane. A later book (Physiologie de la lecture et de l'ecriture, 1905) collects his work; its details are not confirmed here. No sample size or effect size can be reported honestly for these pioneering observations.

**Criticism and status.** The finding that reading proceeds by saccades and fixations is among the most replicated results in perception research, and no serious dispute remains. What was superseded is the measurement. Javal's counts were approximate, and his listening technique could be affected by other eyelid movements. The bibliographic details of the Annales articles (volumes and page ranges) were not verified by search in this session, and the source is marked as memory-verified accordingly.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Design for landing points. Because the eye lands rather than sweeps, a lesson page should give the eye clear places to land: headings, first words of paragraphs, bold key terms and short callouts.
2. A consistent left edge makes the return sweep to the next line easier, so avoid ragged indentation and centered paragraphs for anything longer than a heading.
3. Content is taken in during fixations, so one primary item per screen region is better than many small fragments scattered across a card.
4. In fly mode, where the learner glances at the screen between sim actions, expect one or two fixations. Put the one required fact in the place the eye is most likely to land first (top left of the content region in left-to-right reading).
5. Do not rely on motion or animation to move the reader's eye along text; the eye jumps on its own schedule.

## Huey1908

**Core claims.** Edmund Burke Huey, an American psychologist, published The Psychology and Pedagogy of Reading with Macmillan in 1908. It was the first comprehensive scientific treatment of reading in English, assembling the experimental work of the late nineteenth century (Javal on saccades, Cattell on the speed of recognizing words and letters, Erdmann and Dodge on the perceptual span and on suppression during jumps) with Huey's own experiments and a review of how reading is taught. Its structure runs from the physiology of the eye in reading, through the perception of words and the role of inner speech, to the history of teaching methods and the question of what type and print conditions suit the reader. Huey argued that skilled reading is a fast, partly automatic perceptual act in which context helps the reader to recognize words from partial information, and that teaching should follow what is known about the act, not the other way round.

**Specifics.** On eye movements, Huey describes his own apparatus: a light pointer attached to a small cup placed on the anesthetized cornea, whose movements were recorded on a smoked drum. This was an invasive method that gave direct traces of the jumps and pauses in a line. Huey reviewed evidence that fixations last roughly a fifth to a third of a second and that the eye does not take in new information while jumping. He also reviewed Cattell's finding from 1886 that words are recognized as fast as single letters in brief exposure, which Huey took as evidence that skilled readers perceive words as wholes. In the legibility chapters he considered which letter parts carry the most information, and reported that the upper half of letters is more informative than the lower half. He also discussed readers' preferences and fatigue from print. Exact figures from the book (sample sizes, reading times) were not checked in this session and are not given here. One often-quoted sentence from the first chapter says that to analyze what we do when we read would be the acme of a psychologist's achievements; the exact wording and page were not confirmed, so it is not quoted.

**Criticism and status.** Huey's word-shape claim, that readers recognize words by their outline silhouette, was later weakened. Experiments with alternating case (for example words written in mixed upper and lower case remain readable) and the work of Pelli and colleagues in the early 2000s on letter-by-letter feature detection pointed to parallel letter recognition, not shape. Rayner's reviews treat shape as a minor cue. The book is nonetheless valuable as the source of many questions that later research answered, and for its pedagogical argument that reading teaching should rest on evidence. The cornea-cup method was abandoned as unsafe. The book is a historical document, not a basis for numbers.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Familiar words are recognized quickly and almost automatically, so use the same term for the same thing across lessons (for example always "airspeed indicator") and let learners build recognition.
2. The upper part of letters carries more information, so avoid containers that clip the tops of text and avoid line heights so tight that ascenders touch descenders above.
3. Sentence case keeps ascender and descender patterns that all caps removes; use sentence case for anything over a few words.
4. Teaching should follow the act of reading: instructions in fly mode should be short, familiar-word sentences that can be taken in at one or two glances.
5. Treat the shape theory with caution: do not justify a design choice by saying words are recognized by their shape.

## Tinker1963

**Core claims.** Miles A. Tinker of the University of Minnesota spent a career, much of it with Donald G. Paterson, testing what makes printed material easy to read. Legibility of Print (Iowa State University Press, 1963) is his summary of studies mostly from the 1920s to the 1950s. It is the canonical review of type size, line width, leading, typeface, case, color, paper and the interactions among them. Its governing principle is empirical: legibility is established by how fast and accurately a reader can read under controlled conditions, not by designers' opinion. It reports that the commonly used text types are about equally readable, and that what matters is size, line length and leading in sensible combination, adequate contrast and reasonable page conditions. The chapter list includes kinds of type, size of type, width of line, leading, the relationship of leading, type size and line width, type form (lowercase, capitals, italics, bold), color of print and background, and paper surface.

**Specifics.** The measures were speed of reading (words or comprehension per minute on controlled passages, often Chapman-Cook speed test material), visibility (the distance at which print can be read), perception time in brief exposures, and fatigue indicators such as blink rate. Findings reported in secondary summaries and Tinker's own papers include: (a) type sizes around 9 to 11 point read well on paper, one summary giving 11 point as faster than 10 point and 12 point as slightly slower; (b) a 1963 paper reported that 7, 8 and 9 point in a 12-pica line with 2-point leading read most rapidly and equally fast; (c) line widths from roughly 14 to 31 picas read at similar speed, with very long and very short lines slower; (d) leading of a point or two improves reading and is preferred by readers; (e) all capitals read more slowly than lowercase, a figure of about 10 to 15 percent is commonly cited and not confirmed here; (f) bold reads at about the same speed as roman, italic a little slower; (g) black on white, or generally dark on light, is preferred over light on dark, and brightness contrast matters more than hue. I could not open the book's text this session, so these figures are marked as taken from secondary summaries and should be checked against the book before being quoted as Tinker's numbers.

**Criticism and status.** Directions replicate: lowercase beats capitals, adequate contrast helps, and a mid-sized measure is comfortable. Many details (the specific best size) depend on the typeface, paper and reading distance of the era. Later critics, among them Zachrisson (1965) and Spencer (1968) in The Visible Word, argued that some studies confounded variables or used measures insensitive to small differences. Screens, backlighting and variable fonts are outside the book's scope. The finding that common faces differ little in speed has been supported by the later work of Arditi and Cho (2005) and Bigelow (2019).

**Use for LTF.**
1. Prioritize size, contrast, measure and leading before typeface choice; these are the strong levers.
2. Use sentence case for text and reserve all capitals for very short labels, adding letter spacing.
3. Prefer dark on light for long reading; in Night mode compensate (see Buchner2007).
4. Do not pick a typeface on a claim that it reads faster; choose for character and identity within the set of well-behaved faces.
5. Avoid long runs of italic.
6. Keep line length in a mid range and increase leading modestly for longer lines.

## Bouma1970

**Core claims.** Herman Bouma of Philips Research (IPO, Eindhoven) published a short letter in Nature in 1970 titled "Interaction effects in parafoveal letter recognition". It showed that recognition of a letter away from the point of fixation is impaired by neighboring letters, even when the same letter is easily recognized alone at that eccentricity. The loss depends on the spacing between the target and its neighbors in relation to how far the target is from fixation. This phenomenon is now called crowding, and the spacing rule is called Bouma's law. It matters for text because every letter except the fixated one is seen in the periphery, and for interfaces because small adjacent elements cannot be read without fixating them.

**Specifics.** Bouma's method presented a target letter at a set eccentricity from a fixation point, with flanking letters on either side, in brief exposures, and measured the percent correct. He found that interference extended over a distance proportional to eccentricity. The commonly quoted summary, repeated by Pelli and Tillman (2008), is that crowding is negligible when the center-to-center spacing exceeds about half the eccentricity. The constant is approximately 0.5, varying roughly 0.3 to 0.5 in later measurements and with task; I could not confirm Bouma's own reported value this session, so the constant is stated as the later consensus, not as Bouma's figure. Later results add that the crowding zone is elongated along the line from fixation, that the outer flanker interferes more than the inner one, and that flankers similar to the target interfere more. In a related 1973 paper Bouma discussed the initial and final letters of words being recognized more easily because they have a flanker on one side only, which fed the (later contested) idea of word shape.

**Criticism and status.** The law has replicated in hundreds of studies and is the foundation of Pelli's work on reading and object recognition. Its details are disputed: the mechanism (pooling, substitution, feature mislocalization) is debated, and the constant varies with stimulus type. The claim that crowding explains word-shape effects is only partly supported. Bibliographic details beyond author, year, title and journal were not confirmed in this session, so volume, pages and DOI are omitted.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Small adjacent elements (instrument labels, tightly packed buttons) cannot be read from the periphery; if the learner must read it, it must be fixated, so design for it.
2. Spacing is the remedy: increase space around key readouts before increasing size.
3. Do not stack similar glyphs or numerals tightly (for example a column of V-speeds in the same size) without separation.
4. In fly mode, place the critical single number in an isolated area with surrounding empty space.
5. Group items using spacing to create groups with larger gaps between groups than within them.

## Rayner1998

**Core claims.** Keith Rayner's review in Psychological Bulletin 124(3), 372-422, is the standard summary of twenty years of eye-movement research on reading and other visual tasks (music reading, typing, visual search, scene perception). Its central claims are: (1) the eyes take in useful visual information only during fixations, and vision is suppressed during saccades; (2) the duration of fixations and the choice of where to move next are controlled by ongoing language processing, so they are a moment-to-moment window on cognition and not a fixed rhythm; (3) the useful field around fixation during reading, the perceptual span, is small and asymmetric; and (4) fixation times differ systematically by task, with reading faster per fixation than scene viewing or search. The review also treats the integration of information across saccades (what is previewed to the right of fixation), individual differences including dyslexia, and early models of eye-movement control.

**Specifics.** Typical values for skilled adult silent reading of English: fixation durations average about 200 to 250 ms (range from under 100 to over 500 ms); saccade length averages 7 to 9 character spaces, about 2 degrees; about 10 to 15 percent of saccades are regressions to earlier text. Reported averages for other tasks are longer, for example oral reading around 275 ms and scene perception around 330 ms. The perceptual span extends about 3 to 4 characters to the left of the fixated character (roughly to the start of the fixated word) and about 14 to 15 characters to the right; the asymmetry reverses for Hebrew, which reads right to left, which showed that the span is shaped by attention and reading direction and is not a fixed property of the retina. Within the span, only about 7 to 8 letters to the right support identification of letters; further out, readers pick up word length and initial letters. The span is scaled in characters, not degrees, so over a range of print sizes it stays roughly constant in letters. Key methods: the moving-window technique (McConkie and Rayner, 1975), where text is replaced by Xs outside a window that moves with the eye, and the boundary technique (Rayner, 1975), where a word changes when the eye crosses an invisible boundary, which shows what was previewed. Word-level effects: more frequent, more predictable and shorter words are fixated for less time and are skipped more often; content words are fixated far more often (roughly four out of five) than function words (roughly one in three). These skip rates are approximations that I recalled and could not re-check against the text this session.

**Criticism and status.** The empirical patterns are among the most replicated in cognitive psychology. Competing computational accounts (E-Z Reader, SWIFT) disagree on serial versus parallel word processing; the review predates both in their mature form. Evidence is for alphabetic left-to-right languages, mostly on screens in lab conditions with a fixed viewing distance. It does not cover scanning of mixed interface layouts, though the principle that processing drives eye movement carries over.

**Use for LTF.**
1. A fixation yields only a short span of text, so a line wider than the reader can scan comfortably in a few fixations costs return sweeps and errors; keep the line measure moderate.
2. Short, frequent words are skipped and read quickly; use plain, common vocabulary except where a new term is the point of the lesson, and then expect a long fixation and a likely regression.
3. Since the span is wider to the right, text to the right of a key word is previewed; avoid visual clutter or a competing element immediately to the right of important terms.
4. Regressions cluster at difficulty. Where a step needs the learner to go back, supply the referent (a figure or checklist item) nearby.
5. In fly mode a reader has perhaps one or two fixations; plan for single-word or numeral reading, not sentence reading.

## Rayner2016

**Core claims.** Rayner, Schotter, Masson, Potter and Treiman wrote a monograph-length review for the Association for Psychological Science, in Psychological Science in the Public Interest 17(1), 4-34 (2016). Rayner died in 2015, so this was among his last works. It has two parts. First it lays out what is known about how people read: letter and word recognition, eye movements, the perceptual span, the role of language comprehension, and the factors that influence speed. Second it evaluates speed-reading claims and training methods, including skimming, "photoreading", reading with a pacer, and rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP). The conclusion is that claims of reading at several times the normal rate with equal comprehension are not supported and conflict with what is known about the visual and linguistic processes. A trade-off between speed and accuracy exists and cannot be escaped by training.

**Specifics.** The review reports that typical skilled adults read at about 200 to 400 words per minute and that the claim of going from about 250 to 500 or 750 with full comprehension is implausible. Eye movements take no more than approximately 10 percent of reading time, so freeing the eyes from movement cannot double speed; the main limit is the time to process language. Preventing regressions and making readers keep to a pacer harms comprehension. Individual differences in reading speed relate strongly to vocabulary and language skill; modest gains from practice are possible, chiefly through more reading and better language ability. RSVP, in which words appear one at a time at a fixed point, can raise presentation rate but removes the ability to re-read and generally reduces comprehension at high rates. Skimming can be done faster than normal reading but with lower understanding, and the authors suggest that speed-reading courses may be best seen as training in skimming. A related later meta-analysis by Brysbaert (2019) reported averages of about 238 words per minute for non-fiction and about 260 for fiction in adult silent reading; that figure is outside this source and was not re-checked here.

**Criticism and status.** It is a consensus review by senior researchers, widely cited, and its main message has not been contradicted by controlled studies. Commercial speed-reading vendors dispute it but without controlled evidence. The review concerns prose for skilled readers and so should not be applied unchanged to short instructional text, tables or interface labels, where skimming and selective reading are legitimate strategies.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Do not build reading-speed features (RSVP, flashing words, spritz-style readers). They remove the ability to re-read and lower comprehension.
2. Plan reading time with the assumption of roughly 200 to 250 words per minute for new technical material, slower for novices with unfamiliar terms.
3. Support skimming with structure (headings, bold key terms, summaries) for review, but put procedures in a format that does not require reading the full prose.
4. Because language skill drives speed, keep sentences simple for beginners and define terms at first use.
5. Give learners control of pace and allow easy re-reading (scroll, back).

## Legge2011

**Core claims.** Gordon Legge, a vision scientist at the University of Minnesota, and Charles Bigelow, a type designer, wrote "Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography" for Journal of Vision 11(5):8 (2011). The paper addresses a language gap: typographers specify type in points, a physical size, while vision scientists specify angular size (degrees or minutes of arc), which depends on viewing distance. Because the retina responds to angular size, the same point size can be easy or hard to read depending on distance. The review combines the psychophysics of reading speed against print size with the typographic literature. Its main model: reading speed as a function of angular character size rises steeply up to a critical print size (CPS), then plateaus at a maximum reading speed over a wide range, then declines at very large sizes.

**Specifics.** The authors report that the range of print sizes over which reading is fluent (near maximum speed) spans about a factor of 10 in angular x-height, from approximately 0.2 degrees to approximately 2 degrees. Below the critical print size, reading slows as characters shrink toward the acuity limit. Critical print size is a little above reading acuity: it is commonly estimated at about two times the smallest readable size and about 0.2 degrees x-height for normal vision. Typical printed text of about 10 to 12 point read at ordinary distances falls in the lower part of the fluent range, near but above critical print size, which explains why type design and spacing details can matter at that size while at larger sizes they do not. The review stresses x-height over point size because typefaces with equal point sizes have different x-heights, so their angular sizes differ. It discusses measurement methods including MNREAD reading charts and rapid serial visual presentation, and the practice in low vision of recommending print about two to three times critical print size for comfortable reading (an "acuity reserve"); the exact ratio is attributed to other authors and is approximate here. Maximum reading speeds of roughly 200 words per minute appear in RSVP and chart tests, though speed depends on the test. I could not open the full text this session; the figures above that appear in the abstract (the factor of 10, 0.2 to 2 degrees) were confirmed by search, and the rest are described from memory with approximate wording.

**Criticism and status.** The paper is a review and its numbers align with a large literature. Limits: most studies used short sentences at a fixed distance, so real-world conditions with varying distance and fatigue are less well covered. Older readers and low-vision readers have a larger critical print size, so the lower edge of the fluent range is not safe for them.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Specify text by x-height and expected viewing distance: phone about 30 to 40 cm, laptop about 50 to 60 cm, a second monitor beside a sim at 70 cm or more. The same CSS pixel size is a different angle at each.
2. Body text should sit comfortably inside the fluent range, not at its lower edge; round up for fly mode.
3. Choose typefaces with generous x-height for small UI text.
4. Do not chase huge text: above the fluent range reading slows, so scale in moderation.
5. Test sizes on actual devices at actual distances, and offer a text-size control for older learners.

## Bigelow2019

**Core claims.** Charles Bigelow, co-designer of Lucida and Wingdings and a scholar of type history, published "Typeface features and legibility research" in Vision Research 165, 162-172 (2019). It reviews more than a century of experiments on which features of typefaces affect legibility, and relates them to models of letter and word recognition and to crowding. Bibliographic details (title, journal, volume, pages, DOI) were confirmed by search; the full text could not be read in this session because fetch tools were blocked. The statements below therefore describe what the experimental literature, which this review surveys, supports, and I mark claims I cannot trace to this paper.

**Specifics.** The features typically examined are x-height, stroke weight, character width, letter spacing, serif presence, case, italic slant, and letter-shape details such as apertures and terminals. In the literature the review covers, consistent results include: (a) serif versus sans serif shows no reliable difference in reading speed in comfortable conditions (see Arditi and Cho, 2005); (b) at small sizes, larger x-height and looser spacing improve performance because letters are closer to critical size and crowding; (c) very light and very heavy weights both reduce legibility, with intermediate weights best, a result associated with the stroke width and spacing needed for letters to be told apart; (d) uppercase text is read more slowly than lowercase, though uppercase can be easier to identify as isolated letters at small sizes; (e) different tasks (acuity, glance, reading speed of continuous text) give different rankings of typefaces, so "legibility" must be defined by task. A secondary source attributes to Bigelow (2019) the point that the critical x-height size is tied to visual angle; this matches Legge and Bigelow (2011). I could not confirm further specific numbers from the paper.

**Criticism and status.** A review's authority rests on its coverage, which is strong, but it inherits the weaknesses of its sources: many studies had small samples, used old typefaces, and confounded features (for example serifs also change spacing). The popular belief that serifs guide the eye along a line is not supported by controlled studies. Where the review's conclusions were not read directly, they should be cited with the original experiments.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Choose serif or sans on identity and context, not on a legibility claim.
2. For small UI text and for fly mode, select a face with large x-height, open apertures and clear differences among confusable characters.
3. Avoid hairline weights, especially on dark backgrounds.
4. Add letter spacing for small text and for capitals.
5. Define legibility by task: test glance reading for fly mode and continuous reading for lessons separately.

## Arditi2005

**Core claims.** Aries Arditi (a low-vision researcher then at Lighthouse International) and Jianna Cho published "Serifs and font legibility" in Vision Research 45(23), 2926-2933 (2005). The long-standing folk belief says serifs make text easier to read and sans serif is cleaner but harder. Arditi and Cho asked whether serifs change legibility when everything else, especially letter spacing, is held fixed. Their answer: serifs have little or no effect on legibility once spacing is controlled, and any apparent advantage of a serifed face comes largely from the extra spacing that serifs add. The paper is the best-controlled single test of this question and supports the view that the serif versus sans debate is largely a matter of taste and tradition.

**Specifics.** The authors constructed fonts that differed only in the presence and size of serifs. Reported conditions include a sans-serif version and versions with small serifs of 5 percent and 10 percent of letter size (as described in secondary summaries; the exact scale definitions were not confirmed here). Serifs add width to each letter, so a serifed text is more widely spaced on average than its sans-serif twin. To separate the two effects, spacing was varied independently of serif size. Participants identified random five-letter strings at the legibility threshold (the smallest size or shortest exposure at which they could name the letters), which isolates letter identification from word and context effects. In this task, the serifed fonts produced lower thresholds than sans serif, but the benefit tracked the added spacing, and when spacing was equated the effect of serifs disappeared or became very small. The sample was small: four normally sighted participants and two participants with low vision. Spacing mattered strongly for the low-vision readers. A later paper cited in the search (Minakata and colleagues, 2023, on serifs in low vision) extends the question to readers with reduced vision; I did not read it and report only that it exists.

**Criticism and status.** The main limits are the tiny sample and the task of reading random letter strings, which differs from reading meaningful text, where context lets readers tolerate letter-level confusion. Still, its conclusion agrees with Tinker (1963) and with the broader literature reviewed by Bigelow (2019): serifs are a weak variable. Studies that find serif advantages often have uncontrolled spacing, and studies that find sans advantages often involve low-resolution displays, where thin serifs break up. The finding does not mean typefaces never matter: letter shapes, width and x-height do.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Do not cite legibility as a reason for or against a serif; pick for voice and identity.
2. Spacing is the lever: increase letter spacing for small text and for text on dark backgrounds.
3. When using a sans for UI labels and a serif for headings or lesson prose (or the reverse), match x-height and spacing so that neither feels cramped.
4. Check fine serifs on low-resolution or low-contrast displays, where thin features can disappear.
5. Treat low-vision users as the case where spacing and size decisions matter most.

## Beier2010

**Core claims.** Sofie Beier (then a typographer and researcher at the Royal Danish Academy of Fine Arts) and Kevin Larson (a Microsoft researcher in typography and reading) published "Design improvements for frequently misrecognized letters" in Information Design Journal 18(2), 118-137 (2010). Their idea is practical: legibility research should not only compare existing typefaces, it should find which letters get confused most and ask whether redesign can reduce those confusions. They identified frequently misrecognized letters and designed variations, then measured whether the variations improved recognition. The work grew into Beier's book Reading Letters: Designing for Legibility (BIS Publishers, 2012), which argues for design based on evidence about letter features.

**Specifics.** The abstract and secondary summaries say that three fonts were created, each containing several variations of the most frequently misrecognized letters, and that the variations were tested with two methods: viewing at a distance and short exposure. Reported outcomes (taken from summaries, not from the paper itself, so treat as approximate): adding a serif to the lowercase i and j improved their legibility in the distance test; broadening narrow letters such as t and l improved recognition; a larger tail and a broader descender helped the j. The pattern is that features which make a letter more distinct from its look-alikes, and wider, more open forms, are helpful at the edge of legibility. The study is single-letter recognition at threshold, not continuous reading, which means gains can be large for the letters tested and smaller for text. I could not retrieve the full text this session, so I give no sample sizes or numeric effects. The DOI I had in memory was removed because it was unconfirmed.

**Criticism and status.** Tests of this kind (distance and brief exposure) are standard in legibility research and the principle is widely accepted in the design of signage, automotive and screen fonts. Findings about specific letters depend on the starting typeface: a redesign that helps one design might not help another. Effects on text reading speed in comfortable conditions are not established by this study.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Choose a typeface where 1, l and I, and 0 and O, are clearly distinct, because aviation text is full of numbers and codes (altitudes, frequencies, squawk codes).
2. Test the actual strings the app displays: for example "I", "l", "1", "O", "0", and combinations such as "S/5".
3. Where space is tight, prefer wider, more open letterforms to condensed fashionable ones.
4. Pay the most attention to letter distinctiveness in glance contexts such as fly mode.
5. If a custom or display face is used for identity, keep it away from small and numeric text unless it passes these checks.

## Pelli2008

**Core claims.** Denis Pelli (New York University) and Katharine Tillman published "The uncrowded window of object recognition" in Nature Neuroscience 11(10), 1129-1135 (2008), with an erratum correcting the legend of Figure 5. They argue that the main limit on recognizing objects, letters and words in the periphery is not size or acuity but spacing: objects interfere with each other when too close (crowding), and recognition is possible only inside a window around fixation where spacing exceeds a critical value. They call this the uncrowded window. They say the size of this window limits the speed of reading and visual search, and that the window grows during childhood, which accounts for the rise in reading speed with age.

**Specifics.** The review states that critical spacing is roughly half the eccentricity (Bouma's rule) and is largely independent of the size of the target: a bigger letter does not escape crowding if its neighbors are equally close in proportion. It proposes a two-stage account in which features are first detected and then combined, and crowding results from the second stage, so that detection can succeed while identification fails. It reviews evidence that crowding occurs for letters, faces, and objects, and that it is elongated radially. The authors tie reading rate to the number of letters that fit in the uncrowded region (the visual span); this refers to work such as Pelli and colleagues (2007, "Crowding and eccentricity determine reading rate", Journal of Vision), which is related but not part of the confirmed bibliography here. A key implication: past critical print size, enlarging type does not raise reading speed, because spacing, not size, is limiting; and for readers with a smaller uncrowded window (children, people with certain conditions) wider spacing helps. The press coverage of the paper emphasized "spacing, not size".

**Criticism and status.** The paper is a review and a theory piece. Bouma's law is well replicated; the two-stage model and the claim about universality of critical spacing are debated, with other work finding that critical spacing varies with stimulus type, task and visual field location. The link between crowding and reading speed is supported, but other factors (language, eye-movement control) also matter.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Space before size: if text is hard to read in a dense panel, increase spacing and line spacing before making it bigger.
2. Dense instrument-like clusters will be unreadable without fixating each element, so design so that important values are read one at a time.
3. Use grouping through generous gaps between groups and tight gaps within.
4. Expect beginners and older learners to have a smaller effective window; give them more spacing.
5. Letter spacing in small text and wider tracking for small caps are justified by crowding.

## Buchner2007

**Core claims.** Axel Buchner and Nina Baumgartner (Heinrich Heine University Dusseldorf) published "Text-background polarity affects performance irrespective of ambient illumination and colour contrast" in Ergonomics 50, 1036-1063 (2007). Across a series of experiments they found that dark text on a light background (positive polarity) gave better performance than light text on a dark background (negative polarity), whatever the ambient lighting and whichever colors were used. They also found that color contrast cannot make up for a lack of luminance contrast. The paper was designed to test claims that the polarity advantage might disappear in dim rooms, where bright displays glare, or with colored text.

**Specifics.** The task was proofreading: readers searched a text for errors, which makes sustained reading demanding and error sensitive. Ambient illumination ranged from darkness to typical office lighting. Color pairs included black and white and blue and yellow, with polarities varied. The positive-polarity advantage appeared in every illumination condition tested. In the last experiment, red text on a green background (very different hue, similar luminance) performed badly, showing that hue difference alone does not give legibility. The sample sizes and effect sizes were not confirmed in this session, so they are not reported. Follow-up work: Buchner, Mayr and Brandt (2009) argued that the advantage is due to the higher display luminance of positive polarity (the pupil constricts and the optical image is sharper); Piepenbrock, Mayr and Buchner reported that the advantage holds for older adults as well as younger (2013 to 2014); and Dobres and colleagues (2017, Applied Ergonomics) found a polarity effect in glance reading that depended on ambient light and size, which they attributed to pupil size. I did not re-verify the later papers' details this session.

**Criticism and status.** The positive-polarity advantage is well replicated, although effect sizes are modest and shrink as text gets larger, heavier, and brighter. It does not mean dark themes are unreadable: many readers prefer them in low light, and glare and eye strain from bright screens at night are real concerns. The evidence says dark mode costs something at small sizes and light weights, so compensation is warranted. Studies used proofreading and short tasks, not long learning sessions.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Make Day (light) the default for long reading of lessons.
2. In the Night theme, increase body text weight or size slightly, and avoid thin weights and very small captions.
3. Avoid pure white on pure black; use a slightly dimmer text color on a dark gray, as long as contrast stays high.
4. Never rely on hue difference alone for contrast; check luminance contrast.
5. Test small text and numerals in Night mode at glance exposure, since this is where the polarity penalty is largest.

## Dobres2016

**Core claims.** Dobres, Chahine, Reimer, Gould, Mehler and Coughlin (MIT AgeLab with Monotype; Ergonomics, 2016) used psychophysical methods to measure glance legibility: how quickly can a person read text when they have only a short look, as a driver does at an in-vehicle display. The title names four variables: age, typeface design, size and display polarity. The motivating problem is that drivers cannot spend long looking away from the road, so the usual measure of legibility (reading speed or comfort) is less relevant than the exposure time needed to identify text. The approach adapts threshold methods from vision science: the display time is adjusted until the participant is right at a criterion level, and a shorter threshold means better legibility.

**Specifics.** From the abstract and summaries: the study compared a humanist sans serif and a square grotesque typeface (the specific font names were not confirmed in this session). Text was less legible at 3 mm character height than at 4 mm, and the penalty was especially large for the square grotesque. Legibility thresholds increased roughly linearly with age and more than doubled across the age range studied, so older participants needed much longer exposures. Typeface also interacted with age: differences between typefaces were larger in conditions with higher thresholds (small size, older participants). A polarity manipulation was part of the design (positive versus negative polarity); the direction and size of its effect are not confirmed here beyond the later Dobres et al. (2017) summary in the Buchner2007 section, so I do not state numeric results. The sample size, the number of participants per age group and the specific age bands were not confirmed, and are omitted. Related work from the same group: Reimer and colleagues (2014) examined typeface in an automotive text interface, finding differences between designs in glance behavior, and Dobres and colleagues (2017) studied illumination, polarity and letter size.

**Criticism and status.** The study is a single-lab experiment in an automotive context, using two typefaces, so it cannot rank typefaces in general. The qualitative result, that typeface choice matters most when exposure is short, size is small and viewers are older, fits the crowding and critical print size literature (Legge and Bigelow, 2011; Pelli and Tillman, 2008). Volume, issue and page numbers were not confirmed and were removed from the bibliography; the DOI was confirmed by search.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Treat fly mode as a glance task and test it with short exposures, not by asking whether the text is comfortable.
2. Make glance text larger than you think: the effect of going from a small to a slightly larger size is big.
3. Prefer open, humanist letterforms over wide, squared forms for small glance text.
4. Plan for older learners: allow larger text and spacing.
5. Include an age range when testing and do not rely on the designer's own eyes.

## Dobres2018

**Core claims.** Jonathan Dobres, Benjamin Wolfe, Nadine Chahine and Bryan Reimer published "The effects of visual crowding, text size, and positional uncertainty on text legibility at a glance" in Applied Ergonomics 70, 240-246 (2018). The study links glance legibility to crowding: when text is small and lines are close, neighboring lines interfere with recognition. It asks whether larger line spacing (leading) can rescue small text, and whether uncertainty about where text will appear affects legibility.

**Specifics.** From summaries of the abstract: participants viewed arrays of words and performed a lexical decision task (deciding whether a string is a real word) with brief exposure. Text size and the vertical spacing between lines were varied. Wider line spacing significantly improved legibility, as predicted from crowding theory, but contrary to expectations it did not fully offset the loss from smaller text size. Positional uncertainty was also manipulated (text appearing at a predictable versus unpredictable location); I did not retrieve the result, so I do not state it. The full text could not be read this session, so sample size, the exact sizes and spacings, and effect magnitudes are not reported. The design builds on Bouma's law: if crowding were the only limit, widening spacing should restore legibility; the partial benefit suggests that size itself still matters below some threshold, consistent with critical print size.

**Criticism and status.** The task, lexical decision on word arrays in a brief display, is far from continuous reading, and the study comes from a single research group. Replication by other labs is not documented here. The result is nonetheless coherent with the crowding literature (Pelli and Tillman, 2008) and with Legge and Bigelow (2011): both spacing and size matter, and neither can fully replace the other near the limit of legibility.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Give small text more leading, particularly in dense lists and instrument-like layouts.
2. Do not use extra leading as an excuse to go smaller; keep a size floor.
3. In fly mode, place glance text where the learner expects it, so positional uncertainty does not add to the cost.
4. Do not stack many short lines of small text in a column that has to be read at a glance; show fewer items larger.
5. Combine line spacing with word spacing so that neighboring words do not crowd each other.
6. When a design review asks whether small text is "fine", ask for the exposure time and the line spacing used in the test, since this study shows that the two trade off only partly.

## Dyson2001

**Core claims.** Mary Dyson of the University of Reading has made line length and layout on screen her main field. With Mark Haselgrove she published "The influence of reading speed and line length on the effectiveness of reading from screen" in the International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 54(4), 585-612 (2001). The study separated two questions often run together: how fast people read different line lengths, and how well they understand and remember what they read, since a layout can be fast and shallow. It extends Dyson and Kipping (1998, "The effects of line length and method of movement on patterns of reading from screen", Visible Language 32, 150-181), which compared line lengths of about 1.8 inches and 5.5 inches and found the longer line read reliably faster, and Dyson and Kipping (1997, Computers and Graphics 21(6), 703-712), on single versus multiple columns.

**Specifics.** Secondary summaries report that lines of about 55 characters supported both effective speed and comprehension, and that the paper distinguishes faster reading from more accurate recall, noting that readers who read faster at their normal speed recalled more than slower readers. The comprehension measures used several question types (title, main idea, main factual, structure, incidental and recognition), as cited by later authors. Dyson's 2004 review ("How physical text layout affects reading from screen", Behaviour and Information Technology) is reported in a summary as concluding that very long lines of about 100 characters give the fastest reading while readers prefer short or medium lengths of about 45 to 72 characters. The specific line lengths tested in the 2001 paper (I recalled 25, 55 and 100 characters, but a search did not confirm 25) are not stated as fact. Duchnicky and Kolers (1983, Human Factors) reported that a single wide column was read faster than three narrow columns even though users preferred the three-column layout; that is a different study and is mentioned only to show the pattern of speed versus preference.

**Criticism and status.** The experiments used 1990s monitors, small windows and scrolling or paging, so absolute numbers should not be copied to modern devices. Speed differences between line lengths are modest, and preference often points the other way, so the advice to use mid-length lines is a balance. Effects on comprehension are mixed across studies. The classic print recommendation of 45 to 75 characters is close to the middle of the preferred range here.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Set lesson prose to a measure of roughly 50 to 75 characters (about 60ch to 75ch in CSS), where speed, comprehension and preference agree.
2. Avoid very short lines (under about 40 characters) in main text; they interrupt reading with frequent return sweeps.
3. Do not stretch prose to full width on large monitors; cap the width.
4. Allow wider measures for code, tables and diagrams, which are not read as prose.
5. When measuring a new layout, measure both speed and recall, not preference alone.

## Wallace2022

**Core claims.** Shaun Wallace and ten co-authors from Adobe, MIT, Brown and the University of Central Florida published "Towards individuated reading experiences: Different fonts increase reading speed for different individuals" in ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction 29(4), article 38 (2022). The claim: no single font is fastest for everyone. Individuals differ in which font lets them read fastest, and personalizing the font can raise reading speed without lowering comprehension. The authors also report that people's stated preferences for fonts do not reliably predict which font is fastest for them.

**Specifics.** The study involved 352 participants aged 18 to 71, recruited online, who read in sixteen fonts chosen from those common on the web, in newsprint and in PDFs. Reading speed was measured in words per minute and comprehension was checked. Comparing each person's fastest and slowest fonts, reading speed increased by about 35 percent without a drop in comprehension, a finding the authors present as showing high variability across fonts and individuals. Preference was not a reliable guide to speed. The project released materials and a short online test, the Virtual Readability Lab, so that readers can find their own best font. Further detail on the passage materials, how fonts were assigned, and statistical treatment was not retrieved this session and is not stated. A press summary says the paper's reading speed gains held for a diverse group; I treat that as the authors' claim, not independent evidence.

**Criticism and status.** The experiment was online with uncontrolled devices, screens and viewing distances, which adds noise and means differences between fonts may partly reflect rendering on the participants' systems. The finding that a person's fastest font differs from their stated favorite is interesting, but whether an individual's best font is stable over time and across devices needs retesting. The paper has not been widely replicated and should be seen as suggestive. Its authors also include researchers affiliated with a type company, which readers should note when weighing the claims; this is not evidence of bias, only context.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Pick a robust, widely legible default and avoid claims that it is "the most readable font".
2. If a reading-preference setting is offered (font, size, spacing), present it as a comfort option, not as a learning accelerator.
3. Do not trust learners' stated preference as evidence of faster reading; if a feature depends on it, test it.
4. Do not tailor fonts in the fly-mode glance display on the basis of personalization claims; keep it stable and tested.
5. Document the evidence level in the design record (suggestive, one study).

## Lane synthesis

The sources agree on a hierarchy of effects. Size (in angular terms), contrast, spacing and line length are the strong levers of legibility; typeface category, such as serif versus sans, is weak. Tinker (1963) found small differences among common faces, Arditi and Cho (2005) found no serif effect once spacing was equated, and the review by Bigelow (2019) points the same way. Eye-movement work, from Javal's saccades through Huey (1908) to Rayner (1998, 2016), shows that reading is a sequence of short fixations driven by language processing, that the useful span is small and asymmetric, and that the bottleneck is comprehension, not eye movement. Crowding research (Bouma, 1970; Pelli and Tillman, 2008) explains why spacing helps and why size beyond critical print size does not (Legge and Bigelow, 2011). Glance work (Dobres 2016, 2018) shows that typeface and spacing matter most when exposure is short, text small and readers older.

The sources conflict or leave open: line length (longer lines read faster, readers prefer medium; Dyson and Haselgrove, 2001); polarity (positive polarity wins in lab tasks, Buchner and Baumgartner, 2007, yet dark themes are widely preferred); and personalization (Wallace et al., 2022, versus the finding that typefaces barely differ on average). Huey's word-shape account has been displaced by letter-based models. Several details could not be re-verified in this session because search and fetch tools were exhausted or blocked: Javal's and Bouma's bibliographic details, Bigelow's conclusions, Dobres 2016 typeface names and sample, and the exact numbers in secondary summaries of Tinker.

The ten most actionable takeaways:
1. Specify type by x-height and viewing distance, and stay well inside the fluent range of 0.2 to 2 degrees.
2. Keep lesson prose at about 50 to 75 characters per line.
3. Use sentence case for text, all caps only for short labels with extra tracking.
4. Add leading to small text but do not use it to justify smaller sizes.
5. Fix crowding with spacing before size.
6. Choose a face with distinct 1, l, I, 0 and O, open apertures and a generous x-height.
7. Do not claim a legibility advantage for serif or sans; decide on identity.
8. In the Night theme, compensate for the polarity penalty with weight, size or spacing, and avoid pure white on pure black.
9. Treat fly mode as a glance task: few large words, high contrast, tested with short exposures and with older readers.
10. Do not build speed-reading or word-flashing features; support understanding and skimming with structure.
