# Lane 13: Information architecture, navigation and wayfinding

Research notes for the Learn to Fly Design Knowledge Base. One section per source, running from built-space wayfinding, through information foraging and menu structure, to practitioner IA craft.

## lynch1960image

Lynch, K. (1960). *The Image of the City*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press. Book (urban design, empirical study plus design argument).

### Core claims

Lynch argued that what matters most for finding one's way in a city is legibility: how easily its parts can be recognized and organized into a coherent pattern in the mind of the person moving through it. He proposed a related design criterion, imageability, the quality of a physical object that gives it a high probability of evoking a strong image in an observer. People build their mental map from a small set of recognizable physical elements, so designers can deliberately make an environment easier to hold in the mind.

### Specifics

The book reports a roughly five-year study of three US cities chosen for contrast: Boston (diverse, many distinct elements), Jersey City (low distinctiveness) and Los Angeles (a newer type of city). Secondary accounts describe the method as office interviews in which residents described the city, drew a quick sketch map as for a stranger and described an imaginary trip through it, followed by systematic field examination by trained observers. Exact sample sizes were not confirmed this session, so none are given.

From the mental maps Lynch isolated five element types:

- Paths: channels along which people move (streets, walkways, transit lines). They organize the other elements.
- Edges: linear boundaries that are not used as paths (shores, walls, rail cuts).
- Districts: medium to large areas with a common character that a person mentally enters.
- Nodes: strategic points such as junctions or concentrations that a person can enter and that act as foci. The most successful nodes were both unique and intensifying of their surroundings.
- Landmarks: external reference points a person does not enter, from buildings and signs to mountains; at least one aspect is unique or memorable in context.

One feature can play different roles for different people: a motorway is a path to the driver and an edge to the pedestrian. The five types can also be grouped geometrically as points (nodes, landmarks), lines (paths, edges) and polygons (districts). The book also discusses qualities of form that support legibility; that list was not confirmed here and is not reproduced.

### Replication and criticism

The evidence is interview and sketch-map data from small samples, not controlled experiments. A summary of Lynch's own text notes he acknowledged the small size, class bias and partial locational bias of his samples, and critics say he neglected variation among observers. A university design resource states that the specifics of his spatial image were contested by later researchers (it cites Downs and Stea, 1973) while most of his elements are still accepted as design considerations. Later work adds that an element can be memorable for personal, historical or cultural meaning, not only visual distinctiveness. Arthur and Passini (1992) argued that wayfinding is more dynamic than a static image implies (see that section). The book strongly influenced environmental psychology, and "cognitive mapping" travelled far beyond urban design.

### Implications for LTF

LTF is a small information space, but a learner still builds a mental map of it, and the five elements become checkable design questions.

- Paths: the lesson sequence is the main path. Keep the order stable and the "next step" unmistakable, so a learner can say where they have been and where they are going.
- Districts: modules should each have a recognizable character (consistent header treatment and accent) so a learner arriving from a bookmark knows which district they are in.
- Edges: fly mode, quizzes and challenges are different kinds of place. Give them a distinct frame and an explicit exit so nobody drifts into a timed or in-sim context by accident.
- Nodes: the dashboard and module overviews are the junctions where choices happen. Show progress and one clear next action, not a pile of equal links.
- Landmarks: keep a few persistent, unique anchors (site mark, current-lesson title, progress indicator) in the same place on every page. Distinctiveness only works when it is rare.

Because the evidence is qualitative and from city streets, use this as a review vocabulary, not a source of effect sizes.

## arthur1992wayfinding

Arthur, P., & Passini, R. (1992). *Wayfinding: People, Signs, and Architecture*. New York: McGraw-Hill. Book (design handbook built on environmental psychology; craft and research synthesis).

### Core claims

Arthur (a Toronto graphic designer) and Passini (a Montreal architect and environmental psychologist) treat wayfinding as a purposeful problem-solving activity, not a matter of putting up directional signs. Their central move is to stress that people do not simply consult a static mental map. They make decisions as they move, in response to the information and cues they meet, so a wayfinding system has to deliver the right information at the place where a decision is made. Architecture and graphics are treated as two parts of one information system and are meant to be planned together.

### Specifics

The publisher description says the book spells out the principles of wayfinding and applies them to architectural design, starting from spatial, orientation and perception factors, and showing how routes and destinations must integrate graphic, verbal, auditory and tactile stimuli. It adds checklists to prevent wayfinding problems at the design stage and to troubleshoot existing buildings. A catalog abstract describes six parts: the issue, principles of wayfinding, principles of wayfinding design, and then architectural, graphic, and audible and tactile components. The authors' own definition, as quoted by a retailer, is strategies people use to find their way in familiar or new settings, based on perceptual and cognitive abilities and habits.

Secondary accounts report the following specifics, with page references to the book:

- Wayfinding is described as a spatial problem-solving process of decision making, decision execution and information processing. The book is also summarized as treating decision making (the action plan) and decision execution (carrying it out) as the two linked stages.
- A wayfinding decision is behavior (turn right, go up, look for information) in response to an environmental stimulus such as an intersection, a stairway or a billboard (p. 31).
- Environmental perception is "directed and purposeful perception" (p. 33).
- Information received "at the wrong place is as good as no information at all" (p. 34). Relevant information buried in a complex body of information may be screened out and not remembered.
- Chapter 8 deals with differences between people (abilities, age, culture, stature).

A university design resource credits the book as the first to distinguish architectural and information components of wayfinding, compile the evidence and turn it into design guidance. The resource's guideline derived from this tradition is to design for the first-time visitor, because repeat visitors can rely on experience.

### Replication and criticism

This is a craft and synthesis book, not a single experiment, and it predates most digital navigation research. It is treated as a standard reference (a 2002 limited commemorative edition was issued). Later reviews qualify it. Space syntax studies found that after a short exposure to a building, people tend to head for spaces from which the rest of the building is most accessible, which suggests they absorb the layout's configuration and not only landmarks, signs and other cues. Research gaps are also still listed: how people use several simultaneous wayfinding cues, how they give and take directions, and how interior and exterior wayfinding differ. Golledge is cited in the same review for the finding that most people are unaware of their wayfinding strategies and find them hard to report, which limits interview-based evidence.

### Implications for LTF

- Treat each screen as a decision point. The learner decides to continue, repeat, open the sim, or leave. Put the information for that decision next to it ("Next: Lesson 4", progress, an estimated length), not in a separate help page. The book's principle is that information at the wrong place is as good as none.
- Design for the first-time visitor. LTF's audience is complete beginners, so labels and sequence cues must work without prior knowledge of aviation vocabulary.
- Plan content and interface together. The architectural component is the structure of the course (clear sequence, chunking, repeated layouts) and the graphic component is labels, headings and progress marks. Reviews should check both.
- Fly mode is a place where attention is divided with the sim. Keep decisions there to a minimum and make the one that matters (what to do next in the checklist) unmistakable.
- Audit with the book's checklist spirit: for each page, ask where the learner is, where they can go, and how they know they are finished.

Confirmed this session: Open Library work OL3807999W (publisher description, 1992 McGraw-Hill, 238 pages in editions); web search results for the Semantic Scholar abstract (six parts), Amazon listing (authors' definition) and the Buffalo IDeA Center resource DR-14 and the MLTPA wayfinding design PDF (mltpa.org/images/downloads/MT_SWF_WayfindingDesign.pdf, read in full text) for page-referenced claims and the space syntax and design-guideline material. The book itself was not read.

## golledge1999wayfinding

Golledge, R. G. (Ed.). (1999). *Wayfinding Behavior: Cognitive Mapping and Other Spatial Processes*. Baltimore: Johns Hopkins University Press. Edited volume (survey of research in psychology, geography and planning).

### Core claims

The volume brings together researchers from psychology, geography, urban planning, neuroscience and animal behavior to survey what is known about how people (and other species) find their way. Its organizing idea is the cognitive map: knowledge of places and the relations among them, stored in memory and used to plan and carry out travel. The publisher's description frames the opening question as how humans process and use spatial information, why people make wayfinding errors, and what makes one person a better navigator than another, and it sets human research alongside the debate over whether animals have cognitive maps or other internal spatial representations.

### Specifics

The publisher lists 448 pages and 83 black-and-white illustrations. Chapter titles seen this session include Human Wayfinding and Cognitive Maps (Golledge, pp. 5 to 45 as cited elsewhere), Spatial Abilities, Cognitive Maps and Wayfinding (individual differences), Human Information Processing in Sequential Spatial Choice, Environmental Cognition and Decision Making in Urban Navigation, a neurocognitive approach to human navigation, Dynamic Spatial Orientation, Spatial Processing in Animals and Humans, Neural Mechanisms of Spatial Orientation and Wayfinding, Dissociation between Distance and Direction during Locomotor Navigation, and Error Tolerance and Generalization in Cognitive Maps. The full table of contents was not confirmed, so this list is partial.

To get concrete definitions I read a later chapter by Golledge and Gärling, "Cognitive Maps and Urban Travel," which restates the framework:

- A cognitive map is a metaphor or hypothetical construct for place-based knowledge in long-term memory that lets a person establish locations, distances and directions and link places in sequence.
- Cognitive mapping is the process of encoding, storing and manipulating spatially referenced information.
- People define common anchors (significant places recognized by most people) and personalized anchors tied to their own activities. Minor information attaches to anchors as "primers and fillers."
- Two kinds of knowledge are distinguished: egocentric referencing (person-to-object relations that change as you move) and the anchoring structure (object-to-object relations that stay put). Poor person-to-object comprehension can leave a traveler locally disoriented while still grasping the larger layout.
- Human spatial representations are incomplete and error prone. The chapter reports that pointing errors even between familiar places average about 25 to 30 degrees, shorter distances tend to be overestimated and longer ones underestimated. A correct map is not needed to follow a route: route knowledge can be self-contained, anchored by choice points and landmarks.
- A distinction is drawn between navigation (a predetermined, calculated route, optimized for time or cost) and wayfinding (finding a path, often unfamiliar, involving search, exploration, satisficing and emotion).

### Replication and criticism

This is a literature survey with contributions of mixed methods (experiments, field studies, modeling, neuroscience), so there is no single replication story. The cognitive map metaphor itself has been debated since its introduction in the late 1940s, and the volume deliberately includes the biologists' dispute over whether animals have maps. The chapter excerpt flags the metaphor status explicitly ("hypothetical construct") and little research on how network knowledge forms. Individual differences are large, which cautions against a single "typical user."

### Implications for LTF

- Learners rarely hold an accurate map of a site. Design so that route knowledge (a clear next step, a consistent lesson template) is enough, and the full "survey" view (the course map) is available but optional.
- Provide stable anchors: a course map, module names and a persistent "you are here" mark that most learners will come to recognize and use as common anchors.
- Distinguish navigation and wayfinding contexts. In fly mode the learner follows a predetermined route (checklist, procedure), so show strictly ordered steps with the current step marked. On the dashboard the learner is wayfinding, so support exploration and satisficing with a recommended path.
- Expect individual differences in spatial ability and do not rely on spatial metaphors alone for orientation; pair them with text labels.

Confirmed this session: Open Library work OL3349342W (publisher description, Johns Hopkins University Press, editions 1998 to 1999); web search of press.jhu.edu and library listings for chapter titles, page count, illustrations and chapter 1 pagination; read in full the escholarship PDF of Golledge and Gärling, "Cognitive Maps and Urban Travel" (escholarship.org/content/qt1bp9f7wc), and the Buffalo IDeA Center resource DR-14 for the remark that most people are unaware of their wayfinding strategies. The volume itself was not read.

## pirolli1999information

Pirolli, P., & Card, S. (1999). Information foraging. *Psychological Review, 106*(4), 643-675. Journal article (theory with rational analysis and a computational process model).

### Core claims

Pirolli and Card argue that people seeking information behave like foragers adapting to the structure of their environment, and that this can be analyzed with the rational-analysis tools of optimal foraging theory from biology and anthropology. The abstract says the theory is built in two ways: an adaptation (rational) analysis of information foraging problems, and a detailed process model called ACT-IF (adaptive control of thought in information foraging), implemented as a production system model of people interacting with complex information technology. The practical upshot is that information seeking can be understood as a cost-benefit activity: users try to maximize the value of information gained per unit of effort, and they use cues in the interface to estimate where value lies.

### Specifics

I could not read the 1999 article itself this session, so the details below come from its abstract record, a textbook-style summary of it, and the authors' CHI '95 precursor paper (which I read in a detailed summary). Items from the precursor are labeled as such.

- Information scent: the value of an information source is judged from navigation cues and metadata, which the theory calls information scent. Later NN/g material describes scent as the user's estimate, made before visiting, of how useful a source will be, shaped by what the user sees (labels, summaries, images) and what they know.
- Information patches: information is clustered in spatial and temporal groupings, and users decide whether to stay in a patch or move on.
- Information diet: users choose which sources to pursue over others, and the value of information depends on the task it serves.
- Precursor (CHI '95): optimality models have decision, currency and constraint assumptions; the currency is information value, not energy; people satisfice. The patch model uses Charnov's marginal value theorem: leave a patch when the instantaneous gain rate falls to the average rate across the environment. The diet model ranks items by profitability (expected gain per handling time). Both were illustrated with Scatter/Gather, a document-clustering browser, using time-coded video of four PARC researchers working one hour each. The authors suggested that ordering cluster titles by probability of relevance would raise the gain rate, a design implication drawn from the theory.
- Prediction check: Fu and Pirolli (2007) say the 1999 analysis of ACT-IF showed that its scent-based rankings of Scatter/Gather clusters correlated well with the clusters users selected.

### Replication and criticism

The strongest follow-up evidence is the SNIF-ACT line of models, which carry scent into Web navigation and fit user link choices (see the fu2007snifact section). In the Web setting scent is operationalized with word-association statistics, which is a measurable proxy for the original construct. The theory is a framework and a model, not a single experiment, and the first empirical illustrations were small (a handful of expert users of one browser). A glossary summary notes that ACT-IF's applicability is not fully understood, that building such models is complex, and that information foraging is mostly used as a design perspective, not a fully validated predictive tool. The authors themselves assume satisficing, not perfect optimizing.

### Implications for LTF

- Treat every link, button and card as a scent cue. A learner should be able to predict from the label alone what the next page contains and whether it moves them toward their goal. Use plain language that matches their words, not internal course jargon.
- Make patches (lessons) easy to leave and re-enter. The theory predicts that learners leave when the rate of gain falls, so show progress and the remaining effort, and give explicit completion points.
- Put the highest-value items first within a patch (the key step, the common error) because ordering by relevance improves gain rates.
- Avoid "clickbait" labels that overpromise: they inflate scent and erode trust once the destination fails to deliver.
- In fly mode, the cost of a lookup is high because attention is on the sim. Keep scent strong and cost low: one short, high-confidence cue per decision.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record for DOI 10.1037/0033-295x.106.4.643 (title, authors, journal, volume 106, issue 4, pages 643-675, 1999); web search returning the abstract's two-part method; IxDF glossary page on information foraging theory (patches, scent, diet, ACT-IF, limits); NN/g article by Raluca Budiu (2020) on information foraging and scent; the CHI '95 proceedings page (chi1995 archive) for the patch, diet and Scatter/Gather detail; Fu and Pirolli (2007) full text for the 1999 correlation claim.

## fu2007snifact

Fu, W.-T., & Pirolli, P. (2007). SNIF-ACT: A cognitive model of user navigation on the World Wide Web. *Human-Computer Interaction, 22*(4), 355-412. Journal article (computational cognitive model with empirical validation).

### Core claims

Fu and Pirolli present SNIF-ACT (Scent-based Navigation and Information Foraging in the ACT architecture), a computational model of how people use the text of links to decide where to go on the Web and when to give up. The model rests on information foraging theory, which quantifies the perceived relevance of a link to a user's goal through a spreading activation mechanism. They claim that combining information scent with a Bayesian satisficing model explains both which links people choose and when they leave a page or a site.

### Specifics

- SNIF-ACT 1.0 was tested against detailed protocol data from 8 participants performing two information-seeking tasks. The model predicts link selections and site-leaving actions; these two action types were 72 percent of the 189 actions extracted from the logs (48 percent link-following, 24 percent site-leaving).
- The scent of a link for a goal is computed by summing activations of goal words given the words in the link, where association strength approximates pointwise mutual information (PMI) from large text corpora. In Figure 6 the link with the highest model scent was chosen 19 times and the next highest 15 times, while a Position model (choose by order on the page) fit worse.
- SNIF-ACT 2.0 adds a Bayesian satisficing mechanism: links on a page are evaluated sequentially, and the model clicks, or goes back, once the accumulated assessment is good enough. It was validated on data from 74 subjects, and Monte Carlo simulations fit human data better than version 1.0 and the Position model.
- Site leaving: mean page scent was high at first and dropped before participants left a site, consistent with moving to a new information patch when the expected gain from the current patch falls below the expected gain of finding another.
- The model also fits the Law of Surfing, an empirical regularity in the distribution of navigation path lengths before users give up.

### Replication and criticism

This is a modeling paper validated on two datasets, so it is stronger evidence than a framework alone but still depends on the tasks and sites used (Web sites circa the mid-2000s and mostly text links). The authors acknowledge limits, including that links are evaluated sequentially in the model while real users also process pages hierarchically, and that users with different background knowledge are not modeled. They compare SNIF-ACT with two other models of Web navigation, MESA and CoLiDeS, and discuss a usability analysis system, Bloodhound. A correlation between scent and position of links (r = .64 reported in the paper) is one reason the authors tested a combined model.

### Implications for LTF

- Link text is the main lever. Test labels by asking whether a first-time learner with a stated goal ("learn to flare the landing") would pick the right link; a card or link set where several labels share words with the goal will split attention.
- Satisficing is normal: learners take the first good-enough link. Put the recommended next step first and visually distinct, and do not rely on learners comparing all options.
- Watch for drops in scent along a path, the signal that learners abandon a patch. After a lesson, a weak "what next" page is where learners leave.
- Use analytics on link choices as a check; position bias is real and scent is separate from it.

Confirmed this session: Crossref search returned DOI 10.1080/07370020701638806 for this article (Human-Computer Interaction, vol. 22, issue 4, pages 355-412, 2007); read the full text of the publicly hosted PDF (research.google.com pubs archive 25938) including the abstract, Section 2 on scent equations, Section 4 results and the discussion.

## miller1981depth

Miller, D. P. (1981). The depth/breadth tradeoff in hierarchical computer menus. *Proceedings of the Human Factors Society Annual Meeting, 25*(1), 296-300. Conference paper (laboratory experiment, between-subjects).

### Core claims

Miller asked how many choices per menu and how many menu levels give the fastest and most accurate selection when the total number of end items is fixed. The abstract reports that goal acquisition time for four menu configurations of a semantic hierarchy formed a U-shaped function, with the minimum at the configuration of two levels with eight choices per level, and that error data corroborated the timing, so the fastest conditions also produced the fewest errors. He concluded that optimizing the depth/breadth tradeoff can matter in goal acquisition tasks requiring speed and accuracy.

### Specifics

- Task: participants located target items in a semantic hierarchy on an interactive computer terminal. Goal acquisition speed and accuracy were measured.
- Design: depth (menu levels) varied from one to six and breadth (choices per menu) from two to 64. Secondary accounts say four structures held the number of end items at 64: one level of 64 items, two levels of 8, three levels of 4, and six levels of 2.
- Result: the two-level, eight-per-level structure was fastest and least error prone; both the very deep (six levels of 2) and the fully flat (64 on one screen) structures were worse, hence the U shape. Larson and Czerwinski (1998) add that Miller suggested depth should be minimized, but not at the expense of display crowding, and that the breadth that tested well sits within the range of G. A. Miller's "seven plus or minus two."
- The study was one of the earliest on the topic, run on an interactive terminal with a 64-item semantic space.

### Replication and criticism

Several later studies replicated the main pattern. Snowberry, Parkinson and Sisson (1983) re-ran the same four structures, adding memory span and visual scanning pre-tests, and found that memory span did not predict performance but visual scanning did, especially in the deepest hierarchies. They also showed that when the 64 items were grouped into coherent categories the broad flat structure did better than when items were randomly arranged, so category quality matters as much as shape. Kiger (1984) and Zaphiris (2000) found depth costs time (see their sections), and Larson and Czerwinski (1998) found two-level structures beat a three-level one on the Web.

Criticisms: Miller and Kiger built structures with identical bottom-level nodes, which forced unnatural categories in some conditions and confounded category soundness with depth. Participants studied the hierarchies at length and received feedback, so results reflect best-case, trained performance rather than a first visit. A student review states that Miller's study was inconclusive statistically, whereas the abstract describes a clear U-shaped function; I did not see Miller's statistics, so the strength of the effect is uncertain. A popular reading that these results "prove the seven plus or minus two rule for menus" is not supported: G. A. Miller (1956) concerned short-term memory span, and Larson and Czerwinski report their own results do not depend on memory span as the main driver.

### Implications for LTF

- Use this as a defensible starting heuristic, not a law: for a course of a few dozen lessons, two levels (module, then lesson) with roughly 6 to 10 entries per level is a sound default. Do not claim eight is magic; the U shape only says both extremes are worse.
- Category quality outweighs shape. Group lessons by coherent topic and label them in the learner's words (see Snowberry et al.).
- Avoid a three-level hierarchy for core course content (course, unit, module, lesson); if the syllabus grows, add a search or "continue where you left off" path instead of adding depth.
- For the fly-mode reference (checklists, speeds), keep a single screen with a flat list of items grouped by phase of flight, since this is the "64 items on one screen" case where grouping makes broad pages work.
- Findings come from text menus and trained users, so validate with learner tests in LTF.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record for DOI 10.1177/107118138102500179 (title, author Dwight P. Miller, volume 25 issue 1, pages 296-300, October 1981) including the abstract (depth one to six, breadth two to 64, U-shaped function, minimum at two levels of eight); full text of Larson and Czerwinski (1998), Zaphiris (2000) and a student survey of depth/breadth studies for the descriptions of structures, replications and criticisms. Miller's paper itself was not read.

## kiger1984depth

Kiger, J. I. (1984). The depth/breadth trade-off in the design of menu-driven user interfaces. *International Journal of Man-Machine Studies, 20*(2), 201-213. Journal article (laboratory experiment with performance and preference data).

### Core claims

Kiger extended Miller's (1981) experiment to test whether broad, shallow menu trees really beat deep, narrow ones, and to add asymmetric structures. Secondary accounts agree he found that time and errors rose with depth and that broad structures performed best, and he recommended keeping tree depth low with fairly broad menus. In Larson and Czerwinski's quotation of his discussion, he said the data pointed to a preference and performance advantage for broad, shallow trees, and gave as a general principle that depth should be minimized by providing broad menus of up to eight or nine items each.

### Specifics

- Structures: five menu designs with 64 bottom-level nodes. Three symmetric trees (eight items on each of two levels, four items on each of three levels, two items on each of six levels) plus two asymmetric two-level structures: a 4-item menu followed by a 16-item menu, and a 16-item menu followed by a 4-item menu. He dropped the flat 64-item condition used by Miller.
- Measures: response time, errors and preference or ease-of-use ratings.
- Results as reported by others: the 4-then-16 structure had the fastest response times and the fewest errors, followed closely by, and not reliably different from, the 16-then-4 and 8-by-8 structures. Subjects favored the two-level 8-by-8 structure in ratings of ease of use and preference. Deeper structures cost time and errors. One secondary account says search times slowed when a menu had more than eight items.
- Because the winner on speed was a 4-then-16 menu, which has a 16-item page, the finding is not simply "eight is best." It is that two levels beat three or six.

### Replication and criticism

Zaphiris and Mtei (1997) repeated Kiger's structures on the Web with hyperlinks and found results in line with his: the 8-by-8 structure was fastest, followed by 4-by-16 with no reliable difference. Zaphiris (2000) found that depth drove task time and number of links per page did not, in the range 2 to 16 links. Larson and Czerwinski (1998) agree depth hurts, but warn that the same-bottom-node control creates unnatural categories (for example "Science News: Psychology" as one structure's category repeated as two levels in another). Larson and Czerwinski note that the best structures in these early studies had breadths near G. A. Miller's "seven plus or minus two," but their own best results came with 16 and 32 items per page, and they conclude memory is only one variable. The experiments involve trained participants and artificial item sets.

### Implications for LTF

- Where learners must choose among sections, a two-level layout with one broad level is supported by several independent experiments. Use it for the course map.
- Do not assume users prefer what is fastest: Kiger's participants preferred the 8-by-8 structure while 4-then-16 was fastest. When trading speed against preference in LTF, collect both and test.
- Asymmetric structures work: a short top-level (a few big categories) followed by a longer, well-grouped second level (many lessons) is a legitimate pattern for a course with few modules and many lessons.
- Count hierarchy levels, not only items per page, when auditing navigation.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record for DOI 10.1016/s0020-7373(84)80018-8 (title, author John I. Kiger, volume 20 issue 2, pages 201-213, February 1984; no abstract returned); descriptions of Kiger's structures and results from the full texts of Larson and Czerwinski (1998), Zaphiris (2000) and the Zaphiris et al. expandable-indexes paper (cs.umd.edu PDF), and a student survey. Kiger's paper itself was not read.

## larson1998web

Larson, K., & Czerwinski, M. (1998). Web page design: Implications of memory, structure and scent for information retrieval. In *Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '98)* (pp. 25-32). ACM. Conference paper (laboratory experiment, within-subjects).

### Core claims

The authors tested whether the menu-era finding that broad, shallow structures win holds on the Web when each structure is built from natural categories with good "scent." The abstract reports that increased depth did harm search performance on the Web, but that a medium condition of depth and breadth outperformed the broadest shallow structure overall. They warn against generalizing to the idea that broader is always better, and argue that designers must balance the number of category decisions against the number of items to be scanned on a page.

### Specifics

- Participants: 19 experienced computer and web users from a Microsoft volunteer pool.
- Materials: three structures each with 512 bottom-level nodes built from the Encarta encyclopedia: 8x8x8 (three levels of eight), 16x32 (16 top-level categories with 32 content items each) and 32x16. Because categories were made natural, only a quarter of the items (128) appeared in all three structures, and those were the possible search targets. An editor, not a user test, set the category contents, which the authors admit as a limitation. Pages with 8 or 16 items used one column and 32 items used two columns, so no scrolling was needed.
- Procedure: 8 searches per structure, 24 trials in random order, with the target shown on paper. Pre-tests (Auditory Number Span and Finding A's, one fifth of each) measured memory span and visual scanning. Lostness followed Smith's measure (above 0.5 lost, below 0.4 not lost).
- Reaction time: 16x32 fastest (36 seconds, SD 16), 32x16 next (46 seconds, SD 26), 8x8x8 slowest (58 seconds, SD 23); main effect F(2,36) = 6.34, p < .01. 8x8x8 was reliably slower than both two-level structures; 16x32 versus 32x16 was not reliably different.
- Time-outs (5 minutes) numbered 14 in total: 9 in 8x8x8, 2 in 16x32, 3 in 32x16.
- Lostness: 0.35 (not lost) in 16x32, 0.49 in 32x16, 0.63 (very lost) in 8x8x8; 8x8x8 was reliably worse.
- Preference: 32x16 was ranked best most often (11 of 19), but ratings on the five Likert items did not differ reliably.
- Pre-tests: correlations with time were not reliable (memory span r = -0.27, visual scanning r = -0.12).

### Replication and criticism

The result that three levels beat two on the Web has been echoed by Zaphiris (2000) and others, and the paper itself reviews earlier work. It is a single study with 19 subjects and one well-edited content domain; error data showed no meaningful pattern because participants had to keep searching until they found the target. The authors note that earlier studies used trained subjects and identical bottom nodes, so they reflect best-case performance. They argue that memory is only one variable and that, within their conditions, depth was a stronger determinant than the number of items on a page. The paper is also a useful correction to two myths: "seven plus or minus two items per menu" (G. A. Miller's finding concerns immediate memory span, and their best structures had 16 and 32 items per page) and "broader is always better" (they cite designs with enormous breadth, and their 32 top-level structure had more subjects feeling lost than the 16 one, though not reliably).

### Implications for LTF

- A two-level hierarchy with 16 to 32 well-labeled items on the broad level is defensible when categories are sound and the page is scannable without scrolling. Use this as an upper bound for an LTF menu, not a target.
- Balance decisions against scanning. If a module list gets long, group it visibly instead of adding a third level.
- Because the best condition had the clearest top-level labels (scent), invest in label testing before restructuring.
- Track "lostness" in LTF user tests: unique pages visited versus pages required. It is a simple, quantifiable signal.
- Preference and performance diverged in this study, so do not choose a navigation structure on preference alone.

## zaphiris2000depth

Zaphiris, P. G. (2000). Depth vs breadth in the arrangement of Web links. *Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting, 44*(4), 453-456. Conference paper (small within-subjects experiment).

### Core claims

Zaphiris applied the depth/breadth menu-tradeoff procedures of Miller and Kiger to Web link hierarchies. The abstract reports that task completion time increases as the depth of the site structure increases. In the discussion he adds that the number of links per page (over the range of 2 to 16) did not seem to affect retrieval speed; depth did.

### Specifics

- Participants: 17 (6 women, 11 men), aged 19 to 45 (mean 28.3, SD 6.0), mostly University of Maryland students, all with at least two years of computer use and weekly Internet use.
- Materials: five link hierarchies with 64 end nodes of general information about Cyprus from an almanac, built in a web editor on a local server: 2x6 (breadth 2, depth 6), 4x3, 8x2, and two asymmetric two-level designs (4 then 16 links, and 16 then 4 links). Following Kiger, the 2x6 tree was built first and the others derived from it.
- Procedure: ten search tasks of equal difficulty (two per hierarchy, in random order), with Back-button navigation only. Time and errors came from server logs, and participants ranked the hierarchies on difficulty at the end.
- Mean total time (seconds, SD): 2x6 36.0 (14.9); 4x3 32.2 (27.9); 8x2 20.3 (11.7); 4 then 16 21.0 (11.3); 16 then 4 32.4 (19.8). The effect of structure on time was significant, F(4,16) = 3.20, p < .05.
- Errors (clicks beyond the optimal path) showed no significant effect, F(4,16) = 1.29, because there were few errors.
- Difficulty rankings differed significantly (F(4,16) = 3.40, p < .05): 2x6 was judged hardest and the two mixed structures received the lowest difficulty scores.

### Replication and criticism

The study is a small, quick replication of a menu finding on the Web, run in a class project context, so it is weak evidence on its own. Its anomalies are instructive: the fastest design (8x2) ranked only third on preference, while the slowest of the two-level designs ranked first. The author attributes this to taste for heterogeneous designs but says the data cannot explain it. The no-effect finding for breadth is limited to 2 to 16 links and to a sample of 17. The paper agrees with Kiger (1984) and, in direction, with Larson and Czerwinski (1998). Citations to earlier studies give extra context: Wallace and colleagues reported that when users were rushed they made 96 percent more errors and took 16 percent longer (secondary report in this paper).

### Implications for LTF

- Depth costs time on the Web too, so keep lesson access to two levels.
- Where a page already shows 8 to 16 links, adding more does not appear to slow users much if depth stays low, so do not split a flat list into a deeper tree merely to look tidy.
- Stress degrades performance, a useful reminder for fly mode, where a learner is busy flying: reduce navigation depth and choices there to the minimum.
- Collect preference separately, because it can contradict speed.
- Since the tasks were simple look-ups, validate with LTF-style tasks (such as "find the lesson on crosswind landings").

Zaphiris, vol. 44 issue 4, pages 453-456, July 2000, with abstract); read the full text PDF at ktisis.cut.ac.cy (participants, hierarchies, Tables 1 and 2, discussion). ## porter2003testing

Porter, J. (2003). Testing the three-click rule. User Interface Engineering (UIE), April 16, 2003. Web article (field analysis of usability sessions; with a 2019 NN/g commentary).

### Core claims

Porter analyzed usability session data to check the folk rule that users abandon a site if they cannot find what they want within three clicks. He found no correlation between the number of clicks and task success, and concluded the rule has no support in the data and is "just a myth." What matters to users, he argued, is whether they succeed, not how many clicks they take.

### Specifics

- Data: 44 users, 620 tasks and more than 8,000 clicks on a site. Task lengths ranged from two or three pages up to as many as 25 pages. Users rated satisfaction at the end of each task.
- Success: the distribution of click-stream lengths was the same for successful and unsuccessful tasks. As summarized from the article, 80 percent of tasks were not completed until about 15 clicks, for both successful and unsuccessful streams.
- Abandonment: users did not give up after three clicks.
- Satisfaction: the share of dissatisfied users ranged from only 46 to 61 percent across click-stream lengths; Porter concluded that fewer clicks do not make more satisfied users.
- Complaints: users complained about the number of clicks only when they failed to find what they wanted; successful users did not.
- NN/g (Laubheimer, 2019) calls the rule an arbitrary rule of thumb, cites Zeldman (2001) as the earliest known print reference, notes it conflicts with the other myth of at most seven main navigation categories, and says the right number of clicks depends on task complexity, click cost (page load versus instant expand) and confusion. It recommends strong scent in labels, breadcrumbs, mega menus over cascading dropdowns, hubs and surfacing key tasks.

### Replication and criticism

This is an observational analysis of one set of sessions; the article does not say when the sessions were run beyond "recent," and the method is not a controlled test, so it shows lack of support for the rule rather than proof of a "right" count. It is nevertheless consistent with the information foraging view that scent, not count, drives abandonment. The 44-user, 620-task, 8,000-click figures were confirmed from the UIE page itself.

### Implications for LTF

- Do not use a click budget as a design rule or an acceptance test. Replace it with task success and "did the learner know where they were."
- A longer path with clear labels beats a shorter path with ambiguous ones, which supports step-by-step lesson flows over cramming content on one screen.
- Measure what matters: task success, time to find a lesson, and reversals.
- Make clicks cheap (fast loads, instant expand) and every click confidence-building.

## furnas1987vocabulary

Furnas, G. W., Landauer, T. K., Gomez, L. M., & Dumais, S. T. (1987). The vocabulary problem in human-system communication. *Communications of the ACM, 30*(11), 964-971. Journal article (empirical study of word choice plus simulation).

### Core claims

People name the same thing with very different words, and systems that expect users to guess one "correct" word therefore fail often. The abstract states that spontaneous word choice for objects in five application-related domains was surprisingly variable, with two people favoring the same term with probability below 0.20 in every case. The authors conclude that for success without extensive training, or on a first try at a new target, a system must recognize the terms people will choose spontaneously.

### Specifics

Details I could confirm come from the abstract and from two secondary records of it; I could not read the full paper this session, so the names of the five domains, the number of participants and the simulation settings are not reported here.

- Measure: the probability that two people, asked to name the same object or action, would choose the same word. In every one of the five domains the abstract reports this as below 0.20.
- Simulation: the authors simulated vocabulary-driven access schemes and found that the popular approach of access through one designer's favorite single word would produce 80 to 90 percent failure rates in many common situations.
- Remedy: "unlimited aliasing," meaning that the system accepts as many alternative words as possible for the same target. The abstract reports that it was derived as the optimal strategy and was capable of several-fold improvements over single-word access.
- Crossref also lists an earlier paper by the same four authors, "Database Systems: Human Factors in Data Access" (Bell System Technical Journal, 1982), which I did not read.

### Replication and criticism

I found no direct replication this session. A research paper from Google applies the unlimited-aliasing idea to shared tags by letting users rename tags locally while the underlying tag stays shared; that is an application, not a replication. Limits: the headline probability below 0.20 describes free naming of objects in the studied domains; it does not say that every label in every interface has under a 20 percent hit rate, and it does not say users cannot learn a vocabulary with training. A secondary summary gives the chance of agreement as roughly one in eight in some demonstration, which is not in the abstract and is treated here as unconfirmed. The paper's point is that designers cannot assume their own word is the learner's word.

### Implications for LTF

This is the strongest argument in the lane for testing labels with beginners, because LTF's learners do not yet share the vocabulary of pilots.

- Search must be forgiving. Index lessons and glossary entries under several terms (the aviator's term, the sim's term, a plain-language term), and show the aliases ("also called ...") on the lesson page.
- Do not rely on one label. A lesson on the flare could be reached from "landing", "round out" or "touchdown"; plan synonyms and redirect.
- Use card sorting and label tests (see Spencer and NN/g) with real beginners, not with instructors, to find the words they use.
- Glossary pop-ups and first-use definitions are a form of aliasing: they connect the learner's word to the formal word.
- Avoid unexplained abbreviations in navigation labels. In fly mode, where the learner cannot afford a failed lookup, offer both the short term and a plain one.
- Analytics: log search queries that return nothing and treat them as a list of missing aliases.

## bates1989design

Bates, M. J. (1989). The design of browsing and berrypicking techniques for the online search interface. *Online Review, 13*(5), 407-424. Journal article (conceptual model grounded in the information-seeking literature).

### Core claims

Bates proposed a new model of searching, "berrypicking," which she argued is much closer to how real searchers behave than the classic information retrieval model, and which therefore should guide interface design. In the classic model a user states a single query, matches it against a database, and receives a single output set. In berrypicking, the query itself keeps changing as the searcher learns from what they find, and the need is met by a series of selections gathered bit by bit, like picking scattered berries rather than harvesting a bunch. The paper then argues that online systems should support the full range of techniques searchers use, with particular attention to browsing.

### Specifics

The model differs from the classic one in four respects: the nature of the query (an "evolving search," where the query and not only the search terms shifts), the nature of the process (a bit-at-a-time "berrypicking" path rather than one best retrieved set), the range of techniques used, and the information domain or territory searched. Bates describes six widely used techniques, drawing on Stoan and on Ellis:

1. Footnote chasing (backward chaining), following references from books and articles.
2. Citation searching (forward chaining), finding who cites a known item.
3. Journal run, reading the run of a central journal in an area.
4. Area scanning, browsing materials collocated with those already found, such as shelves.
5. Subject searches in bibliographies and abstracting and indexing services.
6. Author searching.

Evidence cited includes Stenstrom and McBride, who found that over 87 percent of social science faculty said they used abstracting journals only occasionally, rarely or never, while 69 percent relied on footnote chasing. She also stresses that browsing and berrypicking are not the same thing, and lists key design features for each technique: for citation chaining, simple jumps in any direction without "returning to go"; for journal runs, easy moves between contents lists and articles; for area scanning, ability to browse the classification at several levels of generality and to "jump the rails" of the scheme.

### Replication and criticism

The model is taught and cited in information science courses (Berkeley information school lecture slides cite it), and its emphasis on evolving needs and browsing is close to what Marchionini later called exploratory search. It is an argument from earlier studies of mostly academic searchers, not an experiment, and Bates herself notes that much of the evidence comes from social scientists and humanists in academic settings, though she suggests the pattern applies more widely. A recent arXiv paper titled "Information Farming: From Berry Picking to Berry Growing" shows the model is still being built upon. The paper makes design suggestions but does not report a tested interface.

### Implications for LTF

- Learners do not read a course once from start to finish. They re-open earlier lessons, look up a term, jump from a quiz to the lesson, and hop between the sim and the site. Support it: every lesson page should link to its prerequisites and to the next lesson, and glossary terms should link to the lessons where they are used.
- Provide several ways in, as Bates recommends: search, the course map, "continue where you left off," and tags or topics. Do not force a single path.
- Citation-like cross-links ("used in", "see also") enable forward and backward chaining within the course.
- Allow area-scanning style browsing: show neighboring lessons and checklists in the same module so serendipitous discovery is possible.
- Treat the learner's goal as evolving. A challenge failure may redirect them to a lesson; the interface should respond by suggesting that lesson, not just a retry.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record for DOI 10.1108/eb024320 (title, author, Online Review vol. 13 issue 5, pages 407-424, 1989, abstract); read the author's full-text reprint at pages.gseis.ucla.edu/faculty/bates/berrypicking.html through the section on browsing design features (the six techniques, the Stenstrom and McBride percentages and the design features quoted above); web search results listing the Berkeley lecture slides and the arXiv paper (titles only).

## marchionini2006exploratory

Marchionini, G. (2006). Exploratory search: From finding to understanding. *Communications of the ACM, 49*(4), 41-46. Journal article (conceptual and review article, special-section opener).

### Core claims

Marchionini distinguishes lookup (fact retrieval and known-item search) from the richer activities of learning and investigating, which together he calls exploratory search. His abstract-level claim is that the research tools critical for exploratory search success involve new interfaces that move the process beyond predictable fact retrieval. In his account, search for learning and investigation is not just a harder lookup: it involves browsing and repeated iterations.

### Specifics

I could not read the full article this session; the confirmed content is the one-sentence abstract plus accounts of the article in a special-issue review and a later literature review.

- Taxonomy: three kinds of search activity, lookup, learn and investigate. A literature review (Medlar, Kotkov and Głowacka, 2023) summarizes lookup as depending on well-defined, unambiguous queries that retrieve discrete facts or specific documents, and describes learn and investigate as the core exploratory activities, requiring browsing, several search iterations and assessment of many potentially relevant results. Learning is illustrated by self-directed learning across multiple sources. Investigation involves synthesis, discovery and planning (for example a literature review), and leans on recall.
- Definition: exploratory search as a process that blends querying with browsing strategies to satisfy complex information needs.
- Context: the article opened a special issue of Communications of the ACM (April 2006); the special-issue introduction (pages 36 to 39) frames it as opening the discussion of the difference between exploratory search and lookup or retrieval.

### Replication and criticism

This is a conceptual framework, not an experiment, and the same literature review reports that experimental studies often define exploratory search indirectly through task attributes borrowed from the learning and investigation goals (Wildemuth and Freund, 2012). Savolainen (2018) is cited as arguing that the distinctions are unnecessary and that exploratory search can be described with other information-seeking models. White and Roth are cited as treating exploratory search as both a problem context and a search process with uncertainty and proposing a two-phase model of exploratory browsing and focused searching. The taxonomy is therefore influential but contested at the margins.

### Implications for LTF

- Separate "lookup" from "learn" in the interface. A learner mid-flight who needs a V-speed or a checklist item is doing lookup and needs a direct, fast answer in one step. A learner starting a topic is doing exploratory learning and needs overviews, related lessons and room to browse.
- The dashboard should offer an overview for exploring, while fly mode should be lookup-only.
- Support learning with comparisons (two procedures side by side), progressive disclosure and "why" explanations, not only answers.
- Do not measure the whole product by time-to-answer. Exploratory pages are better judged on understanding, quiz results and return visits.

## rosenfeld2015information

Rosenfeld, L., Morville, P., & Arango, J. (2015). *Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond* (4th ed.). Sebastopol, CA: O'Reilly Media. Book (practitioner textbook; craft authority built on three earlier editions, the first published in 1998).

### Core claims

The authors present information architecture (IA) as the structural design of shared information environments, and treat findability as a critical success factor for usability: if people cannot find what they need through some combination of browsing, searching and asking, the system fails. The fourth edition widens the scope from websites to digital products across devices. Its argument is that good IA is an integrated system of organization, labeling, navigation and search, supported by metadata and controlled vocabularies, built from research into context, content and users.

### Specifics

I read the publisher's preview (front matter, table of contents and the opening of Chapter 2) but not the full chapters.

- Four working definitions in Chapter 2: the structural design of shared information environments; the synthesis of organization, labeling, search and navigation systems; the art and science of shaping information products to support usability, findability and understanding; and an emerging discipline and community of practice.
- Terms: the authors distinguish information from data (facts in highly structured databases) and from knowledge (in people's heads), placing information in "the messy middle," where there is often no single right answer. They define structuring (granularity of information "atoms" and their relations), organizing (grouping into meaningful categories) and labeling (what to call the categories and navigation elements).

- Secondary summaries of the book's organization schemes list exact schemes (alphabetical, chronological, geographical) as objective and mutually exclusive, and ambiguous schemes (topic, task, audience, metaphor, hybrid) as subjective; one summary reports the book's view that ambiguous schemes, though harder to build, often serve users better. I could not confirm that wording against the book.

### Replication and criticism

This is craft and expert opinion, not experimental evidence, though it synthesizes research methods (card sorting, usability testing, log analysis). The authors themselves took the fourth edition beyond the web (the subtitle changed to "For the Web and Beyond"), a response to the earlier web-centric framing. I found no independent evaluation of the framework this session. Research elsewhere in this lane (information foraging, berrypicking, the vocabulary problem) is consistent with the book's emphasis on labels and multiple routes to content.

### Implications for LTF

- Use the four systems as an audit frame for the LTF site: organization (modules, lessons, challenges, glossary), labeling (names consistent across menu, page title and breadcrumb), navigation (global, local, in-lesson, "next"), and search.
- Decide scheme by purpose: a beginner course is naturally task or sequence based, which is an ambiguous scheme, but reference material (V-speeds, checklists, glossary) works best with exact schemes such as alphabetical or by phase of flight, so learners can look up known items.
- Ask the book's own question before building: does a product of LTF's size need search? A course of a few dozen lessons may serve most learners with good organization and a glossary, with search added for glossary and checklists.
- Treat metadata as an asset: tag lessons with aircraft system, phase of flight and skill, to drive related-lesson links and the dashboard.
- Keep sitemaps and a content model as living documents; the book's final chapter on design and documentation covers sitemaps, wireframes, content inventory and style guides.

## spencer2009card

Spencer, D. (2009). *Card Sorting: Designing Usable Categories*. Brooklyn, NY: Rosenfeld Media. Book (practitioner guide to a research method).

### Core claims

Spencer presents card sorting as a practical technique for discovering how people group and label content, and gives a complete process: choose the method, content and participants, make the cards, run the sort, analyze the data and apply the results. A reviewer summarizes her central message as that card sorting is a tool, and "you are the one who is doing the thinking": it informs decisions but does not replace them.

### Specifics

All details come from reviews and listings, since the book itself was not accessible.

- Content: a review in the Designer's Review of Books describes an under-200-page book that covers only card sorting, beginning with brief chapters on theory and benefits, then following the stages of a sort. Four preparation chapters cover choosing the method, choosing the content, choosing participants and making cards; one chapter covers managing the sort; the final four cover analysis and reporting.
- Methods compared: open and closed sorts, individual and group sorts, and in-person and software-based sorts. A retailer description adds hybrid sorts and remote sessions.
- Open versus closed (general definitions reported by a summary source): in an open sort participants create their own categories and names, which is generative; in a closed sort they place cards in predefined categories, which tests whether the names work.
- Content chapter: how to select representative items from a site with thousands of pages, how to handle a mix of content and functionality, and how to write card labels that do not steer people toward particular groups. The reviewer calls it the most valuable chapter and attributes the failure of his first sort to not following this advice.
- Analysis: a spreadsheet technique Spencer refined over several projects (offered as a free download), with only cursory treatment of statistical methods.

### Replication and criticism

The book is craft advice from a practitioner, not research. The Designer's Review of Books rated it 3.5 stars, called it practical and a "fail-proof recipe" for those new to the method, and said experienced practitioners would find little groundbreaking; it noted thin theory and statistics. Independent evidence on how many participants a sort needs comes from Tullis and Wood (see the nielsen2004card section). My own caution, not taken from the sources: results depend on which cards are chosen, participants do not experience the product while sorting, and a sort shows groupings, not task success, so pair it with usability tasks.

### Implications for LTF

- Run an open card sort with beginners, using one card per lesson and glossary term, to see whether they group lessons the way the syllabus does and what they call the groups. Use their labels to check module names.
- Use a closed sort (or a tree test, a related technique) after any restructuring to check that learners can place lessons in the proposed modules.
- Write cards in learner language and avoid aviation jargon that gives away the answer or biases the grouping.
- Pick cards representatively for a large set instead of testing all pages at once; LTF could test one module at a time.
- Remember Spencer's caution: the designer does the thinking. Sorting results should inform, not dictate, the curriculum order, which depends on pedagogy and safety sequencing the sort cannot see.

## nielsen2004card

Nielsen, J. (2004, July 18). Card sorting: How many users to test. Nielsen Norman Group. Web article reporting a study by Tullis and Wood.

### Core claims

Nielsen reports a study by Tom Tullis and Larry Wood of Fidelity Investments that used resampling to answer how many participants a card sorting study needs. His conclusion is that card sorting needs more users than traditional usability testing, and that 15 is the number to aim for in most projects.

### Specifics

- Data: Tullis and Wood had 168 users complete a card sort. They then drew random subsets of different sizes and compared each subset's similarity scores with the full-sample results, presenting the work at the 2004 UPA conference.
- Correlation with the full-sample result by number of users: 5 users, 0.75; 15 users, 0.90; 30 users, 0.95; 60 users, 0.98. Nielsen estimates about 0.93 at 20 users. Gains flatten after about 15.
- Recommendations: Tullis and Wood recommend 20 to 30 users. Nielsen recommends 15 for most projects and 30 for large, well-funded ones, and says his teams have had good results with as few as 12 when participants were hard to recruit.
- Contrast: Nielsen says traditional usability testing needs about 5 users, while card sorting is a generative method that needs more participants to reach stable results.
- Caveats from Nielsen: it is a single study (he would like replications with other companies and information spaces), the analysis was purely quantitative and ignored participants' comments, and numeric similarity scores should not be the only basis for an architecture. He recommends iterating with usability evaluations of a draft design, which are cheaper than enlarging card sorts.

### Replication and criticism

I found no replication this session, and Nielsen himself asks for one. The sample is a single company's content, which limits generalizing to a learning product. The resampling method measures stability of similarity scores, not whether the resulting architecture works. Nielsen is a respected usability authority; the article is expert commentary on someone else's study and reports no method for the study beyond what is given above.

### Implications for LTF

- Budget about 15 beginner participants for an open sort of the course structure; a pilot of 5 can find gross problems but should not set the final structure.
- Because LTF's target is complete beginners, recruit people who have never flown, not simulation enthusiasts who already know the terms; their groupings differ.
- Add a qualitative pass: ask participants to explain groupings, and read comments, since numbers alone can mislead.
- After restructuring, run quick usability tasks ("find where to learn about the flaps") on a prototype, as Nielsen advises.
- Re-run small sorts when the syllabus doubles, not every release.

Confirmed this session: fetched nngroup.com/articles/card-sorting-how-many-users-to-test/ (author Jakob Nielsen, July 18, 2004; Tullis and Wood study details, correlation table and recommendations). The original Tullis and Wood paper was not read.
