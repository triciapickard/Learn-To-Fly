# Lane 25: Icons, pictograms, symbols and illustration

Research notes for the Learn to Fly Design Knowledge Base. Sources are ordered from the icon-recognition experiments, through instructional illustration, to the historical craft traditions and testing standards. Every number below was read in the source itself, in its abstract, or in a fetched secondary account named in the "Confirmed this session" line. Where only an abstract or a secondary summary was available, the section says so.

## mcdougall1999measuring

McDougall, S. J. P., Curry, M. B., & de Bruijn, O. (1999). Measuring symbol and icon characteristics: Norms for concreteness, complexity, meaningfulness, familiarity, and semantic distance for 239 symbols. *Behavior Research Methods, Instruments, & Computers*, 31(3), 487-519.

### Core claims

Research on why some symbols are easy to use had been held back because every lab built its own idiosyncratic stimulus set, so the symbol property being manipulated was usually the judgment of one experimenter rather than a controlled variable. The authors supply rating norms for 239 symbols and icons on five properties that earlier work had singled out: concreteness, visual complexity, meaningfulness, familiarity and semantic distance. Their most useful argument is that these properties are not one thing. Concreteness and complexity in particular had been confounded in earlier studies, so some of the apparent "concrete icons are better" findings may have been "detailed icons are better" or "simple icons are better" findings in disguise.

They also propose treating Peirce's three-way split of signs (icon, index, symbol) as a single continuum of semantic distance, the closeness of the relationship between what a symbol shows and what it means.

### Specifics

Participants were 200 students from one Welsh university, each paid a small fee; 40 students rated each of the five dimensions, with equal numbers of women and men. Symbols came from a deliberately broad corpus: electrical equipment, public information symbols (including AIGA and ISO sets), military symbology, websites, vehicle and aircraft controls and displays, and computer interfaces. Ten volunteers first sorted the corpus into concrete, moderately concrete and abstract (68, 61 and 69 symbols), simple, moderately complex and complex, and familiar, moderately familiar and unfamiliar groups, and the final set was picked to spread evenly across those bins. By use, the set held 77 computer symbols, 48 traffic and public information symbols, 69 industrial symbols and 50 household symbols.

Each rater received booklets of 20 pages with 12 symbols per page and a 5-point scale beside each symbol, ordered by Latin square. Concreteness meant "depicts real objects, materials or people"; complexity meant amount of detail or intricacy; familiarity meant how often the rater had met the symbol; semantic distance was rated with the function label supplied (1 = not closely related, 5 = very strongly related). For meaningfulness, raters also wrote what they thought the symbol meant, which yielded two agreement measures: concept agreement (percent giving the intended function, synonyms accepted) and name agreement (percent giving the single most common answer, right or wrong). Complexity was also computed with a published component-counting metric (lines at three orientations, closed figures, open figures, letters).

Reliability was high: split-half correlations were .95 (concreteness), .94 (complexity), .95 (familiarity), .96 (meaningfulness) and .95 (semantic distance). The complexity metric correlated with the complexity ratings at rs = .73. Mean ratings sat near the scale midpoint (concreteness 3.26, complexity 2.62, familiarity 2.97, meaningfulness 2.80, semantic distance 2.59). The sobering number is mean concept agreement: 15.49 percent, with a median of 2.5 percent. Most of these real-world symbols were not correctly guessed by most people with no context.

Key correlations: concreteness and complexity were unrelated (n.s.), so concrete symbols can be simple; concreteness correlated .78 with familiarity, .82 with meaningfulness and .65 with semantic distance; familiarity and meaningfulness correlated .93 and were described as virtually interchangeable; concept agreement correlated most strongly with semantic distance (.69). The authors observed that people often used a "picture-labeling" strategy, naming what they saw, which made a symbol feel meaningful and familiar even when the guessed function was wrong (a portable-file symbol read as luggage storage).

### Replication and criticism

This is a measurement resource, not a theory test, so it has been used rather than replicated. Later icon papers from the same group (see the Isherwood and McDougall entries in this lane) run learning experiments on the same icon set and report that semantic distance and familiarity predict identification better than concreteness. Limits are plain: raters were students at one university in the late 1990s, the set leans on desktop icons of that period, and ratings are subjective and heavily intercorrelated, which later authors flag as a problem when trying to tease the properties apart in regression. The rating of complexity is also tangled with familiarity (familiar items look simpler).

### Implications for LTF

Treat "recognizable" as an empirical property, not a designer's opinion. The 15 percent mean concept agreement is the warning for any aviation icon (a flap handle, a trim wheel, a mixture knob): an icon a pilot finds obvious will not be obvious to a beginner. For each icon in a lesson, fly mode or challenge briefing, decide whether the audience can reach its meaning, and if the answer is "not at first glance" pair it with a text label. Keep detail down where speed of finding matters (a fly-mode toolbar) but do not assume a bare geometric shape is simpler to understand than a small, close metaphor. When a new icon is proposed, a short in-house rating round of semantic distance and a free-text "what does this mean?" probe with five or six beginners is the cheap version of this paper's method.

## mcdougall2000exploring

McDougall, S. J. P., de Bruijn, O., & Curry, M. B. (2000). Exploring the effects of icon characteristics on user performance: The role of icon concreteness, complexity, and distinctiveness. *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 6(4), 291-306.

### Core claims

The paper tests, with experiments rather than ratings, three properties that design folklore treated as simple virtues: make icons concrete, make them simple, make them distinct from their neighbors. The summary of the abstract that I could retrieve states the answer in one line each. Concreteness effects were mainly about the initial grasp of an icon's meaning. Complexity effects lasted longer and were tied to how efficiently users could search a display for an icon. Distinctiveness was the most intricate case, but icons became more distinctive when designers used both semantic contrast (different meanings) and visual contrast (different appearance) together.

### Specifics

According to the abstract summary, the authors first quantified concreteness, complexity and discriminability for their icon sets (the same group's norms paper, see the mcdougall1999measuring entry, set out the rating approach; I did not confirm that this paper used those exact ratings), then measured the effect of each on user performance while varying how experienced users were, what the task demanded, and the context in which icons appeared. A later paper from the same group (McDougall and Isherwood 2009) describes the design in passing: Experiment 1 was a visual search task and Experiment 2 was an identification task. In that account, visual complexity matters when people must search a display for an icon, and has much less of a role when they must identify an icon they are looking at.

That split is the key practical point. "Simple" and "complex" are not good or bad in general; they are good or bad for a task. Search is a perceptual job (find the one that looks like X among many), and extra detail slows it. Identification is a meaning job (what does this mean?), and extra detail is less harmful there and may even help by giving a viewer more to interpret. The paper also complicates the claim that concrete means better: the concreteness advantage was an early-learning advantage, which fits the later finding that early differences between concrete and abstract icons diminish with practice.

I read the abstract summary and later descriptions of the paper, not the full text, so I have not confirmed participant numbers, exact timings or effect sizes, and I give none.

### Replication and criticism

The pattern of findings has been carried forward rather than overturned. Isherwood, McDougall and Curry (2007) found that icon characteristics accounted for up to 69 percent of the variance in identification performance and that their relative importance shifted with experience. McDougall and Isherwood (2009) summarize the same literature: complexity effects are tied to visual search and are "far from robust" in the picture-naming literature, partly because rated complexity is confounded with familiarity. This is a laboratory result with students on icon sets of its era, so it says little about modern flat or outline icon styles, and it is not an app study.

### Implications for LTF

Choose icon detail by task. For anything a learner must locate quickly among several items (the strip of controls in fly mode, tabs, a row of checklist-status markers), keep the glyphs simple and visually distinct from one another, and make neighbors differ in meaning as well as shape. For icons that are looked at one at a time to learn what they mean (a lesson sidebar glossary, a quiz illustration) a little more depicted detail is acceptable. Do not expect a more concrete picture to keep paying off after a learner has used the app for a few sessions; meaning is learned, and the label can retire once the learner knows the set. Distinctiveness is a property of a set, so review icons side by side, not one at a time.

## isherwood2007icon

Isherwood, S. J., McDougall, S. J. P., & Curry, M. B. (2007). Icon identification in context: The changing role of icon characteristics with user experience. *Human Factors*, 49(3), 465-476.

### Core claims

Almost all earlier icon studies looked at one or two icon characteristics at the moment users first meet an icon set. This paper asks what happens as people gain experience, and which characteristics matter most overall. The answer: icon characteristics were major determinants of performance, together accounting for up to 69 percent of the variance in speed and accuracy, but their ranking changes with practice. Semantic distance (how closely the picture relates to the function) is crucial at first, while people are learning which icon goes with which function. Familiarity matters later, because it has lasting effects on access to long-term memory representations. The authors conclude that concreteness may not be of primary importance and that designers need to attend to semantic distance and familiarity. The exact balance depends on whether icons are rarely encountered or frequently used.

### Specifics

Thirty participants carried out an icon identification task over a long series of trials designed to simulate learning through experience. The four icon characteristics investigated were semantic distance, concreteness, familiarity and visual complexity, the same four properties that the group's earlier rating norms quantify (see mcdougall1999measuring). Performance was measured as speed and accuracy. The 69 percent variance figure is an upper bound across the learning series, not a constant.

A later paper that extends this work (McDougall and Isherwood 2009) reports what Isherwood et al. found about particular properties: familiarity with the graphic predicted response time in early trials, which the authors read as people using their knowledge of the depicted object to work out what the icon means; and initial performance differences between concrete and abstract icons are not long lasting, fading as users gain experience.

The design implication stated in the abstract is practical and testable: designers should consider other characteristics than concreteness, especially semantic distance and familiarity, and the relative importance depends on how often an icon will be met.

### Replication and criticism

The same group reproduced the dynamic pattern in a second study with a different analysis. In McDougall and Isherwood (2009) semantic distance was again the best predictor in early trials (accounting for up to 55 percent of variance there), with function familiarity more important in later trials. Earlier work by other groups, cited in both papers, had also found concrete-over-abstract advantages shrink with learning. Limits: regression on subjective ratings that correlate with one another makes it hard to say which property is doing the work; participants were students; and the task was identification of icons from a fixed set, not natural use inside a task. The paper's data do not address modern icon styles or touch interfaces.

### Implications for LTF

A beginner meets every LTF icon cold, then meets it again and again. The two phases need different design attention. In the first minutes the question is semantic distance: does the picture point at the meaning, or is it an arbitrary shape the learner must memorize? Choose metaphors close to the function and always show a label at first exposure. After repeated use, familiarity takes over, which argues for keeping each icon's appearance perfectly stable across lessons, fly mode, dashboards and checklists. Redrawing an icon between lessons throws away the learning the first phase paid for. For rarely seen icons (a once-per-lesson callout type, for example) the early-phase rules never end, so keep their labels.

Confirmed this session: PubMed record 17552310 abstract retrieved through NCBI E-utilities efetch; Crossref DOI record; secondary description in the McDougall and Isherwood 2009 full text.

## mcdougall2009whats

McDougall, S., & Isherwood, S. (2009). What's in a name? The role of graphics, functions, and their interrelationships in icon identification. *Behavior Research Methods*, 41(2), 325-336.

### Core claims

Most icon research studies the picture and the picture-to-meaning relationship. This paper adds the third element, the function name the icon stands for. Treating icon identification like picture naming, with three parts (the graphic, the function, and the relationship between them), the authors show that the strongest predictors of identification change as learning proceeds. In early trials semantic distance was the best predictor of performance, accounting for up to 55 percent of the variance; function familiarity became more important in later trials. Familiarity with the graphic and concreteness mattered too, but concreteness was not a major predictor overall.

### Specifics

The paper describes the icon set as 238 icons (one of the 239 in the 1999 norms was omitted by error from the rating booklets). Eighty-three participants supplied new ratings of the function names, 43 rating concreteness and 40 rating familiarity, on 5-point scales; function familiarity averaged 3.62 and function concreteness 3.02. The identification experiment used 13 blocks of learning trials. Mean response time (the table of block means) fell from about 2.9 seconds in the first block to roughly 1.2 seconds by the later blocks.

Predictors examined were: icon characteristics (concreteness, familiarity, visual complexity), icon-function relationship (semantic distance, name agreement) and function characteristics (concreteness, familiarity, number of syllables). Findings the authors emphasize: semantic distance dominated early accuracy and speed; function familiarity predicted unique variance in accuracy throughout and was the top accuracy predictor after the first blocks; icon familiarity predicted response time early; complexity correlated positively with response time in later blocks but not with accuracy, explaining a modest 9 to 13 percent of variance in blocks 7 to 13, and the authors advise caution because the learning curve had flattened by then. Concreteness was not a major predictor "probably because only a limited number of functions can easily be represented pictorially": for abstract functions (a "search" or "filter" idea) the closeness of the icon-function mapping matters more than how picture-like the icon is. Shorter function names were the familiar ones, in line with Zipf's law.

The discussion also notes that rated complexity is confounded with familiarity (familiar items are rated as less complex) and that picture-naming research itself finds the complexity effect "far from robust."

### Replication and criticism

It is a conceptual extension of Isherwood et al. (2007) with new data. The authors acknowledge that covariance among subjective ratings makes it hard to say what is actually being measured and recommend developing more objective metrics (for example, computed visual complexity). They also observe that mapping each variable to a single processing stage is difficult because the effects are probably interactive. Evidence is a single-lab experiment with student participants and a fixed set of icons.

### Implications for LTF

The function name matters as much as the picture. An unfamiliar word ("squawk", "pitot", "flare") makes any icon harder to identify, and the learner's familiarity with the word grows with lessons. So an icon that stands for a concept the beginner has not learned yet should be introduced together with the lesson that teaches the concept, not before. Use plain, short, frequent words as icon labels where possible. Where an icon stands for a concrete, picturable thing (a headset, a map) a depiction works; for abstract concepts (stability, energy, trim) invest in a close visual metaphor and test it, because concreteness will not do the work.

Confirmed this session: full text PDF read (link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BRM.41.2.325.pdf); Crossref DOI record.

## wiedenbeck1999use

Wiedenbeck, S. (1999). The use of icons and labels in an end user application program: An empirical study of learning and retention. *Behaviour & Information Technology*, 18(2), 68-82.

### Core claims

Icons are often justified as faster to recognize and easier to remember than words, but evidence for beginners learning a real application was thin. Wiedenbeck compared three versions of the same application interface, built with buttons that carried text labels only, icons only, or both together (a fully redundant icon-label design), and followed novice users through an initial learning session and a later delayed session, also recording attitudes. The headline result is that in the first session performance was best with the label-only and the icon-label interfaces. The icon-only interface was clearly poorer at first.

### Specifics

From the abstract text and secondary accounts: the measures covered initial learning, retention across a delay, and perceptions of ease of use and usefulness gathered at the end of each session. In session 1, performance on the icon-only interface was much poorer, particularly in time and in the number of references to help. By session 2 the icon-only users had improved to the point where they approached the other groups. Retention of skill between the sessions was worse for the icon-only interface, but the effect was described as short-lived. Perceptions of ease of use were consistently better for the icon-label interface than for the other two.

A later paper that summarizes the study (found through the search, not read in full) describes 60 undergraduates with little computer experience working with a desktop email program, and says labeled icons were judged easiest to use. I treat the sample description as a secondary report and did not confirm it from the paper itself. One secondary source also says that in the delayed session perceptions of usefulness and task performance became more positive for the icon-only signs; this sits slightly uneasily with the "short-lived" retention gap in the abstract summary, and I could not check the full text to reconcile them. I therefore draw no conclusion from the delayed-session details beyond the abstract-level one: icon-only was not better, and the penalty shrank with practice.

### Replication and criticism

The finding that text labels rescue novices is consistent with the other icon experiments in this lane. The McDougall and Isherwood 2009 paper cites work showing that people often rely on text labels attached to icons to get at meaning, particularly when the graphic is unfamiliar (Guastello, Traut and Korienek 1989; Vukelich and Whitaker 1993, as cited there). The study is one application, one set of icons, and a modest sample of novices, so effect sizes should not be extrapolated. It also tests icons on buttons in the 1990s; a modern interface with larger touch targets and more icon conventions (a magnifying glass for search, a gear for settings) shifts what counts as familiar. I have not seen a failed replication, but I also have not found one that repeats the exact three-way design.

### Implications for LTF

For a beginner audience, the safest default is icon plus visible text label. The evidence says the label does the carrying in the first session, and the icon adds a faster recognition cue and better perceived ease of use once both are present. Lesson pages and quiz answers should never rely on a bare icon to carry a meaning the learner has to get right. Where space is tight (fly mode used next to the simulator on a small second screen), icon-only controls are only acceptable if the learner has already met them with labels in earlier lessons, and if the control has a text tooltip or a spoken-aloud name in the lesson that introduced it. Reserve bare icons for standard conventions the audience already knows from everyday software (close, search, menu), not for flight-specific ideas.

Since the icon-only penalty was largest in time and help look-ups, a challenge briefing that makes the learner hunt for the meaning of an unlabeled icon is spending the very attention budget they need for the simulator. Plan on labels everywhere that a wrong interpretation costs a missed step.

## rogers1989icons

Rogers, Y. (1989). Icons at the interface: their usefulness. *Interacting with Computers*, 1(1), 105-117.

### Core claims

Rogers asks when icons are actually useful rather than merely fashionable at a time when "iconic interfacing" was becoming widespread, and offers a way to reason about them: a classification of icons by the kind of relationship between the picture and the thing it stands for, and a proposal that icon forms could follow a simple grammar that maps onto the structure of the underlying system. The practical stance that other authors draw from her work is that what makes an icon work is how meaningful the link between the depicted thing and the function is, not how pictorial the icon is.

### Specifics

I could not read the paper itself (publisher pages blocked; no open copy located), so what follows is what I could confirm from a full-text paper that describes her work in detail and from two secondary descriptions.

McDougall, Curry and de Bruijn (1999), whose full text I read, describe Rogers's experiments with symbols that varied in concreteness: six symbol types drawn in her doctoral work, namely abstract symbols, concrete analogies associated with an action, concrete objects operated on, and combinations of these. When participants matched written functions to symbols, performance was poor for abstract symbols and even worse when concrete analogies were used to depict functions. Rogers therefore concluded that the critical determinant of usability is the meaningfulness of the relationship between what is depicted and the function it refers to, rather than concreteness as such. McDougall et al. also note that Rogers (1989) endorsed the minimalist idea that extra detail does not help unambiguous, rapid interpretation.

Secondary descriptions add two items. A machine-generated summary of the paper on Semantic Scholar says it outlines a classification of the function and form of icons together with a proposal for a grammar of icon forms that maps onto the system structure. And Lidwell's Universal Principles of Design, in a footnote reproduced on the O'Reilly site, derives its four kinds of iconic representation (similar, example, symbolic and arbitrary) from this paper. Treat those two as pointers, not confirmed readings.

### Replication and criticism

The central claim, that closeness of the picture-function relationship beats concreteness, was later supported and quantified: Isherwood, McDougall and Curry (2007) and McDougall and Isherwood (2009) found semantic distance a stronger early predictor than concreteness. Rogers' own framework is classificatory and conceptual rather than a controlled test across many icons, so the grammar-of-icons idea is a design proposal more than a demonstrated result. A caution from the same literature: concreteness gains are real early in learning for easily pictured functions, so "concreteness is irrelevant" overstates it.

### Implications for LTF

Rogers' lesson is a question to ask of each LTF icon: what is the relationship between the picture and the function, and could a beginner infer it? A four-way classification is a practical review tool. Similar (looks like the thing, a picture of a headset for audio settings), example (a typical instance, a picture of a runway for the whole airport topic), symbolic (a higher-level concept, a gauge for performance) and arbitrary (learned convention, a letter code for a checklist step) have increasingly long learning costs, and the arbitrary kind always needs a label until learned. A consistent grammar also helps a flight-training app with many instruments: if all instrument widgets use one style of frame, one position for the label and one set of state colors, a learner who has learned one can read the next. Do the grammar work once, document it in the design system, and stop adding one-off pictograms that break it.

## gittins1986icon

Gittins, D. (1986). Icon-based human-computer interaction. *International Journal of Man-Machine Studies*, 24(6), 519-543.

### Core claims

An early and widely cited survey of icon-based dialogue, written when graphical icons were still new in commercial interfaces. Per the abstract text, the paper describes the characteristics, advantages and disadvantages of icon-based dialogues, mentions attempts by researchers to measure the effectiveness of icon designs empirically, and attempts a more systematic treatment of icon interfaces than had been offered before, with a classification meant to help the dialogue designer. Later authors cite it as one of the sources of icon design guidelines.

### Specifics

My access was limited to the abstract-level description and to how later papers cite the work, so the specifics are modest. The paper is a long review article (25 pages in a major human-computer interaction journal of the period), and its contribution is organizational: a vocabulary and classification for describing icon interfaces and a discussion of when icons help or hinder. McDougall, Curry and de Bruijn (1999), whose full text I read, cite it (spelling the author's name "Gittens") alongside a 1993 pictogram evaluation paper as an example of the guidelines that "delineate good symbol design practice," and note that such guideline sets are constrained by how much is known about why some symbols are easier to use than others. A citing paper describes it as supporting the idea that simple icons are identified more readily and that reducing icon complexity improves visual search; I treat that as a secondary gloss and did not verify it against the paper.

I did not confirm the contents of the classification scheme, the specific guidelines, or any numerical results, and I do not state them.

### Replication and criticism

This is a survey, not an experiment, so the question of replication applies to the claims it collects. The two guideline claims that it is associated with (keep icons simple; use icons where they offer advantages over text) have been refined, not overturned: the experiments by the McDougall group show simplicity helps visual search more than identification, and the Wiedenbeck study shows that icons without labels hurt novices at first. Guidelines of this era were mainly expert synthesis, with limited testing. The field's later move to rating norms and controlled learning experiments (see the McDougall entries) was partly a reaction to guideline lists that could not say why one icon beat another. The citation spelling variation ("Gittens"/"Gittins") is worth knowing when searching: the Crossref record gives David Gittins.

### Implications for LTF

Use the paper as historical context and as a prompt for an LTF icon policy, not as a rulebook. A short written policy is a better use of the guideline tradition than copying lists: state where LTF uses icons (navigation, state markers, widget controls), where it uses text only (anything a learner has to understand to stay safe in the simulator), and when it uses both. Record, for each icon, its intended meaning, its label and its evidence of comprehension. The early guideline literature is also a reminder that a design convention that feels settled (a gear for settings) is a learned convention; a beginner aviation audience is not guaranteed to share it, so test the three or four most important icons with real beginners before a lesson set depends on them.

Confirmed this session: Crossref DOI record (David Gittins, IJMMS 24(6) 519-543); web search returning the abstract text and citing contexts; McDougall et al. 1999 full text for the way it is cited. The paper itself was not read.

## kendall2016iconic

Kendall, L. N., Raffaelli, Q., Kingstone, A., & Todd, R. M. (2016). Iconic faces are not real faces: enhanced emotion detection and altered neural processing as faces become more iconic. *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*, 1(1), article 19.

### Core claims

Cartoons, emoticons and other iconic images are everywhere, but cognitive science had barely asked what communicative work they do. Psychologists had often used schematic faces as convenient stand-ins for real faces on the unexamined assumption that the two are equivalent. The authors test the opposite idea, taken from comic-book artists' own theory: that iconic (simplified, high-contrast) representations communicate some information, especially emotion, more efficiently than realistic images, and that this is driven by low-level visual features. The paper cites Scott McCloud's popular book on comics as the source of the artist's argument that simplicity of iconic faces lets viewers project themselves into a character. It does not test projection or identification; it tests how fast and accurately emotion is read.

### Specifics

Experiment 1 used 50 undergraduates. Stimuli formed five sets that ran from realistic to schematic: unmanipulated photographs, "rotoscoped" photographs (drawn over to emphasize high-contrast features such as eyes and mouth), "mid-rotoscoped" (the same with the skin tone kept), "mid-cartoon" (iconic faces with a skin tone), and "cartoon" (iconic faces with only the communicative features, white background). Each set had four expressions (disgusted, happy, surprised/"shocked", neutral) with eight variants each, 160 images in all. Each image appeared at four very short presentation times (16.7, 33.3, 50 and 66.7 ms, one to four frames at 60 Hz), 640 trials per participant, between random line masks. A separate group of 60 people rank-ordered the sets by realism, and 70 percent ordered them as intended.

Result: at short presentation times, accuracy for identifying emotion on more cartoonized images was enhanced, and increasing contrast and decreasing featural complexity each benefited accuracy. Experiment 2 recorded an early event-related potential, the P1, which is sensitive to low-level visual features: lower contrast and lower complexity were associated with lower P1 amplitudes, which the authors read as simpler, higher-contrast stimuli being processed more efficiently at an early stage.

### Replication and criticism

I found no direct replication. Notes on scope: the stimuli were four expressions, brief exposures and young adult students; speed of emotion reading on a face is not the same as understanding an icon's function or a pictogram's meaning. The authors' own framing is cautious ("support the hypothesis"). The same group followed with a conference abstract on how symbolic elements in cartoon faces are processed (title confirmed in Crossref, content not read). The finding is consistent with McCloud's intuition about simplification and amplification, and with the lane's other evidence that complexity mainly harms speed of visual processing, but it is an indirect test: it measures a perceptual advantage, not the "identification" claim.

### Implications for LTF

This is evidence that simple, high-contrast marks are read faster and more reliably at a glance, which supports spare, bold glyphs for anything used in fly mode, where a learner's eyes dart between a simulator screen and the app. It supports using a small number of schematic characters or mascots for tone (a calm face on a mistake message) rather than photographic faces. It also argues for keeping contrast high in both light and dark themes: the study varied contrast and complexity separately, and both mattered. Do not stretch it: it does not show that cartoons teach better, and it says nothing about aviation content. Treat it as perceptual support for a visual style, to be backed by comprehension testing for meaning.

Confirmed this session: Crossref DOI record; Europe PMC full text PMC5256461 read (abstract, background, Experiment 1 methods); the Experiment 2 and discussion sections were read only in outline.
