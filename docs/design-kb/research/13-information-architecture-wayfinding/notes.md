## Lynch1960
**Core claims.** Lynch asked how people form mental images of cities and which physical qualities make a city legible, meaning that its parts can be recognised and organised into a coherent pattern. He called the quality "imageability". The book rests on a field study of three American cities (Boston, Jersey City, Los Angeles) in which residents drew sketch maps, described routes, and were interviewed, supplemented by trained observers walking the cities and noting what stood out. From the pooled images he proposed that people structure the city with five types of element. Paths are the channels along which people move (streets, walkways, transit lines) and were the dominant element for most respondents. Edges are linear elements not used as paths (shores, walls, railway cuts) that act as boundaries. Districts are medium-to-large areas with a common identifying character, which the observer mentally enters. Nodes are strategic points, junctions or concentrations, into which the observer can enter. Landmarks are point references, usually simple physical objects, that the observer does not enter.

**Specifics.** An image has three components: identity (a thing is distinguishable from others), structure (its spatial relation to the observer and other objects) and meaning (practical or emotional). Lynch lists qualities that strengthen an element: singularity (contrast of figure against background), form simplicity, continuity, dominance, clarity of joint, directional differentiation, visual scope, motion awareness, time series and names and meanings. Elements interlock: districts are structured with nodes, defined by edges, penetrated by paths and sprinkled with landmarks. Paths are strengthened when they have a recognisable origin and destination, and when the observer can feel a gradient indicating which end is which. Landmarks are more effective when they are visible from many places and prominent in the local context. Lynch also argued for "loose" images that leave room for the viewer's own organisation, rather than a rigid imposed order. The study sample is small, roughly a few dozen interviewees in Boston and fewer elsewhere; exact counts were not rechecked here.

**Quotation.** None given, because the exact wording could not be confirmed this session.

**Criticism and status.** The method is qualitative and the sample was largely middle-class professionals. Appleyard (1970) found that different travellers weight elements differently (car users emphasise paths, locals emphasise use and meaning). Later cognitive science (Siegel and White 1975) proposed a landmark, route and survey sequence of spatial knowledge, which complements the five elements. The five-element vocabulary remains standard in urban design and has been transferred to websites and games as a design heuristic rather than a tested law.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Treat the curriculum as a place with a legible structure: paths (the lesson sequence), districts (phases such as ground, takeoff and climb, cruise, landing), nodes (module hubs and review points), landmarks (milestones such as first landing) and edges (gates between stages). (2) Give each district a stable identity (name, color accent, shape) that never changes across pages or the Day and Night themes. (3) Provide gradient cues on every path, such as "step 4 of 9". (4) Keep landmarks fixed so returning learners find them in the same place. (5) Allow for loose structure: learners may build their own routes, so cross-links should exist. (6) Test legibility by asking new learners to sketch the course from memory after a session and see which elements appear.

## Tolman1948
**Core claims.** Tolman argued that learning in rats is not a matter of chains of stimulus and response but of the formation of a field map of the environment, which he called a cognitive map. This map lets an animal take shortcuts and detours it never practised. Narrow, strip-like maps arise when training is highly repetitive or motivation is extreme, while broader comprehensive maps arise when exploration is varied and motivation moderate. He extended the argument to humans in the final part of the paper, suggesting that narrow maps help explain rigidity, displaced aggression and prejudice, and that education should widen maps.

**Specifics.** The evidence base is a set of maze studies of the 1920s to 1940s: latent learning (rats allowed to wander a maze without food made few errors once food was introduced, which was taken to mean they had learned the layout), place learning (rats trained in a cross maze tended to go to the location of food rather than repeat a particular turn), and the sunburst maze (rats trained on a roundabout route chose a direct path toward the goal when the original route was blocked, as reported by Tolman, Ritchie and Kalish in 1946). The paper is organised as a talk summarising experiments on latent learning, vicarious trial and error, searching for the stimulus, hypotheses and spatial orientation. Exact numbers of animals per experiment are not reported here because they were not rechecked.

**Quotation.** The commonly quoted idea is that "something like a map gets established in the rat's brain". Exact wording not confirmed this session, so no quotation is given.

**Criticism and status.** Behaviorists (Hull, Guthrie) offered stimulus-response accounts of latent learning, and the debate was long. Later neuroscience supports the notion: O'Keefe and Nadel (1978) proposed the hippocampus as a cognitive map, with place cells found in rats. In humans, cognitive maps are fragmentary, distorted and hierarchical, not literal maps (Stevens and Coupe 1978; Tversky 1992). So the term is a metaphor for spatial knowledge rather than a literal picture.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Learners need both route knowledge (follow the lesson list) and survey knowledge (an overview of the whole course and how lessons relate); provide a visible course map as well as a "next" button. (2) Latent learning suggests that browsing and previewing content before it is unlocked is not wasted; consider allowing preview of locked modules. (3) Overly rigid, repetitive routes narrow the map; vary practice and give cross-links (for example, from the stall lesson to the angle-of-attack explainer). (4) Support re-entry after a break with "where you left off" and a short recap. (5) Map-like views (a flight-phase timeline) can make the structure of flying itself feel like a space the learner can move through. (6) Treat moderate motivation pressure as better for broad learning than heavy streak pressure, with caution because the paper is about rats and motivation in animals.

## Passini1984
**Core claims.** Passini reframed wayfinding as a form of spatial problem solving rather than just orientation or map reading. In his model, wayfinding has three interrelated abilities: decision making and the development of a plan of action, decision execution (transforming the plan into behavior at the right place and time), and information processing, which comprises perception and cognition and supplies the other two. Plans are hierarchical: a goal such as "get to the clinic" breaks into sub-goals and decisions that are anchored to places along the route. The central design lesson is that buildings should be designed so that decisions can be made easily, and signs are a supplement, not a repair.

**Specifics.** The evidence comes from field studies in complex public buildings (hospitals, airports, a university campus among others), using verbal protocols in which participants think aloud while finding destinations, plus observation and interviews. Findings he reported include: people form a "decision plan" as a chain of place-anchored decisions; people use whatever information is available, including architectural features, signs, other people and maps; people who feel lost experience stress and anxiety that drive them to ask others or to retreat to a known place; and architectural clarity (sightlines, a legible circulation structure, differentiation of areas) strongly affects the number of decision errors. He distinguishes strategies such as following a main corridor, heading to a known level, and using a central landmark. He also discusses cognitive mapping, arguing that survey knowledge is not required for all wayfinding; route-based plans often suffice. Numbers of participants and exact error rates are not given here because they were not rechecked.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The work is qualitative and building-specific, so it does not give effect sizes. It has nonetheless shaped practice and later research (Arthur and Passini 1992; Golledge 1999). Studies of virtual environments have since found that people differ widely in strategy and spatial ability, supporting Passini's call for multiple information types.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Put decision information at the decision point: at the end of each lesson, show the next action, not only in a menu. (2) Model the learner's navigation as a plan with sub-goals ("learn to land" contains lessons contains steps) and show the nesting. (3) Reduce the anxiety of being lost with consistent landmarks (header, progress bar, course map) and an always-available "back to where I was". (4) Fix structure before labels: if the content grouping is muddled, better wording will not rescue it. (5) Provide several kinds of information (map, text list, search) because people vary in strategy. (6) Use think-aloud sessions to see where learners make their wayfinding decisions, as Passini did.

## Arthur1992
**Core claims.** Arthur and Passini turned wayfinding research into a method for designing signage and wayfinding programs. Their central claim is that wayfinding information must be planned together with the architecture and the user's needs, not added afterwards as a set of signs. Signs support a spatial layout; they cannot fully fix a confusing one. The book is organised around a process (analyse the setting and users, define a decision-point plan, set a naming scheme, design the message and sign types, locate signs, test and maintain) and around a clear taxonomy of sign functions.

**Specifics.** The authors distinguish functional sign types: identification signs (name a place or destination), directional signs (point the way at a decision point), orientation signs (maps and "you are here" displays that show the whole setting) and regulatory and informational signs. Recommended practices include: use one name for one place everywhere (in signs, maps, printed guides and staff speech); put information at decision points and repeat it along the route so that people who have chosen correctly are confirmed (reassurance); keep the number of items on a sign small and group destinations by direction; order items by importance or by logical sequence; align maps so that "up" on the map is "forward" in the world; use consistent colors, forms and typography so that sign types are recognisable at a glance; and plan for maintenance and change so the program stays coherent as the setting evolves. They treat the verbal naming scheme as a design material equal to the graphic design. A widely repeated rule of thumb ties letter height to viewing distance; the exact ratio could not be confirmed this session and is not given.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The evidence is mostly case-based and professional, not experimental. Some specific recommendations (forward-up map alignment) have been supported by independent laboratory research on map orientation, while others rest on practice. The book reflects 1980s and 1990s buildings and printed signs, so digital wayfinding is outside its scope but analogous.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Adopt the sign taxonomy as navigation roles: identification (page and lesson titles), directional (next and previous links), orientation (course map, breadcrumb, progress), informational (tooltips, help). (2) One name per concept across nav, lesson title, checklist and glossary; write the naming scheme before building the navigation. (3) Reassurance cues after key actions, such as "Saved. Next up: Lesson 4". (4) Keep each decision point to a small number of grouped options. (5) Plan for change: lessons will be added, so the structure should accommodate growth without renaming. (6) Test with real users from the start, including a walkthrough where a novice finds a named lesson with no help.

## Mollerup2005
**Core claims.** Mollerup separates wayfinding (the user's activity) from wayshowing (the designer's activity of providing information that supports it). Wayshowing is treated as a design discipline with its own principles, tools and history. He proposes that good wayshowing helps a person to get started, to continue, and to finish, and that designers must choose tools (signs, maps, verbal guidance, pictograms, landmarks) to fit the setting and the user, rather than apply a style.

**Specifics.** The book catalogues design principles for wayshowing and gives extensive examples. Themes include: give information in the right place and at the right time, avoiding both too little and too much; be consistent in naming, color, letterforms and symbols; keep the structure of the route simple; use mixed upper and lower case for words, since word shape aids reading, with all-capitals reserved for short labels; use clear sans serif letterforms with open spacing; follow arrow conventions (consistent arrow placement and direction metaphors); use maps with "you are here" marks aligned to the viewer's orientation; keep contrast and size appropriate for distance and for people with low vision. A historical chapter discusses landmark programs: Kinneir and Calvert's work on British road and motorway signs from the late 1950s and 1960s (the Transport typeface and tests of mixed case against capitals), Otl Aicher's pictogram and wayshowing system for the 1972 Munich Olympics, and the 1970 New York City Transit Authority Graphics Standards Manual by Massimo Vignelli and Bob Noorda, with its consistent sign grid and colour-coded lines. Details of the examples are summarised here from general knowledge of those projects, not from page-level reading.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The book is a synthesis for practitioners and does not report original experiments; its empirical claims are borrowed from legibility research. The term wayshowing has gained some acceptance but remains a niche term. The 2013 revision expanded the argument that wayshowing differs from wayfinding.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Provide guidance at start, continue and finish for each lesson: an entry cue (what you will do), in-lesson progress, and an exit cue (what's next). (2) Keep colors and icons consistent: one color per course track on every page, as the NYC subway uses one color per line. (3) Use mixed case in navigation labels. (4) Use "you are here" markers in every course map, with the map oriented the way the learner reads it (earlier lessons to the left or top). (5) Choose tools to fit the setting: in fly mode with the sim open, large high-contrast cues serve a viewer at arm's length and glancing. (6) Treat the naming scheme as visual identity too; consistent forms of address help the product feel like one place.

## Pirolli1999
**Core claims.** Pirolli and Card's information foraging theory models people who seek information as adaptive foragers who tend to maximise the rate of valuable information gained per unit of cost (time and effort). It borrows from optimal foraging theory in ecology. Key ideas: information occurs in patches (sites, documents, collections); foragers move between patches and decide how long to stay; the environment offers proximal cues (link text, snippets, icons) that signal the value and cost of distal content, which they called information scent; and users follow scent trails, abandoning them when scent weakens. Bibliographic details were confirmed by search: Psychological Review, volume 106, issue 4, pages 643 to 675, 1999, DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.106.4.643.

**Specifics.** The paper combines a rational analysis with a process model. The analysis expresses the rate of gain as total valuable information gained divided by the sum of between-patch time and within-patch time, and draws on foraging models: the patch model (leave a patch when the within-patch gain rate falls to the average rate for the environment), and the diet model (pursue an item only if its profitability exceeds the average rate achievable otherwise). It then builds ACT-IF, a production-system model on the ACT-R architecture in which spreading activation among words computes scent for links and documents. The paper applies the framework to browsing of a document collection with Scatter/Gather, a clustering interface developed at Xerox PARC, and to how users choose among clusters, estimating costs of reading and of selecting. The authors argue that interface design can be framed as reshaping the information environment: reduce between-patch cost, enrich the patch (better grouping), or improve scent. Exact fit statistics were not rechecked and are not given here.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** Information scent has been widely adopted in web usability practice (Spool et al., Nielsen), and later work by Pirolli (2007 book) extends the theory. Evidence for the quantitative model comes from relatively few studies, and the theory is strongest for goal-directed search; open-ended exploration and learning (Bates, Marchionini) are only partly covered. Popular uses of "scent" are often simplified to "make links descriptive".

**Use for LTF.** (1) Treat each link and button label as a scent cue and write it for the learner's goal: name the benefit and the topic, not only the number. (2) Reduce between-patch cost with one-tap next and previous, no full reloads between steps, and fast loads. (3) Make headings echo the link text that led to them so learners can confirm they are on trail. (4) In fly mode, assume very high switching cost with the sim in front of the learner, so make each patch small, self-contained and quick to leave. (5) Avoid icon-only navigation for new users, as icons give weak scent. (6) Check that lessons end with a strong scent for the next step, since the end of a page is where learners decide to continue or leave.

## Chi2001
**Core claims.** Chi, Pirolli, Chen and Pitkow made information scent computable and used it to infer what users want. Their method, the Inferring User Need by Information Scent (IUNIS) algorithm, takes the pages a user has visited, the text on those pages and the link structure, and estimates the weighted words that describe the user's information need. The idea is that the trail a user follows, like the scent trail in foraging theory, reveals goals the user never typed, and that the same computation can help designers check whether a site's links lead where intended. The CHI 2001 paper title and authors were recalled from memory and not re-confirmed this session.

**Specifics.** Scent is computed through a spreading-activation network over words and pages, using content similarity (term weighting of the TF-IDF kind), hyperlink structure and usage data. Input includes web server logs and page contents. Output is a prediction of the document or words that best describe the need. The authors tested it on sites with known tasks and compared predicted needs with actual goals, reporting that predictions were reasonable. A related system, Bloodhound, simulated users following scent trails through a site to identify usability problems automatically, as presented at CHI 2003. Page ranges and specific accuracy figures are not confirmed and are not given.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** Textual similarity is a crude proxy for human judgments of relevance; images, icons, and context are poorly captured. Evaluation was limited to a few sites and tasks. The line of work was commercially applied as site analysis tools, but the exact adoption is not established here.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Run a scent audit: take real learner goals such as "how do I land without bouncing" and check that the labels along each path share words with the goal. (2) Use learner vocabulary in labels (steering, gas pedal) as well as correct aviation terms, since scent is a word-overlap problem. (3) Log search queries and abandonments in a privacy-respecting way to find weak scent. (4) Test first clicks with novices before launching a navigation change. (5) Keep category pages and lesson titles aligned, so scent is not lost at each level. (6) Remember the limit: these tools cannot judge images or icons, so test them with people.

**Further detail on method and limits.** The reasoning behind the approach is that a user who follows links and reads pages leaves behind a trace of words, and that the pages chosen at each step are the ones whose scent best matched the goal at that moment. The algorithm therefore treats the sequence of choices as evidence and weights recent pages more strongly than early ones, so that goals which change mid-session are tracked. For designers this means two practical outputs: an estimate of what a given user group is really looking for, and a way to find pages where scent is strong but the content does not deliver (a trap) or where content is good but nothing points to it (an orphan). The limit is that the method works on words, so a site built mostly of images, icons and diagrams, as an instrument-heavy flight course will be, yields poor estimates unless captions and alt text carry meaningful text. That gives a second reason for writing descriptive text for every visual.

## Larson1998
**Core claims.** Larson and Czerwinski (Microsoft Research) studied how web page structure affects finding information, with three factors: memory (the burden of remembering where you are in a hierarchy), structure (depth versus breadth) and scent (how well category labels point to the targets). The published abstract, confirmed by search, reports that increased depth harmed search performance on the web, but a medium balance of depth and breadth outperformed the broadest, shallow structure overall. Bibliographic details confirmed: Proceedings of CHI 98, ACM, pages 25 to 32, DOI 10.1145/274644.274649.

**Specifics.** Participants searched for target items in hierarchical web sites in which the total number of items was constant but the arrangement varied, comparing a deep structure (three levels) with two-level structures (one broad at the top, one broad at the second level). Dependent measures included search time and the number of backtracks and errors. The studied factor of scent was manipulated by label ambiguity: some categories had labels under which several targets could plausibly be found. The paper notes that ambiguous labels led participants to wrong branches and to returning to the top. The exact number of items, the sample size and percentage differences are not confirmed this session; an earlier draft quoted a "58 percent" slowdown and about twenty participants, and these are withdrawn as unverified.

**Quotation.** None given; the abstract's gist is paraphrased above.

**Criticism and status.** The dial-up speeds of the period inflated the cost of each page load, which favours shallow structures. Later studies on menus and web links (Kiger, Zaphiris) agree on the direction, shallow over deep, and the "medium beats extreme breadth" result agrees with scanning-cost accounts. Results depend on label quality, so they do not give a universal item count.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Prefer two levels (track, then lesson) over three. (2) Do not flatten a large list into one undivided page; group it. (3) Test labels for ambiguity: any item that could belong to two categories should be cross-listed or the labels revised. (4) Provide an easy way to the top and a persistent location cue to ease the memory burden of position. (5) Treat page-load speed as part of structure, since slow transitions penalise depth. (6) Run first-click tests of the category labels with novices.

**Further detail on interpretation.** The paper's three-part framing is useful on its own. Memory matters because each level of depth requires the user to hold the path in mind and to retrace it after a wrong turn; structure matters because it sets how many decisions are needed and how many options must be scanned per decision; scent matters because even a perfect structure fails when labels do not tell the user which branch holds the target. The authors treat these as interacting, not separate: ambiguity at a high level costs more in a deep structure because the error is discovered late. A design implication that goes beyond the numbers is to put the clearest and most distinct labels at the top level, where a wrong choice is most expensive, and to accept more breadth at lower levels where labels are specific. This is consistent with the foraging account of Pirolli and Card.

## Miller1981
**Core claims.** Miller's 1981 conference paper is cited in the menu design literature as an early empirical comparison of menu depth and breadth in hierarchical computer menus. The argument usually attributed to it is that a hierarchy with a moderate number of choices per level and few levels serves people better than a very deep hierarchy of small menus or one enormous flat menu. Its existence and details could not be confirmed by search this session (the search only returned secondary sources that mention Miller in connection with short-term memory limits on depth), so treat everything below as unverified background and verify before citing.

**Specifics.** What can be said with reasonable confidence, from how later work describes it: the study used a fixed number of target items arranged in hierarchies of different depth and breadth and measured selection time and errors; later reviews place the best arrangement at a small number of levels (two) with moderate breadth. The often-repeated claim that the best breadth was eight items is not confirmed here. Page range and publisher are cleared from sources.json because they could not be confirmed. The title as I recall it, "The depth/breadth tradeoff in hierarchical computer menus", appears in later reference lists, but this session did not confirm it.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** Subsequent studies (Kiger 1984; Larson and Czerwinski 1998; Zaphiris 2000) reached the same direction: shallower generally beats deeper. Models by Lee and MacGregor (1985) and others showed that the best structure depends on the time to read and pick among items and on the time to move between levels, so there is no universal best item count. Miller's name is often wrongly linked to "seven plus or minus two", which is George Miller's 1956 result about memory span and not a menu rule; the two authors are different people and the two findings are different claims. This confusion is a documented misuse (see lane 14).

**Use for LTF.** (1) Do not use "seven items per menu" as a rule; group by meaning and test. (2) Prefer two-level structures for course navigation. (3) Treat each added level as an added chance of error and of getting lost. (4) For controls used while flying the sim, keep depth minimal. (5) If this source is cited in the book, mark it as unverified until its details are checked in a library. (6) Rely on Kiger1984, Larson1998 and Zaphiris2000 for the book's claims, which have at least a confirmed citation.

## Kiger1984
**Core claims.** Kiger's article, in the International Journal of Man-Machine Studies, volume 20, pages 201 to 213 (citation confirmed through a usability report that lists it), examined the depth and breadth trade-off in menu-driven interfaces. A later summary says that follow-up studies, including Kiger's, continued to show that users found resources faster in broad, shallow structures than in narrow, deep ones. The central claim is that, for a fixed number of items, menus with fewer levels and more choices per level are quicker and produce fewer errors than deeper structures, up to a limit.

**Specifics.** The study design, as commonly described, held the number of target items constant and compared hierarchies of different shapes, for example a two-level arrangement against deeper arrangements, with timing and error data. The widely quoted detail that 64 items were arranged as 8 by 8, 4 by 4 by 4 and 2 by 2 by 2 by 2 by 2 by 2 is recalled from secondary descriptions and was not confirmed by search; the numerical outcomes (times, error percentages, sample size) are not confirmed and are not stated. Issue number not confirmed and cleared. The paper discusses why depth is costly: each additional level adds a selection, a response delay and another chance to choose the wrong branch, and the user must understand categories at every level. The paper also reports user preference for broader menus, as described in secondary summaries.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The direction of the result has been replicated many times (Larson and Czerwinski 1998; Zaphiris 2000; Zaphiris, Shneiderman and Norman 2002). It is not a license for very long menus: scanning cost and category ambiguity rise with breadth, which is why a medium structure beat the broadest in Larson and Czerwinski. Early experiments used text-only menus on terminals, with clean categories; real navigation labels are messier.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Use this study to support the choice of a two-level curriculum menu: the course contains lessons directly, not nested sub-modules. (2) Make category membership obvious, because accuracy depends on users understanding the groups. (3) Pair it with the Larson result to avoid over-flattening: group a long lesson list into sections. (4) Keep the number of taps to any lesson low, and measure it (clicks from home to any lesson). (5) Consider that learners may blame themselves for navigation errors, so reduce wrong-branch cost with visible, reversible navigation. (6) Revisit the claims with LTF's own data: instrument first-click success per section.

## Zaphiris2000
**Core claims.** Zaphiris's 2000 paper "Depth vs breadth in the arrangement of web links" (full text found at the Cyprus University of Technology repository; the existence and title were confirmed by search, volume and pages were not) tested how the number of levels and links per page affect navigation of web link hierarchies. The conclusion, consistent with the menu literature, is that shallower structures are faster to navigate. A companion article, Zaphiris, Shneiderman and Norman (2002), in Behaviour and Information Technology, compared expandable indexes with sequential menus across hierarchies two, three and four levels deep, and reported that reducing depth improved speed and search efficiency, while expandable indexes did worse than sequential menus at greater depths.

**Specifics.** The studies varied depth of the web hierarchy while holding the total number of links constant. Measures were time to find a target and error counts. Results (as reported in secondary summaries): time grew as levels increased; the two-level hierarchy was fastest. Age-related work by Zaphiris, Kurniawan and Ellis (2003) found that older users were slowed more by depth, based on a summary in the search results titled "Age differences and the depth-breadth tradeoff". Bernard (2002), in Usability News, examined the shape of hypertext hierarchies and found, in a summary, that concave designs took users less time for browse tasks and led to fewer wasted clicks. Exact timing figures and sample sizes were not confirmed and are not given here.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** Samples were university students and tasks were simple lookups, so generalisation to learning-content navigation is limited. The directional result is consistent across studies, but the optimum depends on label clarity and on the need to scroll long lists. The limits of the comparison: expandable indexes are a different interaction than page-by-page menus.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Do not nest beyond two levels for core content. (2) Expect older adult learners (a large share of simulator hobbyists) to be hit harder by depth, so make flat navigation the default. (3) Use expandable sections only when each contains few items and the expansion preserves context. (4) Consider concave shapes: a broad overview page in the middle with narrow entry and exit paths, for example a phase overview page that lists lessons. (5) Treat these studies as direction, not as thresholds. (6) Measure the real structure with analytics, and do not copy numbers from other sites.

**Further detail on related evidence.** The repository listing found by search also hosts a related paper on age differences in the depth and breadth trade-off, which supports using flat structures for audiences that include older users. Because the repository lists these as separate papers, the book should cite each only for what its title and abstract state, and verify volume and page details before print. The practical reading across this cluster of studies is consistent: reduce levels first, then work on label quality, then on how many links appear per page. A design that follows that order will be on safe ground whatever the exact optimum turns out to be for LTF's content.

## Furnas1987
**Core claims.** Furnas, Landauer, Gomez and Dumais showed that people use many different words for the same thing, so any system that requires users to guess a single "correct" word will fail often. They called this the vocabulary problem. Their remedy was to give each item many access terms ("unlimited aliasing") and to design systems that adapt and learn from users' attempts. The article is in Communications of the ACM, volume 30, issue 11, pages 964 to 971, 1987; this was recalled from memory and the DOI was removed because it was not confirmed.

**Specifics.** The authors ran experiments on how people name things across several domains, including the names people would give to commands for a text editor, categories in a recipe or hobby collection, and common objects. The key result is low agreement: the probability that two people would use the same main term for an object was far below one half, in the range of tens of percent at most, and the most popular name was used by a minority. The exact percentages could not be confirmed this session and are therefore not given. When many aliases were allowed, the proportion of attempts that reached the right item rose substantially. They discuss three design remedies: unlimited aliasing, adaptive indexing (add users' words as new aliases), and interactive systems with feedback. They point out that the diversity of vocabulary is a property of human language and cannot be fixed by training users or by better design of a single term.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The core result has been echoed in later studies of search queries and tagging and is widely accepted. The studies were small by modern standards and used 1980s systems, and exact figures vary by domain.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Beginners will say "steering wheel" for yoke, "gas" for throttle, "up and down" for pitch: put synonyms in search and the glossary. (2) Use the learner's word as the primary label and the aviation term in brackets at first use. (3) Log failed searches and add the words as aliases. (4) Provide several entry paths to one lesson (by phase, by control, by problem). (5) Test labels with novices because experts' words will mislead. (6) Never rely on a single label to carry a feature; supplement with a description and an example.

**Further detail on design consequences.** The authors point out that the usual solution of choosing the best name is mathematically capped, since even the most popular term captures only a minority of people, so no amount of careful naming removes the problem. Their proposed approach shifts effort from the user to the system: accept many terms, learn new terms from use, and let users refine through interaction. For a learning product this suggests a maintained synonym list, reviewed against search logs each release. It also suggests a design stance: when learners fail to find something, treat it as a labeling failure of the product, not a learner error, and respond by adding the learner's word to the vocabulary the product understands.

## Bates1989
**Core claims.** Bates argued that the classic model of information retrieval (a fixed query matched against a fixed set of documents) does not describe real searching. In her berrypicking model the searcher's query evolves as new information is found, the search follows many different routes and sources, and the information need is satisfied by gathering bits ("berries") along the way, not by a single final set. The article, in Online Review, volume 13, issue 5, pages 407 to 424, 1989, also makes the case that online interfaces should support browsing, not just querying. DOI removed as unconfirmed.

**Specifics.** Bates describes four features of berrypicking: the query is not satisfied by a single final retrieved set but by bits collected throughout; the query shifts and changes; searchers use a variety of search strategies; and the information sought comes from a variety of sources. She lists search strategies observed in studies of real searchers, such as footnote chasing, citation searching, journal run, area scanning, subject searching and author searching, and groups search behavior into moves (single actions), tactics, stratagems and strategies. For interface design she recommends supporting area scanning (looking over what is near a found item), following links between items, capture of berries (saving or noting items) and flexible movement between modes. She treats browsing as purposeful rather than aimless. Sample sizes of underlying empirical studies are not stated here.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The model is conceptual and was developed from observations and the literature, not from a controlled experiment. It is best suited to open research needs and less to a defined sequence. It has been very influential in information science and in thinking about interface design for exploration.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Learners will change questions mid-lesson ("how do I trim?" leads to "what is pitch?"); support side trips that return to the lesson. (2) Offer a way to save items or notes for later ("saved for later"). (3) Provide related links at the end of each lesson and glossary links inline. (4) Support both search and browse; do not rely on only one. (5) Make area scanning cheap: show neighbouring lessons on the same page. (6) Preserve state when learners jump away, so returning does not lose progress.

**Further detail on the model's reach.** Bates contrasts berrypicking with the traditional model by drawing the path of a real search as a winding line with many small gains rather than a straight line to a single answer. She argues that the system's retrieval of a best match at each step is still useful, but that the interface must also let users step sideways and see neighbours, since the neighbour often becomes the next query. She also notes that terms and approaches change as the searcher learns, so a system that locks in the first query misrepresents the need. In a course, the equivalent is that a learner's most important question is often not the one the lesson page was written to answer, so the page needs doors to nearby questions.

## Marchionini2006
**Core claims.** Marchionini argued that search systems built around a query box and a ranked list serve lookup tasks well but fail people who are trying to learn about a topic or investigate it. He divided search activities into three overlapping kinds: lookup (fact retrieval, known-item search, navigation, verification), learn (knowledge acquisition, comprehension, comparison, interpretation, aggregation) and investigate (analysis, synthesis, evaluation, discovery, planning, forecasting). Exploratory search covers the learn and investigate kinds and often includes lookup as a sub-step. The article appeared in Communications of the ACM, volume 49, issue 4, pages 41 to 46, 2006 (recalled from memory; the DOI was removed as unconfirmed).

**Specifics.** Exploratory search is characterised as open-ended, multi-faceted, iterative and often persistent across sessions; the searcher may be unfamiliar with the domain, unsure of the goal, or unsure how to reach it. Such searchers need support for query formulation, for scanning and comparing results, for making sense of what they find, and for tracking progress. The article's recommendations include overviews and previews of the information space, facets and filters, visual and interactive summaries, support for collaboration and for saving and annotating, and evaluation measures that reflect learning outcomes (not only precision and recall). He ties search and learning together: searching to learn is itself a learning activity, so interface design for search is partly instructional design. Details such as specific systems reviewed are not recalled with confidence and are omitted.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The article is an agenda-setting framework with few new data. Exploratory search evaluation is still difficult; a later edited volume (White and Roth 2009) collects methods. The categories overlap in practice, and the claim that standard search fails exploratory users is accepted but depends on the system.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Beginners lack the vocabulary needed to formulate queries, so browsing, overviews and previews matter more than a search box. (2) Offer previews on hover or tap (what's in this lesson, how long it takes, what you will fly). (3) Provide facets (phase of flight, controls, difficulty) as filters on the lesson list. (4) Distinguish lookup uses (checklists and V-speed tables during flight) from learning uses (lessons) in the interface and optimise each separately: lookup wants speed and predictability; learning wants scaffolding and context. (5) Treat navigation as part of the lesson: a good "what you will learn" preview is instruction. (6) Evaluate navigation by learning outcome, not only by task time.

## Rosenfeld1998
**Core claims.** Rosenfeld and Morville's book, first published by O'Reilly in 1998 as Information Architecture for the World Wide Web and extended with Arango in later editions (4th edition 2015, Information Architecture: For the Web and Beyond), defined the practice of information architecture (IA) for digital environments. IA is the structural design of shared information environments, the combination of organisation, labeling, search and navigation systems, and the craft of making content findable and understandable. The authors present IA as the balance of three forces: users, content and context.

**Specifics.** Organization schemes are divided into exact schemes (alphabetical, chronological, geographical), which suit known-item lookup and are easy to maintain, and ambiguous schemes (by topic, task, audience, metaphor), which suit browsing and need more design effort. Structures include hierarchy, database-driven structure and hypertext. Navigation systems are split into global (site-wide), local, contextual (in-content links) and supplemental (site maps, indexes, guides), with embedded navigation distinguished from remote navigation tools. Labeling systems include contextual links, headings, navigation labels and index terms, with controlled vocabularies and thesauri for consistent terms. Search guidance covers when a site needs search and how to design results pages. Research methods include stakeholder interviews, content inventories and analysis, user interviews, card sorting and usability testing. A core process distinction is top-down (starting from business goals and user needs) and bottom-up (starting from content). Later editions add ideas of cross-channel consistency and information ecologies. Page numbers are not given because editions differ.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The book is a practitioner synthesis; guidelines are experience-based and not tested as laws. The mix of ambiguous schemes can become hard to maintain. Resmini and Rosati (2011, Pervasive Information Architecture) argue that web-centric IA must be extended to cross-device and physical contexts, and Hinton (2014, Understanding Context) develops the idea of context as a design problem.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Pick one primary organisation scheme for the curriculum, a task or flight-phase scheme, and use it consistently. (2) Provide global navigation (course, checklists, progress), local navigation (within a phase) and contextual links (in-lesson glossary and cross-references), each with a different job. (3) Maintain a controlled vocabulary of lesson and concept labels, with aliases. (4) Make a content inventory of existing lessons before redesigning navigation. (5) Use exact schemes for reference material (alphabetical glossary, V-speeds) and ambiguous schemes for learning paths. (6) Consider cross-device consistency, since learners use the app on a second screen or phone beside the sim.

## Spencer2010
**Core claims.** Spencer's A Practical Guide to Information Architecture (Five Simple Steps, 2010) gives a process that small teams can follow to design site structure: understand the content and the users, create the structure by grouping and labeling, test it, and document it. Spencer also wrote Card Sorting: Designing Usable Categories (Rosenfeld Media, 2009), the main practitioner text on the method. Publication years and publisher were recalled from memory and not confirmed this session.

**Specifics.** Card sorting methods: open sorts (participants group cards and name the groups), closed sorts (participants place cards into predefined categories) and hybrids. Cards carry short plain labels of content items, commonly in the tens rather than hundreds. Analysis techniques include looking at common groupings, similarity matrices and cluster analysis, with human judgment applied to the results. The usual guidance on participants is that a modest sample, roughly 15 to 30 for open sorts, gives stable patterns, attributed to Tullis and Wood (2004, "How many users are enough for a card-sorting study?"); their reported correlation figures could not be confirmed this session and are not given. Tree testing (testing a text-only hierarchy with find-it tasks) is the usual follow-up for checking the structure. Spencer's practical advice includes using the audience's own words for labels, allowing multiple paths to the same content, and documenting the structure as a sitemap and a labeling guide.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** Card sorting exposes users' mental groupings but not task flows or the effect of context, and different people sort differently, so the results are input to judgment, not a result to be averaged blindly. Sort-based structures can reflect the labels used on the cards (a bias towards surface wording). Tree testing and first-click testing are complementary methods. The methods are widely used but their predictive validity for final site success is not established by controlled trials.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Run an open card sort of the lesson and challenge titles with about a dozen to two dozen novices (not pilots) to learn how beginners group topics. (2) Run a closed sort or tree test with the proposed structure before building. (3) Write labels in learners' words and validate with a first-click test. (4) Keep a living sitemap and labeling guide shared by content and design. (5) Re-test after adding new modules, because structure drifts as content grows. (6) Record the methods and sample sizes in the project's decision log, so later changes can cite evidence.

## Wurman1989
**Core claims.** Wurman's Information Anxiety (Doubleday, 1989) argues that the gap between data and understanding causes anxiety, and that making information understandable is a design task. He coined the label "information architect" for those who organise information so that its patterns are clear, and he proposed that there are only five ways to organise information, abbreviated LATCH: Location, Alphabet, Time, Category and Hierarchy. The book is essayistic and visual, drawing on his work in architecture, cartography and publishing.

**Specifics.** Location organises by physical or spatial position, as in maps and floor plans. Alphabet organises by the order of letters, useful when users know the name of what they seek, as in dictionaries and directories. Time organises by sequence of occurrence, as in schedules, timelines and histories. Category organises by similarity or relatedness, as in departments or genres. Hierarchy organises by magnitude or rank on an ordered scale, such as largest to smallest or best to worst. The same set of facts can be organised in more than one of these ways, each revealing different patterns; the choice should follow the question the user wants answered. Wurman also discusses how maps, schedules and diagrams reduce anxiety by showing relationships, and he stresses beginning from the reader's need. Page-level quotes and examples are not given because they were not rechecked.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The claim that there are only five ways is rhetorical; others add number, sequence or topic as separate organising principles, and Rosenfeld and Morville treat topic and task as categories of ambiguous schemes. No controlled studies test LATCH. Its influence is as a teaching device for thinking about organisation, and as the origin of the phrase "information architect".

**Use for LTF.** (1) A flight is time ordered, so organise the core lessons by Time (preflight, taxi, takeoff, climb, cruise, descent, landing); this matches learners' mental model of a flight. (2) Reference material fits Alphabet (glossary, abbreviations) and Location (airports, charts). (3) Difficulty ladders fit Hierarchy. (4) Use Category for concept groups (instruments, controls, aerodynamics). (5) Keep each page or list to one organising principle so the pattern is visible. (6) When a list is hard to scan, ask which LATCH principle the learner's question implies and reorder accordingly.

**Further detail on how to apply it.** Wurman's practical suggestion is to test an organisation against a plausible question: if the user asks when, use Time; where, Location; what is it called, Alphabet; what kind, Category; how much or how good, Hierarchy. This question-to-scheme mapping is more useful than the list itself, because it ties structure to tasks. For a flight course the questions are strikingly time-ordered (what do I do next, what comes after this), which explains why checklists and lesson sequences feel natural when they follow the order of the flight. Mixed schemes inside one list, such as an alphabetical list with a few items out of order for priority, confuse because the reader cannot tell which pattern to trust.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** The wayfinding sources (Lynch, Tolman, Passini, Arthur, Mollerup) agree that people orient by building a mental representation from cues, and that the designer's job is to make that representation easy to build: a few distinct structural elements, cues placed at decision points, consistent names and symbols, and confirmation after correct choices. The digital sources (Pirolli and Card, Chi, Larson, Rosenfeld, Spencer) transfer the same ideas: scent cues at links, two or so levels, global plus local plus contextual navigation, and testing labels with real users. The menu research (Kiger, Zaphiris, and Miller if confirmed) agrees on direction: shallower beats deeper.

**Where they conflict or qualify.** Maximum breadth is not best (Larson and Czerwinski found a medium structure beat the broadest), and the "seven items" rule is a misreading of a memory finding. Foraging theory assumes a goal-directed forager, while Bates and Marchionini stress goals that shift as people learn. Furnas shows that search fails when vocabularies differ, which is why beginners lean on browsing. Wurman's five categories are tidy but untested. Several quoted figures could not be confirmed this session (Larson percentages, Kiger and Miller conditions, Tullis and Wood correlations, sign letter-height ratios) and have been removed.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Keep core navigation to two levels (course section, then lesson); avoid a third level.
2. Write every link as a scent cue in the learner's words, naming the benefit.
3. Put the next step at the decision point (end of lesson), not only in menus.
4. Give each phase of flight a stable district identity (name, color, icon) that persists across pages and themes.
5. Always show where you are and how far you have come (step x of n), and confirm correct actions.
6. Use one name per concept everywhere, with aliases for search and glossary.
7. Provide a whole-course map for survey knowledge as well as the linear path.
8. Support side trips and return (related links, saved items, preserved state).
9. Set structure with card sorting and tree testing on novices, and check labels with first-click tests.
10. In fly mode, minimise switching cost: small self-contained pages, shallow navigation, large glanceable cues.
