## Berlyne1971

**Core claims.** Daniel Berlyne's *Aesthetics and Psychobiology* (1971) is the book that rebuilt experimental aesthetics on the psychology of arousal, motivation and exploration. Its central proposal is that the hedonic value of a stimulus (how much it is liked) is an inverted-U function of its "arousal potential". A stimulus that produces too little arousal is dull and a stimulus that produces too much is aversive, so pleasure peaks at a moderate level. Berlyne revived the older idea of the Wundt curve and tied it to the physiology of the reticular activation system and to reward and aversion systems in the brain. Aesthetic pleasure is therefore not a special faculty. It comes from the same machinery that governs curiosity, play and exploration.

**Specifics.** Arousal potential is driven by three groups of properties. Psychophysical properties are intensity, color saturation, pitch and similar. Ecological properties are the learned signal value of a stimulus (a siren, a face). Most important for design are the collative properties, which Berlyne named because they require comparing a stimulus with other stimuli or with expectations: novelty, complexity, uncertainty, conflict, surprisingness, incongruity and ambiguity. Berlyne distinguished "diversive exploration", the search for stimulation to escape boredom, from "specific exploration", the search for information to resolve a particular uncertainty. He measured liking with paired comparisons, ratings of pleasingness and interestingness, looking time and choice of what to view, using polygons, random patterns, paintings and musical stimuli. He also proposed two mechanisms of reward: arousal boost, where moderate rises in arousal are pleasant, and arousal jag, where a rise followed by a rapid drop (relief) is pleasant, which is his account of jokes, puzzles and suspense that resolves. Repetition lowers arousal through habituation, so a stimulus slides left along the curve with exposure, which links his theory to Zajonc's mere exposure. He also separated "interestingness" from "pleasingness", noting that complexity tends to raise interest more steadily than pleasure.

**Quotations.** None used. I did not confirm exact wording from the book this session.

**Criticism and status.** The inverted-U is one of the most cited ideas in empirical aesthetics, but support is mixed. Many experiments find it for complexity with simple stimuli. Others find monotonic increases or decreases, or find that the peak shifts with expertise, culture, familiarity and mood. Later work by Colin Martindale argued that prototypicality predicts liking better than arousal. Reviews also note that "complexity" is ambiguous (objective element count versus perceived complexity) and that arousal was rarely measured physiologically in the original tests. For web design, Tuch et al. (2012) and Reinecke et al. (2013) found a mostly monotonic penalty for visual complexity at first glance, which is not what the inverted-U would predict at the low end, so the curve should be treated as a heuristic about balance, not as a law with a known peak. The bibliographic details were confirmed by search; the finer points above come from standard summaries of the book.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Aim for moderate arousal per screen. One focal element, restrained secondary detail, and generous space keep a nervous beginner on the comfortable side of the curve, while an empty template feels dull.
2. Pace novelty. A distinctive visual device repeated on every card habituates and becomes wallpaper, so save the most intense visual moments for earned events such as a first completed lesson or a first landing.
3. Design "arousal jag" payoffs. A challenge result that builds a short moment of suspense and then resolves clearly feels better than an instant score.
4. Treat uncertainty as a tool, not a flaw: teaser questions before an explanation raise specific exploration, which suits instruction.
5. Keep the fly-mode screen near the low-arousal end, because the learner is already highly aroused by the simulator and the aircraft.

## Zajonc1968

**Core claims.** Robert Zajonc's 1968 monograph, "Attitudinal effects of mere exposure", argued that repeated, unreinforced exposure to a stimulus is enough to increase liking for it. Familiarity alone, with no reward and no information, is a sufficient condition for a more positive attitude. He proposed this as a general principle of attitude formation and as a challenge to learning theories that required reinforcement.

**Specifics.** The monograph combined archival and experimental evidence. The archival part showed that how frequently words occur in a language correlates with how positively people rate their meaning, across several languages, for words such as pairs of antonyms where the more frequent member tends to be the favorable one. The experimental part presented unfamiliar items such as nonsense words, Turkish-like words and Chinese-like characters, and photographs of faces, with different participants seeing items 0, 1, 2, 5, 10 or 25 times, then rating how positive they judged the item's meaning or their liking for it. Liking rose with the logarithm of exposure frequency, with most of the gain in the first few exposures. Zajonc also reported that a stimulus rated as more positive was recognized or liked more even without clear memory, which later grew into the claim that the effect can occur without conscious recognition (subliminal exposure studies by Kunst-Wilson and Zajonc 1980). The bibliographic details (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology Monograph Supplement, vol. 9, no. 2, part 2, pp. 1 to 27, June 1968) were confirmed by search. Exact correlation coefficients for the word-frequency analyses were not confirmed this session and are not quoted here.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The effect has been replicated hundreds of times. Bornstein's 1989 meta-analysis of about 200 experiments (as widely cited; the exact count was not rechecked) found a small to moderate overall effect, larger for brief and subliminal exposures, larger for unfamiliar and simple stimuli, and weaker for complex stimuli at first, with a wear-out or reversal when exposure is very frequent or very long. Critics argue that part of the effect reflects the lower uncertainty or higher perceptual fluency that comes with familiarity, which makes it a special case of processing fluency (see Reber2004). It does not guarantee liking for stimuli that are initially disliked, where repetition can make attitudes more negative.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Consistency compounds. A fixed visual vocabulary for instruments, checklists, lesson cards and buttons becomes more liked each session, so avoid redesigning component styles lesson by lesson.
2. A signature typeface, palette and mark gain affection through exposure, which is how identity is built, so decide them early and hold them steady.
3. Match the look of simulator instruments closely, because learners will see both the app and the sim many times and familiarity transfers.
4. Vary content, not chrome. The exposure effect rewards repeating the frame while new material appears inside it.
5. Watch wear-out. Identical congratulation messages or celebratory animations repeated every few minutes will turn from pleasant to irritating.

## Reber2004

**Core claims.** Reber, Schwarz and Winkielman proposed the processing fluency theory of aesthetic pleasure. Aesthetic pleasure is a function of the perceiver's processing dynamics: the more fluently a perceiver can process an object, the more positive is their aesthetic response. Beauty is therefore not simply located in the object, nor purely in the viewer, but in the perceiver's experience of processing, which is partly a function of stimulus properties. The article was published in *Personality and Social Psychology Review* 8(4), 364 to 382, and the details were confirmed by search.

**Specifics.** The authors review evidence that stimulus properties known to increase liking also increase ease of processing. Predictors of fluency are figure-ground contrast, symmetry, clarity (visual and semantic), prototypicality (averageness) and repetition or priming. Experiments they cover include Reber, Winkielman and Schwarz (1998) showing that objects presented with higher contrast, longer duration or primed by matching preceding images were rated more attractive. They describe Winkielman's work on averaged faces and dot patterns, where prototypes were preferred, and work on facial electromyography showing greater activity over the zygomaticus (smile) muscle for fluent stimuli, which they take as evidence that fluency is hedonically marked as positive affect. A central point is misattribution: people feel positive affect when processing is easy and attribute it to the object, which is why pleasure can arise from priming even when the prime is not noticed. They also link fluency to the mere exposure effect, to the beauty of the average, to the preference for symmetry and to judgments such as truth and familiarity.

**Quotations.** The abstract's central claim is that aesthetic pleasure is "a function of the perceiver's processing dynamics" (paraphrased from the abstract; exact phrasing not rechecked).

**Criticism and status.** The fluency account has strong support but also limits. Fluency is not the only route to liking, since novel, complex and challenging works can be loved because processing them is rewarding in itself (Berlyne, Leder). Some commentary distinguishes aesthetic pleasure from aesthetic interest. Predicting that prototypical designs always win is contested, because originality adds value (Hekkert et al. 2003). Fluency effects are generally modest in size, and they can be reduced or reversed when people know the source of the feeling. The effect on judgments of truth makes it ethically sensitive. In the web literature the account fits the finding that low complexity and high prototypicality produce good first impressions (Tuch et al. 2012). DOI was removed because it was not confirmed this session.

**Use for LTF.**
1. High text contrast and clear figure-ground in instrument widgets are a direct fluency gain.
2. Use symmetry and alignment for structure, which are cheap and measurable sources of fluency.
3. Keep interaction patterns conventional so the app is easy to read and therefore pleasant, while reserving distinctiveness for color, type, illustration and voice.
4. Write in plain language. Fluent text also feels more credible and more likable.
5. Do not use fluency to make weak or unverified aviation facts feel true. State sources and verification dates.

## Hekkert2003

**Core claims.** Hekkert, Snelders and van Wieringen (2003) tested the principle Raymond Loewy called MAYA, "most advanced, yet acceptable", in his 1951 book *Never Leave Well Enough Alone*. Their claim is that aesthetic preference for consumer products is predicted jointly by typicality (how representative the product is of its category) and novelty. The most preferred designs combine both. The bibliographic details (British Journal of Psychology 94, 111 to 124) were confirmed.

**Specifics.** In Study 1, participants rated product designs from several categories (including items such as telephones and tea kettles; the exact full category list was not rechecked) on typicality, novelty and preference. Typicality and novelty were strongly negatively correlated, meaning more novel products were rated less typical, and neither one alone correlated significantly with preference. Entered together in a regression, they explained about 56 percent of the variance in aesthetic preference, which shows that the optimum is a combination. In Study 2, 22 participants (11 experts and 11 non-experts) were compared, and expertise did not change the relative contributions of typicality and novelty, even though experts judged novelty differently. The authors also interpret results in terms of exposure and the idea that people prefer what is new within the bounds of what is acceptable. This work gave Loewy's practical rule an empirical basis and became a core reference for Hekkert's later "design aesthetics" framework with four principles of pleasure (the MAYA principle, unity in variety, maximum effect for minimum means, and others in his 2006 essay; the 2006 essay was not separately verified here).

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The finding has been echoed in later studies of product, web and architectural stimuli, which usually find that typicality and novelty both contribute positively once their negative correlation is handled. The limits are real. Stimulus sets were small and tied to specific product categories, so the "optimum" is a combination in that category and culture, not a fixed ratio. The strong negative correlation between typicality and novelty makes it hard to separate their effects statistically. Some later studies find that expertise changes weights, and that preference for novelty rises over time as a category's norms shift, which means "acceptable" moves. DOI removed as not confirmed.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Make structure typical (navigation, lesson page anatomy, form controls) and make the surface novel (illustration, type, color, motion), so the app is both easy and memorable.
2. Borrow the real shapes and layout of Cessna 172 instruments for widgets (typical for the domain), then render them with a distinctive but legible style.
3. Limit novelty to one or two signature devices so the total does not tip into unacceptable.
4. When evaluating a proposed look, rate typicality and novelty separately with a few test users instead of asking only "do you like it".
5. Revisit the balance as the product matures, since familiarity shifts what counts as typical.

## Leder2004

**Core claims.** Leder, Belke, Oeberst and Augustin (2004) proposed an information-processing model of aesthetic appreciation and judgment, built mainly for art but widely applied to design. A perceiver passes through five stages: perceptual analyses, implicit memory integration, explicit classification, cognitive mastering and evaluation. The output has two parts, an aesthetic judgment (cognitive) and an aesthetic emotion (affective), which can diverge. Input includes the situation (art context versus everyday), the perceiver's expertise and their affective state. Details confirmed by search: British Journal of Psychology 95(4), 489 to 508.

**Specifics.** Stage 1 covers low-level features such as complexity, contrast, symmetry, order and grouping, processed quickly. Stage 2 integrates with implicit memory: familiarity, prototypicality and fluency, which are processed without conscious effort. Stage 3 is explicit classification, where the viewer identifies content and style and brings in knowledge. Stage 4 is cognitive mastering, an effortful effort to make sense of the work (interpretation, elaboration), and success here yields a pleasing sense of understanding, which is why art knowledge changes liking. Stage 5 evaluates whether the work was successfully mastered, which feeds back into emotion. The model predicts that different features act at different times, and so Tuch et al. (2012) used it to predict that visual complexity (stage 1) would affect very brief impressions more strongly than prototypicality (stage 2), which their 17 ms data supported. The model also explains why novices and experts differ and why ambiguity can be a source of delight when it can be resolved.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The model is among the most cited in empirical aesthetics, and Leder and Nadal (2014) reviewed ten years of tests in the British Journal of Psychology (vol. 105, pp. 443 to 464). They kept the broad stage structure but noted that emotion and context needed more weight, and Leder (2013) argued the 2004 version underestimated the role of emotional processes. It was developed for artworks and is applied to interfaces by extension, with fewer direct tests. The stages are not strictly serial in neuroimaging data.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Expect two clocks: a fast reaction to clutter, contrast and order in the first moment, and a slower one in which meaning and familiarity count.
2. Give novices help with classification. A short caption saying what an instrument is and why it matters supports "cognitive mastering", and the sense of understanding is itself pleasing.
3. Returning pilots and first-timers bring different expertise, so allow density to rise as learners progress.
4. Account for affective state. A learner who is anxious about a first lesson judges the interface more harshly, so first-run screens should be calm and warm.
5. Use ambiguity deliberately in challenges only when it can be resolved within the session, since unresolved ambiguity reads as confusion.

## Tractinsky2000

**Core claims.** Tractinsky, Katz and Ikar (2000), "What is beautiful is usable", gave HCI its best known statement of the aesthetic-usability effect: users' perceptions of how attractive an interface is correlate strongly with their perceptions of how usable it is, and attractiveness can shape judgments of usability even when actual usability does not differ. The paper was a deliberate follow-up to Kurosu and Kashimura (1995), who reported a similar relation with Japanese ATM layouts and called it "apparent usability". Tractinsky wanted to see whether this held in a different culture, since Japanese design tradition might plausibly make the link stronger. The paper is in Interacting with Computers 13(2), 127 to 145, and the DOI 10.1016/S0953-5438(00)00031-X was confirmed in search results.

**Specifics.** The authors used a computer simulation of an ATM and varied the layout's aesthetics, selected from layouts that had been pre-rated, and also varied actual usability (for example, in how efficiently tasks could be done). Participants rated aesthetics and usability before use and again after completing tasks. Pre-use, perceived aesthetics and perceived usability correlated strongly. The central result from the abstract is that the pre-experimental correlation "remained intact" after use, and a multivariate analysis of covariance showed that the system's aesthetics affected post-use perceptions of both aesthetics and usability, whereas the manipulated degree of actual usability had no such effect on those perceptions. The exact number of participants and the correlations were not confirmed this session and are not quoted. The authors also note a point of terminology: some indexes list the second author as Shoval-Katz, but the paper lists A. S. Katz.

**Quotations.** None used beyond the abstract paraphrase above.

**Criticism and status.** The effect on perceived usability has been replicated in many studies, and it is part of the common argument that beauty buys tolerance and goodwill. But the strong form, that aesthetics beats actual usability, has not held up uniformly. Tuch et al. (2012) found that when usability was strongly manipulated and aesthetics only moderately, usability changed perceived beauty and aesthetics did not change perceived usability. Hassenzahl (2004) found that beauty and perceived usability are different constructs with different relations to goodness. The studies measure perception and rating, not task performance, and most use short sessions with student samples on a single type of interface. A sensible reading is that aesthetics is a strong first-impression and tolerance factor but does not repair a broken task flow.

**Use for LTF.**
1. A calm, well-crafted look earns patience in the first minutes, when a beginner is most likely to give up, so invest in the first-run experience's appearance.
2. Do not use appearance as a substitute for working flows. In fly mode, where the learner is divided between the app and the simulator, actual usability must be tested with task measures, not only ratings.
3. Collect both perceived and measured usability (time on task, errors) when testing, because the two can come apart.
4. Expect beauty to raise ratings of the content's quality too, which raises the duty to verify aviation facts rather than rely on polish.
5. Avoid claiming "users find beautiful interfaces easier to use" as a design justification without noting that it concerns perception.

## Tuch2012Beautiful

**Core claims.** Tuch, Roth, Hornbaek, Opwis and Bargas-Avila (2012), "Is beautiful really usable?", tested the relations among usability, aesthetics and affect in a controlled experiment and found that the aesthetic-usability effect can be reversed. Pre-existing aesthetics did not change perceived usability in their design, whereas usability did change post-use perceived aesthetics, and affect mediated that path. The paper is in Computers in Human Behavior 28(5), 1596 to 1607 (confirmed). Author lists vary across indexes; some leave out Bargas-Avila.

**Specifics.** A laboratory experiment with 80 participants used four versions of an online shop, created by crossing two levels of aesthetics with two levels of usability. Participants completed tasks (finding specific items) and rated the shop before and after use with validated instruments for usability, aesthetics, and affect (positive and negative). The key results from the abstract and summaries: aesthetics did not affect perceived usability, while usability affected post-use perceived aesthetics. The effect of usability on aesthetics was mediated by the user's affective response, in that the frustration of poor usability lowered aesthetic ratings. The authors say that the "what is beautiful is usable" notion can be reversed under certain conditions, namely a strong manipulation of usability combined with a medium-to-large manipulation of aesthetics. The Google Research blog post by the authors summarizes the work for a general audience. Other exact statistics were not confirmed and are not quoted here.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The result does not overturn the earlier work but narrows it: the direction of influence depends on the relative strength of the manipulations, on how aesthetics is measured, and on whether people actually perform tasks (as opposed to rating static screens). It is one site type (a shop), a lab sample and one set of stimuli, so it has been interpreted as a call for more context-aware models such as Thüring and Mahlke's CUE model rather than as a proof that beauty is irrelevant. Replications in other domains are limited. It agrees with the broader finding that affect is an important mediator between product qualities and evaluations.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Frustration in a task flow degrades how beautiful the learner finds the whole product, so a lesson that confuses someone will make a good interface look worse.
2. Fix flows first, then add polish. Visual refinement will not rescue a challenge screen whose instructions are unclear.
3. Test affect during tasks, with a quick frustration or confidence rating after each challenge, not only a post-hoc aesthetic rating.
4. Use performance data (completion, errors, time) alongside ratings, because ratings alone confound beauty and ease.
5. Prioritize low-frustration paths in first sessions: nothing should reset progress, hide the next step, or demand the learner remember sim details they were never taught.

## Lavie2004

**Core claims.** Lavie and Tractinsky (2004), "Assessing dimensions of perceived visual aesthetics of web sites", showed that perceived web aesthetics has two main dimensions, which they called classical aesthetics and expressive aesthetics, and built a scale to measure them. The article is in the International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 60(3), 269 to 298, with DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2003.09.002 (confirmed).

**Specifics.** Classical aesthetics refers to notions of beauty from antiquity through the 18th century: orderly and clear design, closely related to many design guidelines advocated by usability experts. Expressive aesthetics refers to designers' creativity and originality and the ability to break design conventions. In the final instrument, secondary sources list the classical items as clean, clear, pleasant, symmetrical and aesthetic, and the expressive items as original, sophisticated, fascinating, creative and using special effects (item lists reported in secondary summaries and in the paper's follow-ups, not read in full here). The authors ran four studies, using exploratory and then confirmatory factor analysis with users rating web pages, and found a stable two-factor structure with good reliability. Classical aesthetics related more strongly to perceived usability, and expressive aesthetics to originality and pleasure of use. Later work (Thielsch, Moshagen and others) adapted the two factors for questionnaires and found them useful for comparing sites.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** Moshagen and Thielsch (2010) criticized the instrument: items such as "symmetrical" and "uses special effects" are not necessarily aesthetic judgments, and a two-factor structure misses facets like colorfulness and craftsmanship. Still, the classical/expressive distinction is a durable way to think about two kinds of visual quality, and it echoes older distinctions in art theory. Samples were mostly student and not cross-cultural, and the items were English-language. Later cross-cultural work (Reinecke and Gajos 2014) shows that preferences differ by country, age and education.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Plan explicitly for both dimensions. Classical (clean, clear, ordered) builds trust and calm, and expressive (original, creative, fascinating) gives identity. A site with only the first is often what people call generic.
2. The owner's complaint that the current design is interchangeable reads, in this framework, as high classical and near-zero expressive aesthetics.
3. Choose where expression lives: illustration, type voice, color and motion at key moments, with structure staying classical.
4. Use the ten items as a quick survey in design reviews to compare variants, noting their limits.
5. Test expressive choices for harm to clarity: "special effects" can lower classical scores and distract during fly mode.

## Moshagen2010

**Core claims.** Moshagen and Thielsch (2010), "Facets of visual aesthetics", developed the VisAWI (Visual Aesthetics of Websites Inventory) to measure perceived visual aesthetics with more than the classical and expressive factors. Across seven studies they identified and validated four interrelated facets that together represent perceived visual aesthetics yet remain distinguishable and carry unique meaning. The paper is in the International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 68(10), 689 to 709, DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2010.05.006 (confirmed).

**Specifics.** The four facets, as given in the VisAWI literature, are simplicity (order, clarity, structure), diversity (variety, dynamics, creativity, novelty), colorfulness (the choice, combination and attractiveness of colors), and craftsmanship (the skillful and coherent integration of the relevant design dimensions). Only the craftsmanship definition was quoted in the search results this session; the other three facet names are standard in descriptions of the instrument but were not independently confirmed in this session's search results. The instrument has 18 items on a 7-point rating scale, and a four-item short form (VisAWI-S) followed in 2013 in Behaviour & Information Technology, as listed on the official VisAWI site. The authors report good internal consistency for the subscales and evidence for convergent, divergent, discriminative and concurrent validity. A later study by Seckler and Tuch reported a strong positive influence of bilateral symmetry on VisAWI categories. Exact reliability values, sample sizes and correlations were not confirmed and are not given.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** VisAWI is widely used and has been validated in several languages, and it is the closest thing to a standard instrument for web aesthetics, but it measures perceived aesthetics only. It does not tell you whether a design performs, and it was developed largely with German-speaking and student samples. Because it is a rating of overall impression, high scores can come from conventional designs, so it should be paired with an originality measure if distinctiveness is a goal.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use craftsmanship as an explicit design target: alignment, consistent spacing, aligned baselines, consistent corner radii and icon weights are what raters pick up as skill.
2. Diversity is the facet most relevant to the "generic" problem. Variety within a system (different lesson illustrations, a considered rhythm of dense and airy screens) raises it.
3. Keep colorfulness controlled and purposeful, which fits the two-theme (Day and Night) system.
4. Run VisAWI-S before and after redesigns as a cheap benchmark, with 4 items and no heavy infrastructure.
5. Report facets separately: a design can score high on simplicity and low on diversity, which is exactly what a generic template does.

## Hassenzahl2004

**Core claims.** Marc Hassenzahl (2004), "The interplay of beauty, goodness, and usability in interactive products", separates the pragmatic and hedonic qualities of products and shows that beauty and goodness (overall satisfaction or appeal) are related but different. The article is in Human-Computer Interaction 19(4), 319 to 349, DOI 10.1207/s15327051hci1904_2 (confirmed).

**Specifics.** Two studies used four skins of a software MP3 player. Participants rated perceived usability (pragmatic quality), hedonic quality (with subscales for stimulation, the product's capacity to provide novelty and challenge, and identification, its capacity to communicate identity to others), goodness and beauty. Findings: beauty and goodness are related when both reflect subjective evaluation, but their nature differs. Beauty is rather self-oriented than goal-oriented; goodness depends on both perceived usability and hedonic attributes. Perceived usability and goodness were affected by experience with the product, while hedonic attributes and beauty remained stable over time. This supports the claim that the beauty of a product is judged quickly and stays stable, whereas its practical merit depends on use. A 2008 follow-up in Interacting with Computers partly tested and extended the model for web sites and found that beauty was influenced by hedonic attributes, goodness by hedonic and pragmatic attributes plus task performance and mental effort. The model underlies the AttrakDiff questionnaire and Hassenzahl and Tractinsky's (2006) user experience agenda.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The pragmatic/hedonic distinction has been adopted widely, but critics note it can be too simple: "hedonic" bundles quite different things (novelty, identity, aesthetic pleasure), and the original analyses rest on skins of a single type of application with limited sample sizes. Subsequent work (Tuch and colleagues, Thüring and Mahlke) adds emotion as a mediator and context as a moderator. The finding that beauty is stable while usability perceptions shift with experience is useful, but it was shown for a short study period, not for months of use.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Design for identification. Learners should feel the app helps them become a pilot, which implies voice, imagery and rituals (a pre-flight ritual, a logbook) beyond pure task support.
2. Design for stimulation. Progress, discovery and small challenges supply the novelty that stops the app feeling like a manual.
3. Because beauty is stable and appeal to the self is separate from task fit, a strong first look keeps paying off while usability perceptions improve with experience.
4. Measure pragmatic and hedonic quality separately (AttrakDiff or a short custom set), so a redesign that improves one at the cost of the other is visible.
5. Do not expect goodness to follow from beauty. A beautiful lesson with a confusing task will score high on beauty and low on goodness.

## Lindgaard2006

**Core claims.** Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek and Brown (2006), "Attention web designers: You have 50 milliseconds to make a good first impression!", reported that people form a stable judgment of a web page's visual appeal within 50 ms, and that this fast judgment agrees with judgments made after longer viewing. The authors suggest this early emotional response acts as a halo that colors later judgments of other characteristics, including usability and credibility. It appeared in Behaviour & Information Technology 25(2), 115 to 126, DOI 10.1080/01449290500330448 (confirmed).

**Specifics.** Three studies were run. In the first, participants rated the visual appeal of web homepages shown for 500 ms each, twice, and ratings were highly consistent across the two rounds. In the second study, participants again rated homepages after 500 ms and also rated them on seven specific design dimensions, and visual appeal turned out to be closely related to most of these. In the third study, pages were shown for either 500 ms or 50 ms, and the ratings across the two conditions agreed closely, which led to the headline claim. Secondary sources describe the correlation as high, and some quote very high values, but the exact coefficient is NOT confirmed: a search for the commonly repeated figure of about .95 returned no source that states it, so do not cite any numerical correlation from this paper without reading the full text. Participant numbers per study were also not confirmed this session. The pages were chosen to span a range of appeal, which matters for interpreting the agreement: when pages differ clearly in quality, rating agreement is easier to obtain than when pages are similar. A Nature news item in January 2006 reported the study to the public.

**Quotations.** The title's phrase "you have 50 milliseconds" is the only wording quoted.

**Criticism and status.** The result was extended and replicated by Tuch et al. (2012) at 17 ms, 33 ms and 50 ms, and by Reinecke et al. (2013) at 500 ms with 450 sites. Important limits: the paper measures agreement of ratings across exposure durations, not whether people leave a site, not conversion, and not credibility itself. Popular summaries claiming that "users decide in 50 ms whether to stay" go beyond the data. Pages were rated in a lab with no task. Judgments of appeal are partly learned (prototypicality needs memory), which the later Tuch work addresses. Treat the paper as evidence that the visual gestalt is evaluated before content, not as a threshold for bounce.

**Use for LTF.**
1. The first screen, with no reading, must communicate calm, competence and identity through layout, color, type and imagery alone.
2. Test the home, lesson and dashboard screens with 50 to 500 ms flash tests and ask for a one-item appeal rating, comparing variants.
3. Because first impressions carry a halo into judgments of credibility and usability, a polished first impression is worth more in an aviation teaching product, where trust is crucial.
4. Do not repeat the "50 ms" slogan as proof of lost users. Use it only to justify attention to the first glance.
5. Make hierarchy readable in a blurred or thumbnail view, since early appraisal is based on global structure.

## Tuch2012Complexity

**Core claims.** Tuch, Presslaber, Stoecklin, Opwis and Bargas-Avila (2012) studied how visual complexity (VC) and prototypicality (PT) shape first impressions of websites. Websites that are low in complexity and high in prototypicality are judged most appealing, and the effects appear after as little as 17 ms. The paper is in the International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 70(11), 794 to 811, DOI 10.1016/j.ijhcs.2012.06.003 (confirmed).

**Specifics.** In Study 1, 119 screenshots of real websites (as reported in the search record) varied systematically in visual complexity (low, medium and high) and prototypicality (low and high), and participants rated their perceived aesthetics. Study 2 shortened presentation time to 17, 33 and 50 ms. Both VC and PT affected ratings even at 17 ms, but the effect of PT was less pronounced than that of VC at the shortest durations. With increasing presentation time the effect of PT became as influential as the VC effect. The best-rated websites were those low in VC and high in PT. The authors relate the pattern to Leder et al. (2004), in which complexity is processed at an earlier stage than prototypicality, so complexity can drive impressions before familiarity does. The authors' own blog post, "Users love simple and familiar designs", gives a plain-language summary. Specific effect sizes and the numbers of participants in each study were not confirmed this session and are not quoted.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The finding agrees with Reinecke et al. (2013) and with the fluency account (Reber 2004). The caveats are that stimuli were screenshots of existing mostly Western sites, so "prototypical" means "like those sites", which can build conservatism into recommendations. A low-complexity, high-prototypicality design is also the template-like look the owner complains about, so this paper is evidence for a tension: the safest first-impression design is the generic one. The paper itself does not claim that unique designs fail, only that they must pay for novelty with extra processing cost.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Reduce visual complexity first. Fewer elements, fewer type styles, fewer colors on each screen improves first impressions most quickly.
2. Keep structure prototypical (header, content column, familiar controls), and put distinctiveness into elements that do not increase complexity: a single bold typeface, a strong palette, one signature illustration style.
3. Prefer distinctiveness through craft and restraint over added ornament, since added ornament raises complexity and lowers early appeal.
4. For dashboards and fly mode, use progressive disclosure so the first view is low in complexity while detail is reachable.
5. When testing alternatives, rate complexity and familiarity separately, so you know which of the two moved the score.

## Reinecke2013

**Core claims.** Reinecke and colleagues (CHI 2013) built computational models that predict first-impression appeal of web pages from image-derived measures of visual complexity and colorfulness. The models, with demographic variables, explain about half the variance in appeal ratings given after 500 ms. Complexity matters more than colorfulness. The authors motivate the work by noting that the first impression later affects opinions of a site's usability and trustworthiness. Pages 2049 to 2058 and the author list were confirmed; the DOI was not confirmed and has been removed.

**Specifics.** An online study collected ratings of colorfulness, visual complexity and visual appeal for 450 websites from 548 volunteers. Appeal was rated after a 500 ms exposure, while complexity and colorfulness were judged separately. The team then trained models on computed image features: for complexity, measures such as the amount of edges, the number and spread of image regions and similar structural statistics, and for colorfulness, statistics on hue, saturation and color distribution (the precise feature list was not confirmed here). Results: the two models plus age and education explained approximately half of the variance in appeal ratings. The result that colorfulness "plays a role, but is not nearly as important as the overall visual complexity" appears in the authors' summary. A follow-up by Reinecke and Gajos (CHI 2014), "Quantifying visual preferences around the world", used ratings from almost 40,000 participants for 430 website screenshots and found that preferences differ by demographic factors such as age, education and country, so there is no single universal preference.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The models predict ratings at 500 ms, not behavior, and the sites came from a specific time (around the early 2010s), so contemporary flat or dark design could shift values. Because the dataset is of existing sites, it describes the market's design vocabulary, which means high-appeal means "typical of what was online then". Complexity here is perceived complexity as measured, and the model's feature set is simpler than human perception. It nevertheless supports the actionable result that a design can be automatically screened.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Run key screens through a simple complexity estimate (edge density or a rating panel) to catch clutter before user tests.
2. Spend color budget deliberately. Moderate, structured color is a smaller lever than reducing visual complexity.
3. Check Day and Night themes separately, since complexity and colorfulness differ across them.
4. Segment preference testing by age, because adult learners include older beginners who show different preferences than young users.
5. Do not infer that low complexity is always best. The paper predicts appeal at 500 ms for a browsing sample, not the preference of a motivated learner mid-lesson.

## Fogg2003

**Core claims.** B. J. Fogg's "Prominence-interpretation theory" (CHI 2003 Extended Abstracts, pp. 722 to 723, DOI 10.1145/765891.765951, confirmed) proposes that people's assessment of web credibility has two steps. First they must notice something, which is its prominence. Then they make a judgment about what they noticed, which is the interpretation. An element affects credibility only if both occur: a feature that is not noticed has no effect, and a feature that is noticed has an effect whose direction depends on how it is interpreted.

**Specifics.** The theory states that overall credibility impact is a function of prominence times interpretation. As summarized by the Nielsen Norman Group and the Stanford project, five factors influence prominence: the user's involvement (motivation and ability to scrutinize), the topic of the site, the task the user is performing, the user's experience, and individual differences. Interpretation, which can be positive or negative (an ad can raise or lower credibility), is influenced by the user's assumptions (culture, past experience, heuristics), skill and knowledge in the domain, and context (setting and norms). The theory is meant to explain why different studies find different top credibility factors: they sample different users, tasks and sites. It was a conceptual paper, two pages long, building on the Stanford Web Credibility Project, which also issued a set of guidelines (for example, make it easy to verify accuracy, show there is a real organization behind the site, show the honest and trustworthy people, make it easy to contact, design to look professional, keep the site current, and avoid errors of every type). The guidelines come from the Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab and were not independently verified here beyond their existence on credibility.stanford.edu.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** Reported as useful and widely cited, but little tested directly. A 2016 AIS paper noted that the interpretation component had hardly been studied. It is a framework to organize findings, not a predictive model with parameters. The data come from self-report and from Western, English-speaking users, and credibility online has changed since 2003 (social proof, platform reputation).

**Use for LTF.**
1. For trust signals to count they must be prominent where the learner looks: a "last verified in the sim, version X" badge beside a procedure beats one in the footer.
2. Anticipate interpretation. A sponsor or ad block can read as commercial and lower credibility, so keep the teaching surface free of promotional clutter.
3. Learners in a first lesson are low in domain knowledge, so they use surface cues to infer credibility. That raises the duty to make real verification visible.
4. Errors, typos and broken layouts are interpreted as carelessness, which in aviation content is damaging.
5. Test prominence with a "what did you notice" question after a short view, and interpretation with "what does that tell you".

## Fogg2003DUX

**Core claims.** Fogg and colleagues (Stanford Persuasive Technology Lab), "How do users evaluate the credibility of Web sites? A study with over 2,500 participants" (DUX 2003), is the largest coded study of what people say affects web credibility. Its headline finding is that the visual design of a site was the feature mentioned most often, ahead of content and structure. The ACM DOI 10.1145/997078.997097 was confirmed. Author spelling: the author list includes Julianne Stanford. The page range was not confirmed.

**Specifics.** A total of 2,684 people evaluated the credibility of two live Web sites on a similar topic (such as health sites) in an online study, and gave comments on what influenced their judgment. Comments were coded into categories of site features. The ACM abstract states that "design look" was mentioned most frequently, in 46.1 percent of the comments (confirmed in search). The next most common categories were information structure and information focus; roughly 28 percent and 25 percent are the figures I recall, but they were NOT confirmed in this session and should be checked at the source before quoting. The study complements the earlier CHI 2001 survey (Fogg et al., "What makes Web sites credible?") of more than 1,400 participants, which also found that expert-looking features and a real-world organization raise credibility, while commercial implications and poor design lower it (figures for that study not confirmed). The authors considered that consumers were judging a site primarily by its surface, with a skew toward appearance, and also noted that for sites with a higher expert content (such as health) the look was important but information structure was also frequently cited.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The findings rest on comments, which is a measure of what people say and notice, not what actually drives their choices or how accurate their judgments are. The Fogg team's own prominence-interpretation theory explains that comments about design are prominent because they are salient at first glance. Others (Sillence et al. 2004) argue design is an early filter and content decides later trust. Participants were largely English-speaking web users in 2002 and 2003. Credibility is perceived and not the same as being credible, so a polished misleading site can score well, which is an ethical risk, not a recommendation.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Visual quality is the most reported credibility cue in the largest coded sample, so treat professional visual polish as a requirement, not a luxury.
2. Make information structure obvious: titled sections, scannable procedures, and a clear lesson map, which was the second most commented feature.
3. Back the polish with substance, by showing sources and verification status next to facts.
4. Use the study's categories as a checklist when reviewing the learner-facing site.
5. Never use this to justify disguising weak content with pretty design. Actual verification practice is a v1 project rule for good reasons.

## Sillence2004

**Core claims.** Sillence, Briggs, Fishwick and Harris (2004), "Trust and mistrust of online health sites" (CHI 2004, pp. 663 to 670, DOI 10.1145/985692.985776, all confirmed), propose a staged model of trust in websites. Rapid rejection or mistrust is driven by design factors, and acceptance or trust is driven by content factors such as source credibility and personalization. So design acts as a gatekeeper and content as the decider.

**Specifics.** The authors observed fifteen women over four consecutive weeks who were searching the internet for information and advice about a risky health decision. In some sessions the search was unstructured, in others the women were directed to review specific sites chosen for their trust-related design elements. Findings (from the abstract): "design appeal predicted rejection (mistrust)", and credibility of information and personalization of content predicted selection (trust) of advice sites. The staged idea was extended in a WWW 2005 paper on hypertension sites, whose content analysis of group discussion supported a model in which mistrust or rejection of sites is based on design factors (such as poor navigation, too many pop-ups, a commercial feel, or unprofessional look) and trust or selection is based on content factors like source credibility and sensitivity to the user's own situation. I looked for a widely quoted statistic that a large share (a figure around 94 percent) of rejection decisions were due to design: it is NOT confirmed. None of the retrieved sources contained it, and the sample was only fifteen participants, so it should not be cited from this paper.

**Quotations.** None used beyond the short abstract wording above.

**Criticism and status.** The sample is small, female and British, in a single domain (health), and qualitative, so the model is a hypothesis-generating one. Its logic is consistent with Fogg's data on "design look" and with the "amelioration" effect in Robins and Holmes, but its proportion claims should not be generalized. Later work by Sillence and Briggs on trust in health websites elaborated the model across more participants and it has been widely cited in medical informatics.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Pass the design gate first. A learner who is wary will leave a site that looks amateurish before ever reading its content.
2. Then win on content. Visible source credibility (the manual, the FAA handbook, the date checked) and personalization (progress, a plan that adapts) are the stage-two trust builders.
3. Avoid pop-ups, ad-like banners and commercial clutter on teaching pages, which the participants cited as reasons to reject sites.
4. Personalize with real data: "you finished lesson 3 on Tuesday" signals attention to the learner.
5. Treat the health setting as an analogue: both domains involve risk, expert content, and anxious novices.

## Robins2008

**Core claims.** Robins and Holmes (2008), "Aesthetics and credibility in web site design", show that for identical content, a more aesthetic visual treatment increases perceived credibility. They call this the "amelioration effect" of visual design. The paper is in Information Processing & Management 44(1), 386 to 399 (confirmed by dblp). The DOI was not confirmed and has been removed.

**Specifics.** The authors, from Kent State University, took web sites with identical content and created pairs differing only in visual design treatment (a higher versus a lower aesthetic treatment), then had participants rate credibility after short exposure. A later review reports that an aesthetic treatment increased the rating for the same content in 19 of 21 cases, about 90 percent (reported by a secondary review; the exact count was not confirmed in the original text this session). A 2014 paper also describes the study as comparing 20 web sites with identical content. They link the effect to earlier work on credibility (Fogg) and the halo effect, and note that content was seen as less credible when the design was weaker even though the words were the same. They also discuss that users with little time judge by look, since design is processed first. Exact participant numbers, exposure times and statistics were not confirmed and are not quoted.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The result is consistent with Fogg's DUX finding and Sillence's design gate, and the within-content manipulation is a cleaner design than comment studies. Limits: the effect is on perceived credibility, not accuracy, and was tested with short exposures and student-style samples. A later brief review in PMC on credibility judgments in web page design discusses similar results and gaps. It also raises an ethical concern: polish can inflate credibility of poor content, so designers carry a duty not to mislead.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Good typography and spacing will raise the perceived credibility of procedures and checklists even with unchanged text, so polish the instructional surfaces.
2. Because polish raises credibility without raising accuracy, pair it with visible verification (sim version, last verified date, source), which keeps trust warranted.
3. Treat weak visual treatment as a credibility tax: the same checklist in a cramped, inconsistent layout will be trusted less.
4. Apply the same standard to all lesson types so some do not look "less authoritative".
5. Run an A/B style pair test with identical copy to measure the effect for your own audience.

## Norman2004

**Core claims.** Donald Norman's *Emotional Design: Why We Love (or Hate) Everyday Things* (Basic Books, 2004) argues that emotion is an essential part of how designs work, not a decoration. He proposes three levels of processing: visceral (immediate, automatic response to appearance, sound, feel), behavioral (the pleasure and effectiveness of use) and reflective (interpretation, meaning, self-image and memory). His slogan, from his earlier essay, is that attractive things work better, because positive affect makes people more tolerant of minor problems and more flexible in thinking. Bibliographic details are from memory (not re-verified this session, hence verified marked as memory).

**Specifics.** Norman draws on the work of Alice Isen: positive affect induced by a small gift (a bag of candy) or a short comedy film improved performance on creative problem-solving tasks (Isen, Daubman and Nowicki 1987, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 52(6), 1122 to 1131; confirmed by search). He argues that positive affect broadens thinking (good for creative and exploratory tasks), while anxiety and fear narrow focus (useful for dealing with a threat, but poor for open problem-solving). So the design of a stressful task should reduce unnecessary stress, but a safety-critical procedure should be clear and stripped down. He uses his three teapots (in the book and the essay) to show that a product can be strong at different levels. He links the three levels to design disciplines: visceral to appearance, behavioral to usability and function, and reflective to brand, identity and story. He also describes the idea that designs that trigger affection (a person's attachment to a long-used object) have a lasting value, and discusses robots and machines with emotions, which has dated.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The three-level scheme is a useful framework but not an empirically tested theory, and the evidence for "attractive things work better" is mixed: Tuch et al. (2012) find that usability changes aesthetics as well as the reverse. Isen's mood effects have faced questions about replicability in later research, with small effect sizes and context dependence. Norman's book is often cited in design practice as a source for the idea of designing for emotion, which Aarron Walter (2011) turned into a practical approach for web interfaces. The emphasis on delight should not displace basic usability.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Plan each of the three levels on purpose. Visceral: palette, type and illustration in Day and Night. Behavioral: reliable, forgiving lesson and challenge flows. Reflective: the story of becoming a pilot, logbook and milestones.
2. Use calm, positive tone and small moments of delight in learning screens, where flexible thinking helps.
3. Keep emergency and checklist content stripped, direct and low in decoration, since narrowed attention under stress needs clarity.
4. Build reflective value: let learners keep something (a logbook, certificates of milestones) that supports identity.
5. Remember that emotion follows from experience. A frustrating flow cancels a pretty surface.

## Lane synthesis

**Where the sources agree.** Appraisal happens fast and begins with global structure. Lindgaard (2006) and Tuch (2012) show stable appeal judgments at 50 ms and even 17 ms, driven first by visual complexity and then by prototypicality (Leder 2004 explains the order). The fluency account (Reber 2004) and mere exposure (Zajonc 1968) explain why simple, familiar, well-aligned designs feel good, and Reinecke (2013) shows a large share of first-impression appeal is predictable from complexity. Appearance leaks into other judgments: Tractinsky (2000) for usability, and Fogg (2003 DUX), Sillence (2004) and Robins (2008) for credibility. Hassenzahl (2004) and Norman (2004) say experience has a pragmatic and a hedonic side, and that identity and stimulation matter alongside ease.

**Where they conflict.** Berlyne's inverted-U predicts a complexity optimum, but web studies mostly find that lower complexity is better at first glance. Prototypicality is liked (Reber, Tuch) yet Hekkert shows novelty adds preference, which sets up the central tension for a project that wants a distinct identity without losing ease. Tractinsky says beauty raises perceived usability, while Tuch (2012) finds usability can change perceived beauty through frustration. Fogg's data stress look, while Sillence says design only gates and content decides.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Win the first glance with low visual complexity and a clear hierarchy, and test with 50 to 500 ms flashes.
2. Keep structure conventional and put identity in type, color, illustration and voice (MAYA).
3. Aim for moderate arousal, pace intense moments, and avoid repeating the same delight until it wears out.
4. Invest in craftsmanship (alignment, spacing, consistency), the facet raters read as skill.
5. Add diversity inside a consistent system to avoid the generic feel, and plan for both classical and expressive aesthetics.
6. Fix task flows first. Frustration lowers perceived beauty, and beauty does not repair broken usability.
7. Make credibility visible: sources, verification date, sim version, real people, no ad-like clutter, no typos.
8. Never let polish outrun substance, since polish inflates perceived credibility.
9. Design for identification and stimulation as well as ease, using logbook, milestones and a pilot's voice.
10. Measure with short instruments (VisAWI-S, AttrakDiff, a frustration rating) and report facets separately.

Caveats carried from verification: the exact Lindgaard 50 ms to 500 ms correlation, the 28 and 25 percent Fogg DUX figures, and the VisAWI facet names beyond craftsmanship were not confirmed this session, and DOIs not confirmed were removed.
