## Treisman1980
**Core claims.** Treisman and Gelade proposed feature integration theory (FIT). Simple features (color, orientation, size, brightness, line ends and similar) are registered automatically, in parallel, in separate feature maps across the whole visual field. Binding features into one object (a red vertical bar, not just redness plus verticality) needs focal attention applied to one location at a time, acting like a spotlight over a master map of locations. Without attention, features can be wrongly combined, which they called illusory conjunctions. The theory predicts two search signatures. Feature search, where the target differs from distractors by one feature, is "preattentive": reaction time barely changes as the number of items grows. Conjunction search, where the target is defined only by a combination of features that distractors share, requires serial attention and reaction time grows steadily with display size.

**Specifics.** The 1980 paper reports a series of experiments in which observers searched displays of varying numbers of colored letters and reported target presence or absence as fast as possible. Display sizes were small to moderate (a few items up to roughly thirty). A target defined by a single feature (for example a blue letter among brown letters, or an S among Ts and Xs distinguished by shape) gave nearly flat functions of reaction time against display size. A conjunction target (for example a green T among green Xs and brown Ts) gave steep, approximately linear functions, on the order of a few tens of milliseconds per added item, and the slope for target-absent trials was about twice the slope for target-present trials. That 2:1 ratio is the signature of a serial self-terminating search: on average the observer checks half the items before finding a present target, but must check all items to decide a target is absent. The exact slopes are not confirmed here. Further experiments showed that texture regions defined by a single-feature boundary segregate effortlessly, while regions defined by feature conjunctions do not, and that when attention is diverted or displays are brief, observers report illusory conjunctions such as a red X when a red O and a blue X were present (Treisman and Schmidt 1982). Treisman and Souther (1985) added search asymmetries: finding a circle with a line among plain circles is easy, but finding a plain circle among circles with lines is slow, interpreted as the presence of a feature being detectable and absence not.

**Criticism and status.** The basic phenomenon, that single feature differences pop out and conjunctions are slower, is among the most replicated findings in perception. The strict two-category claim did not survive. Duncan and Humphreys (1989) showed that search efficiency varies continuously with target-distractor similarity and distractor-distractor similarity. Wolfe, Cave and Franzel (1989) proposed Guided Search, in which feature maps guide, rather than merely precede, serial attention, so that some conjunction searches are efficient. Wolfe has since argued that search slopes form a continuum from near zero to over 100 ms per item and that "preattentive" is a poor label. Treisman herself revised FIT in later papers, adding feature-based inhibition and object files.

**Use for LTF.**
- A single status that must be noticed (the current checklist step, a failed challenge criterion) should differ from its neighbors by one clear feature such as fill color or weight, not by a conjunction.
- Do not make items findable by a combination (green and bold and with an icon); learners will scan one by one.
- A grid of similar lesson cards is a serial search. Give categories a distinct single feature so groups can be skipped.
- Absence is hard to detect. Show completed steps with a mark, instead of expecting learners to notice a missing item.
- Reserve one accent feature for actionable things, because when many items carry the accent none pops out.

## Wolfe2017Five
**Core claims.** Wolfe and Horowitz (2017, Nature Human Behaviour) review what steers attention during visual search and argue that five factors combine in an attentional priority map. (1) Bottom-up salience: items that differ enough from their surroundings, along an attribute that can guide attention, attract it. (2) Top-down feature guidance: attention is directed to objects with the known features of the desired target. (3) Scene guidance: scene gist and structure direct attention to places where the target is likely. (4) Search history: priming and learning, over timescales from milliseconds (the previous trial) to years (experience). (5) Value: reward associated with targets and distractors modulates priority. A theory of real-world search must specify how these combine. Wolfe and Horowitz also note that search matters in practical settings such as airport baggage screening and radiology.

**Specifics.** This is a review, so numbers come from cited studies, not new data. The companion 2004 Nature Reviews Neuroscience paper by the same authors (Wolfe and Horowitz, "What attributes guide the deployment of visual attention and how do they do it?") sorted attributes by strength of evidence: color, motion, orientation and size are undoubted guiding attributes; luminance onset, luminance polarity, vernier offset, stereoscopic depth and some shape properties (curvature, closure, terminators) are probable or possible; faces and meaningful categories are weak or doubtful as guiding features. Salience is relative: a red item among varied colors is far less salient than among uniform gray items, and its strength depends on the ratio of the target-distractor difference to the distractor variability. Top-down guidance is imperfect, since items resembling the target distract. Scene guidance means a target is found sooner where expected (a toilet in a bathroom). Search history includes priming of pop-out, in which repeating the target feature speeds responses over a few trials, and contextual cueing (Chun and Jiang 1998), where repeated layouts speed search without awareness. Value effects include attentional capture by formerly rewarded colors even when irrelevant (Anderson, Laurent and Yantis 2011). Exact weights of the five factors are not given.

**Criticism and status.** The component findings are well replicated individually, but the authors acknowledge that no model yet specifies how the factors combine. Most evidence uses sparse laboratory arrays or photographs; web pages and applications are rarely studied. The 2004 attribute list is itself revised over time: for example, Wolfe has noted that features like curvature may be guided by more basic properties.

**Use for LTF.**
- Design with all five factors on purpose: salience (what pops), top-down (what the learner is searching for), scene (where things live), history (consistency across sessions), value (what has meant reward or danger).
- Keep recurring elements (Next, progress, checklist) in constant places so scene and history guidance work for learners.
- Signal with the reliable guiding attributes (color, size, motion, orientation) and avoid ornament that competes.
- Use the words the lesson uses in labels so a learner's mental template matches the label.
- Reward cues such as streaks capture attention, so place them away from the flight-critical content.

## Yarbus1967
**Core claims.** Yarbus recorded eye movements with a suction cap carrying a small mirror and showed that the pattern of fixations depends strongly on what the observer is trying to do, not only on the picture. His best known demonstration is Repin's painting "An Unexpected Visitor" viewed by the same person under different instructions: free viewing, estimate the family's material circumstances, give the ages of the people, say what the family had been doing before the visitor arrived, remember the clothes, remember the positions of people and objects, and estimate how long the visitor had been away from the family. The traces of fixations and saccades differ visibly across these tasks.

**Specifics.** Yarbus concluded that observers fixate mainly on informative regions (faces, eyes, hands, objects relevant to the question) and spend little time on plain background. In pictures of faces, the eyes, nose and mouth gather the bulk of fixations. The recordings for each instruction in the famous figure lasted about three minutes (approximate), and the well-known figure is based on one observer. Yarbus also showed that observers return repeatedly to the same elements, so that longer viewing adds fixations to already-viewed informative elements rather than spreading uniformly. The book also covers the stabilized retinal image, where an image fixed on the retina fades within seconds, and the structure of saccades and fixation durations, and it reported that fixation patterns reflect what the observer is thinking about. The English edition (Plenum, 1967, translated by Basil Haigh) is a translation of the Russian original from 1965.

**Criticism and status.** The sample was tiny and the analysis qualitative. Greene, Liu and Wolfe (2012, Vision Research) tried to decode Yarbus's task from simple fixation statistics and failed, calling the claim into question. Later work showed task decoding is possible with better classifiers (Borji and Itti 2014, "Defending Yarbus", Journal of Vision; Haji-Abolhassani and Clark 2014; Henderson, Shinkareva, Wang, Luke and Olejarczyk 2013 in PLoS ONE), though effects are modest and image salience also contributes. DeAngelus and Pelz (2009) replicated the broad pattern with more observers. Tatler, Wade, Kwan, Findlay and Velichkovsky (2010, i-Perception) give the historical context. The accurate summary is that gaze reflects both task and salience.

**Use for LTF.**
- The same screen is looked at differently by someone reading, someone seeking the next action and someone checking a value mid-flight, so design for each task's gaze path.
- In fly mode the learner glances between simulator and page for a specific fact; place that fact where the glance lands.
- Faces and eyes attract fixations. An instructor illustration whose gaze points at the relevant instrument can steer attention.
- Informative regions get the fixations, so put essential facts in the content, not decoration.
- Test designs with defined tasks, not with free viewing or "what do you think of it".

## Itti1998
**Core claims.** Itti, Koch and Niebur built a computational model of bottom-up attention, following the neuronal architecture proposed by Koch and Ullman (1985). The image is decomposed into feature maps for intensity, color opponency and orientation at several spatial scales. Center-surround differences (a fine-scale center minus a coarser-scale surround) compute local contrast. The maps are normalized and summed into a single saliency map. A winner-take-all network picks the most salient location, and inhibition of return then suppresses it so that attention moves to the next most salient location, producing a sequence of attended spots.

**Specifics.** Intensity comes from the average of the red, green and blue channels. Color maps use red-green and blue-yellow opponent channels (the model uses broadly tuned color channels). Orientation maps come from Gabor filters at four orientations (0, 45, 90 and 135 degrees). The model builds a Gaussian pyramid with several scales and computes center-surround differences across scales, giving a set of maps per feature (six per feature in the original, with intensity 6, color 12 and orientation 24 feature maps, for 42 in total, as commonly described). A normalization operator is the key design idea: it amplifies maps with a few strong peaks and suppresses maps with many comparable peaks, which matches the observation that a single odd item pops out while many similar odd items do not. The model reproduced pop-out in single-feature search (the number of distractors did not change time to find the target), it needed more attention shifts for conjunction displays, and it found targets such as traffic signs, colored cones and red soda cans in natural images faster than chance. Itti and Koch (2000, Vision Research) and Itti and Koch (2001, Nature Reviews Neuroscience) extended and reviewed the approach. Parkhurst, Law and Niebur (2002) found that salience predicts fixations best at the first few fixations and decays over viewing time.

**Criticism and status.** Saliency models predict free-viewing fixations above chance but modestly, and perform poorly when observers have a task (Henderson, Brockmole, Castelhano and Mack 2007; Foulsham and Underwood 2008). Faces, text and objects attract gaze beyond their low-level contrast (Cerf et al. 2008), and observers favor the center of images (central bias, Tatler 2007). Torralba et al. (2006) added contextual guidance from scene gist. Deep-learning models since 2014 (DeepGaze and its successors) outperform the original by large margins on benchmarks such as the MIT saliency benchmark. Treat the model as a baseline for "what stands out physically", not for "what will be looked at".

**Use for LTF.**
- Run mockups through a saliency model, or squint-blur them, to check whether the most salient spot is the most important element.
- Make one element stand out by quieting the rest, because normalization means many comparable peaks cancel each other.
- The accent color should differ from the neutral surface in color opponency and in luminance, in both Day and Night themes, or pop-out will vanish in one theme.
- Inhibition of return means repeated flashing in the same place is ignored fast, so do not rely on pulsing.
- Animated or high-contrast decorations near text steal attention from the lesson; avoid them.

## Posner1980
**Core claims.** Posner described attention as an internal orienting mechanism, a "spotlight" that can be moved to a location and that speeds processing there, independently of eye movements (covert attention). He distinguished endogenous orienting, which is voluntary and driven by a symbolic cue such as a central arrow, from exogenous orienting, which is reflexive and captured by a sudden peripheral event. Orienting involves disengaging from the current focus, moving, and engaging at the new target, each stage with measurable costs.

**Specifics.** In the cueing paradigm the participant fixates a central point and responds to a target (a brief light or letter) appearing left or right. A cue precedes the target: a valid cue indicates the correct side, an invalid cue the wrong side, a neutral cue gives no information. Responses on valid trials are faster than neutral trials (a benefit), and responses on invalid trials are slower (a cost). Typical effects are a few tens of milliseconds each, which is small but very reliable. In the classic design central cues are valid about 80 percent of the time, so people learn to trust them. Exogenous peripheral cues act quickly, with benefits within roughly 100 ms even when uninformative, and later reverse into inhibition of return after about 300 ms (Posner and Cohen 1984), so that a location recently attended is slower to be attended again. Endogenous cues need more time, on the order of 300 ms or more, to take full effect. Jonides (1981) contrasted voluntary and automatic orienting and Yantis and Jonides (1984) showed abrupt onsets capture attention. Folk, Remington and Johnston (1992) showed that capture by onsets depends on the observer's top-down set, which qualifies purely stimulus-driven capture, and Theeuwes (1992) argued for salience-based capture. Posner and Petersen (1990) proposed separate alerting, orienting and executive networks.

**Criticism and status.** The cueing effects are robust and replicated for decades. Limits: tiny stimuli on blank screens, milliseconds-scale effects, and the spotlight metaphor was replaced or refined by the zoom lens (Eriksen and St. James 1986), object-based attention (Egly, Driver and Rafal 1994) and feature-based attention. The relation between covert attention and eye movements (premotor theory, Rizzolatti et al. 1987) remains debated. Whether exogenous capture is truly automatic is contested (Folk et al. versus Theeuwes).

**Use for LTF.**
- A reliable cue before the target helps: a highlight or arrow that points to the next element before content changes speeds finding it. An unreliable cue costs time and trust.
- Peripheral flashes, toasts and badge pulses capture attention reflexively and then are inhibited, so reserve them for events that deserve capture.
- Make cues consistently valid. If a highlight sometimes points at the wrong thing, learners stop using it.
- Voluntary shifts take hundreds of milliseconds, so do not remove a hint instantly.
- Disengaging is costly, so place the next control near where attention just was.

## Simons1999
**Core claims.** Observers who count basketball passes in a video often fail to notice an unexpected event, such as a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene. Fully visible objects can go unseen when attention is engaged elsewhere (sustained inattentional blindness for dynamic events). The paper also shows that noticing depends on similarity between the unexpected object and the attended items, and on the difficulty of the primary task.

**Specifics.** Observers watched videos of two teams of three players, one team in white shirts and one in black, moving around and passing basketballs. They counted the passes made by one team. After about a minute, an unexpected event occurred: a woman carrying an umbrella or a person in a gorilla suit crossed the scene for roughly 5 seconds (approximate; durations not re-confirmed). The experiments crossed several factors: the attended team (white or black), task difficulty (counting total passes, versus counting bounce passes and aerial passes separately), and whether the unexpected figure was opaque or transparent (a superimposed ghostly image). About half of the observers failed to notice the unexpected event overall; the exact percentage and sample size are not confirmed here. Noticing was higher when the unexpected object resembled the attended team (a white-clad object when watching white team), lower when it resembled the ignored team, lower under the harder counting task, and transparent figures were missed more than opaque figures, in line with Neisser and Becklen's (1975) earlier "selective looking" study on superimposed transparent games. Afterward, many observers who missed it said they would have noticed such an event. Simons and Chabris (2010) reported that most of the public hold that belief.

**Criticism and status.** The effect is robust. Drew, Vo and Wolfe (2013, Psychological Science) reported that the large majority of expert radiologists (about 20 of 24 is the commonly quoted figure, not re-confirmed) missed a gorilla image inserted in lung CT scans, even though eye tracking showed that many looked at its location. Critics distinguish inattentional blindness from inattentional amnesia (Wolfe 1999): the object may be seen but not remembered. The frequency of such misses in real life and in interfaces is not known, and rates vary widely with task and stimulus.

**Use for LTF.**
- When learners concentrate on a primary task such as holding altitude in the simulator, an unexpected message on the page may go unseen, so critical information must not depend on peripheral noticing.
- Messages that look like their surroundings (gray text, styled like content) are missed, while dissimilar ones are noticed more often.
- Do not trust "it's right there" in your own review; test with people engaged in tasks.
- Do not assume a state change was seen unless it was salient and tied to the learner's action.
- A counting-passes demo could teach inattention itself, which matches the scan discipline taught in flight instruction.

## Rensink1997
**Core claims.** Large changes in a scene are often missed if they coincide with a brief disruption that masks the local motion signal. Seeing a change needs attention at the changing item. Without a transient to attract attention, people have to search for the change item by item, and so detection can take surprisingly long (change blindness).

**Specifics.** In the flicker paradigm an original image (A) alternates with a modified image (A') with a blank gray field of about 80 ms between them, each image being shown for about 240 ms (approximate, as commonly described), repeating until the observer reports the change. Changes were to real-world scenes: an object appearing or disappearing, changing color, or moving. Observers often needed many cycles, on the order of several seconds, and sometimes much longer, to find changes that were obvious once spotted. Changes to items rated as of central interest were found faster than changes to marginal-interest items, which suggests that attention goes first to meaningful items. When the blank was removed, the change created a visible transient that attracted attention, and detection was immediate. Rensink interpreted these results in terms of a sparse representation in which only attended objects are represented in detail. Related work: Simons and Levin (1998) found that about half of pedestrians failed to notice that the person asking them for directions had been swapped during a brief interruption (a door carried between them), and O'Regan, Rensink and Clark (1999, Nature) used "mudsplashes" to show that a distraction elsewhere in the display is enough to cause the same effect. Exact detection times from the paper are not confirmed here.

**Criticism and status.** Replicated by many labs. Hollingworth and Henderson (2002) argued that more visual information is retained than the pure sparse view implies, and Simons and Rensink (2005) reviewed the area, noting that "change blindness" can reflect failure to compare, not failure to store. Real interfaces rarely have a full-field blank, but many updates (route changes, loading flashes, list re-renders) have a similar masking effect on small changes.

**Use for LTF.**
- A page update that replaces content during a route change or loading flash can hide what changed. Mark the changed element (a brief highlight or short animation on that element).
- Keep motion transients local. A full-screen transition removes the cue that would direct attention to the change.
- If a checklist item flips state while attention is elsewhere, the learner will not know, so confirm the change near their focus.
- Avoid silently replacing content under the learner's cursor or gaze.
- Persistent layouts help comparison before and after an update.

## Mack1998
**Core claims.** Mack and Rock argue that there is no conscious perception without attention. Across a series of experiments in the early 1990s they showed that clearly visible, briefly presented stimuli, even at the point of fixation, go unreported when attention is engaged by another task. They named the phenomenon inattentional blindness, and argue it reflects a failure of perception, not merely of memory.

**Specifics.** The main method was an inattention paradigm. Observers judged which arm of a briefly flashed cross was longer, a demanding task. On one critical trial, without warning, an additional small object (a square or other simple shape) appeared near the cross at about the same time. Immediately afterwards observers were asked whether they had seen anything different. A substantial fraction reported nothing, even though the stimulus was in the same region as the attended cross. Miss rates reported across the experiments ranged widely, from a minority to a majority of observers depending on the stimulus location, size and task; I could not confirm single headline figures. Further trials, with the observers now told to expect something (divided attention and full attention trials), showed the same observers could detect the stimulus, ruling out simple sensory limits. Stimuli with personal meaning, such as the observer's own name or a happy face, were noticed more often than neutral words or scrambled or sad faces, which implies that some meaning-based processing occurs before awareness. Larger noticing happened when the cross was at fixation and the stimulus was at the same position than when attention was spread across the display. The methodology was first described in Rock, Linnett, Grant and Mack (1992, Cognitive Psychology 24:502-534, "Perception without attention: Results of a new method"). Most, Scholl, Clifford and Simons (2005, Psychological Review) later showed the effect of attentional set and similarity.

**Criticism and status.** The phenomenon replicates, and was extended by Simons and Chabris (1999) to dynamic events. The interpretation is debated. Wolfe (1999) argued for "inattentional amnesia": the stimulus is seen but immediately forgotten. Moore and Egeth (1997) found that unseen stimuli still affected later judgments, which suggests some implicit processing. The percentage who miss varies strongly across stimuli and tasks, so no figure should be treated as a population constant. Mack herself developed the view that attention is a precondition of conscious perception.

**Use for LTF.**
- Self-relevant content (the learner's own name, their own progress) is noticed more often, so personal framing helps capture attention at key moments.
- Do not depend on a learner noticing a small nonessential element close to their fixation while they concentrate.
- When attention is spread widely during a scan, small peripheral changes go unnoticed.
- Tie notice-critical information to the learner's active goal, not to a parallel channel.
- Tell learners explicitly what to watch; instructed attention overcomes blindness.

## Nielsen2006F
**Core claims.** Jakob Nielsen (2006, Nielsen Norman Group Alertbox) reported an eyetracking study in which gaze heat maps of users reading web pages often formed an F shape. Users first read in a horizontal movement across the upper part of the content area; they then move down and read across a second, shorter horizontal stripe; and finally scan the left side of the content vertically. Nielsen drew practical rules from it: users do not read text thoroughly word by word, the first two paragraphs must carry the most important information, and the first words of headings, subheads and paragraphs must carry information. Kara Pernice (NN/g, 2017) revisited it, with the message that the F-pattern is real but misunderstood.

**Specifics.** NN/g reports the 2006 study as eyetracking of 232 users looking at thousands of web pages (the sample is reported by the vendor). The F is a composite of heat maps. Pernice's update lists several other patterns: the layer-cake pattern, where users fixate on headings and subheadings and skip body text, seen on well formatted pages; the spotted pattern, where users skip over text looking for particular items such as a link, a number or a distinctive word; and the commitment pattern, where a highly motivated user reads everything. She argues that the F appears when text is unformatted, without clear headings, bullet points or bold, and the user is in a hurry, so it is a symptom of poor design, not a model to emulate, and that the pattern still appears on mobile. Other NN/g research reports that people scan more than they read: Morkes and Nielsen (1997) found that the large majority of users scanned pages, with only a small minority reading word by word (figures commonly given are 79 percent and 16 percent, not confirmed here), and Nielsen's 2008 summary of Weinreich et al. (2008, ACM Transactions on the Web) says visitors read perhaps 20 percent of the words on a page on average (approximate, not confirmed). Buscher, Cutrell and Morris (2009) independently found that the right third of pages gets almost no fixations in the first second, and that the top left draws attention first.

**Criticism and status.** This is a practitioner report and not peer reviewed, with the sample and method self-reported. Heat maps merge many users: individual gaze paths are not F-shaped. The pattern depends on content, layout and task, and Pernice agrees. Studies by other groups (for example Djamasbi and colleagues on image-led layouts) find different patterns with different layouts. It assumes left-to-right reading.

**Use for LTF.**
- Front-load: begin lesson paragraphs, list items and headings with the key term.
- Use real headings and chunking so readers use the layer-cake pattern and scan the headings.
- Put the primary action at the start of the reading flow, left aligned, not far right.
- Do not rely on a right-hand sidebar for essential content.
- Make the first screen of each lesson self-sufficient, with the first two sentences carrying the idea.

## Benway1998
**Core claims.** Benway and Lane reported that people searching web pages often fail to find "obvious" links and information when it is styled like a banner (large, colorful, positioned at the top or edges), even when it is the thing they need. People have learned that banner-like elements are advertising and so avoid them. The term banner blindness was coined in this work. The practical message is that important content must not look like an ad.

**Specifics.** The study was a usability test in which participants searched a web site for specific information. A brightly colored, banner-like element contained a link relevant to the task. Many participants missed it and used less prominent plain text links instead, and some searched for a long time. The authors concluded that style, size and position can make a salient element be ignored, and recommended not making important content look like banners. I could not confirm the participant count, the exact proportions that missed the link, or the volume and issue of the journal (the piece appeared in the newsletter Internetworking in 1998), so these are left out of sources.json. Later eyetracking work bears on the effect. Drèze and Hussherr (2003, Journal of Interactive Marketing) found that people rarely fixated banners during tasks yet banners still had some effect on brand measures. Burke, Hornof, Nilsen and Gorman (2005, ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction) found that banners hindered visual search and were poorly remembered when the task was a search. Resnick and Albert (2014, International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction) reported that banner blindness depends on the ad's location and the user's task. Nielsen's later Alertbox articles on banner blindness (2007) described users also avoiding anything ad-like in design, such as right rail items or items placed where ads are common.

**Criticism and status.** The original is a small, early web usability study and is not a controlled experiment. Later studies mostly support reduced fixation of ad-like items, but show that the effect is not total: banner content can be processed implicitly, animation attracts some attention, and ads that match the task are viewed. Treat it as a strong heuristic with task dependence.

**Use for LTF.**
- Do not style critical content as an ad: wide, colorful, full-width strips at the top of a lesson will be skipped.
- Promotional-looking prompts (upgrade, streak boosts) are ignored by habit, so important prompts should look like part of the content.
- Place essential information in the content flow, not in edge areas conventionally used for ads (right rail, top strip).
- Salience alone does not guarantee attention; the expectation that something is irrelevant beats pop-out.
- Style announcements differently from content so users learn which is skippable.

## Rosenholtz2007
**Core claims.** Clutter can be measured from an image. Rosenholtz, Li and Nakano (2007, Journal of Vision 7(2):17) propose two measures: Feature Congestion (local variability in color, luminance contrast and orientation) and Subband Entropy (how efficiently the image can be coded, which is low when the image is regular and high when cluttered). A third measure, Edge Density (Mack and Oliva 2004), is tested for comparison. The measures were checked against how hard it was to find targets in displays. Clutter is not the same as set size, since natural scenes do not have countable objects.

**Specifics.** Feature Congestion treats clutter as the volume of an ellipsoid fitted to the covariance of local feature vectors (color in CIELab, luminance contrast, orientation energy), computed at multiple scales and combined into a single score for an image, so images in which features vary more are more cluttered. Subband Entropy decomposes an image into luminance and chrominance channels, then into orientation and scale subbands, and measures the entropy of the coefficients; a more redundant, predictable image costs fewer bits and counts as less cluttered. The authors validated the measures with search tasks using US maps (the "finding a small target in maps" paradigm, varying the number of map elements) and with subjective clutter ratings. Both measures correlated with performance, and with human ratings, better than the simple edge density, and Feature Congestion in particular tracked search performance. MATLAB code is distributed by MIT. Feature Congestion was introduced earlier for display clutter by Rosenholtz, Li, Mansfield and Jin (2005, CHI). Related work links clutter to peripheral crowding: items too close together cannot be separated by peripheral vision (Whitney and Levi 2011; Rosenholtz 2011). Moacdieh and Sarter (2015, Human Factors) review definitions and measures of display clutter, including in cockpits; I did not re-confirm their details.

**Criticism and status.** Validation was mainly with maps and scenes, not modern flat interfaces. Low-level statistics ignore semantic grouping, white space and alignment, which change perceived clutter at the same item count. Results across measures vary by task. No effect size from the paper is quoted here.

**Use for LTF.**
- Clutter is quantifiable; compare screenshots of layouts with a clutter measure.
- Reduce the variability of features (fewer colors, sizes and orientations), not only the number of elements. Uniform backgrounds lower clutter.
- Crowding in the periphery makes dense panels hard to read at a glance, so separate instrument widgets with space.
- Fly mode, glanced at in the periphery, needs lower clutter than study pages.
- Grouping and consistent alignment lower perceived clutter at the same item count.
- Compare Day and Night themes separately; a theme with more luminance contrast variation may score as more cluttered even with identical content.
- Set a clutter budget per screen type (study, challenge, fly mode) and check new components against it before shipping.

## Buscher2009
**Core claims.** Buscher, Cutrell and Morris (CHI 2009) ran an eye-tracking study in which 20 users viewed 361 web pages while doing information foraging tasks and a page recognition task. They describe where on a page attention goes, how it depends on task and demographics, and build a model that predicts how much attention individual page elements (DOM elements) receive. They also introduce "fixation impact", a way to map gaze to page elements that weights each fixation by its duration and by the element's distance from the fixation, motivated by vision research. (This section was written from the full paper PDF.)

**Specifics.** Participants (10 men and 10 women, aged 18 to 69, mean 33.0) used Internet Explorer 7 in a 1024 by 741 pixel window with a Tobii x50 tracker (50 Hz, accuracy about 0.5 degrees). Fixations were at least 100 ms within a 50 pixel radius. Eight information tasks on four topics (cars, kite surfing, wind energy, diabetes) used nine preselected pages per topic; two free-query tasks allowed free browsing; a final recognition task asked participants to rate familiarity with pages they had seen and twelve others. In all, 2,126 page views on 361 pages were recorded (an average of 11.3 viewers for each of the 36 preselected pages). The viewport was divided into a 3 by 3 grid above the fold (996 by 716 pixels) plus everything below the fold. Findings: the entire right side above the fold was neglected, with about 3 seconds to first fixate the three right regions in foraging tasks and about 2 seconds in recognition tasks, and median fixation impact there was 0. The four upper-left regions drew attention fastest regardless of task. For foraging, center-left drew more and earlier attention; for recognition, top-left drew the most. Women looked longer than men during recognition (mean 3432 ms versus 2866 ms, p < 0.05), and participants over 30 looked longer than under 30 (3699 versus 2593 ms, p < 0.001). Familiar sites changed the first-second distribution. The prediction model used 59 features (44 HTML element-name features, 3 abstract features such as DOM tree level, and 12 rendering features: size, aspect ratio and 10 positional regions). Element size was the strongest predictor. Linear regression on the top 10 features gave a correlation of 0.50; a combination of a decision tree (is there any fixation impact) and regression gave 0.69; HTML-only features gave only 0.28.

**Criticism and status.** Peer reviewed, but small (20 participants, mostly experienced web users, 17 of 20), 2009-era desktop pages, one browser and tracker, and average heat-map style analysis that hides layout-specific behavior, which the authors acknowledge. Predictions were weaker for pages with unusual layouts, such as Wikipedia pages with images on the right. Designs, mobile viewing and responsive layouts have changed since. The results broadly agree with the F-pattern reports but add quantitative regions.

**Use for LTF.**
- Expect the first second of attention to go to the top left and center left. Put the lesson title and key idea there.
- The right third gets almost no early attention, so do not place primary actions or essential facts there without another cue.
- Element size is the strongest predictor of attention: size is an attention budget, so make important things bigger and decorations smaller.
- Task changes where people look, so test foraging (scan to find) separately from reading and recognition (is this the right page).
- Familiarity shifts attention, so a returning learner needs stable layout for fast scanning.

## Lavie1995
**Core claims.** Load theory says that whether irrelevant distractors are processed depends on the perceptual load of the relevant task. When perceptual load is low, spare capacity automatically spills over to distractors and they interfere. When perceptual load is high, capacity is exhausted by the relevant task and distractors are not perceived. This reconciles early selection (Broadbent) and late selection (Deutsch and Deutsch) accounts and gives a rule: selective attention succeeds under high perceptual load, not under low load. Later work (Lavie, Hirst, de Fockert and Viding 2004) added that cognitive control, which depends on working memory, works the opposite way: loading working memory increases distractor interference.

**Specifics.** Lavie used a response-competition paradigm. Participants searched a display for one of two target letters (for example X or N) and pressed a key to indicate which. A distractor letter outside the search display was either compatible (the same response), incompatible, or neutral. In the low load condition the target was alone or among one or a few items that were easily ignored; in the high load condition the target was among several different, similar letters in a ring. With low load, incompatible distractors slowed responses relative to compatible ones. With high load the interference was greatly reduced or vanished. Lavie defined load either by increasing the number of items that are relevant, or by making perceptual discriminations more demanding for the same number of items, as distinct from the difficulty of decisions or responses. The exact reaction times in the original are not quoted here. Later studies extended the account to unexpected stimuli: Cartwright-Finch and Lavie (2007, Cognition) reported that raising perceptual load increased inattentional blindness for an unexpected shape. Lavie's 2005 review (Trends in Cognitive Sciences) summarizes behavioral and neuroimaging support, including reduced distractor processing in visual cortex under high load.

**Criticism and status.** The distractor effects under low and high load have been widely replicated. The definition of load has been criticized as circular, with other explanations such as display density or dilution (Benoni and Tsal 2010; Tsal and Benoni 2010). Effects vary with distractor type; some very salient stimuli (own name, faces) break through. Effects under high working memory load are consistent, but the translation to complex applications is untested.

**Use for LTF.**
- High perceptual load on the main task lowers distraction, but it also lowers noticing of everything else, so a complicated lesson graphic can hide a nearby message.
- On simple, low-load screens, any ornament or animation will be processed and may interfere, so keep simple screens calm.
- Learners holding numbers in memory (headings and altitudes to enter in the simulator) are more distractible, so remove nonessential motion from fly mode.
- Do not mix demands: avoid asking learners to remember something while they scan.
- Tune distraction to the task. Study mode can have light richness; fly mode should minimize anything extra.

## Wickens2003
**Core claims.** The SEEV model predicts where pilots (and other operators) look. The probability of attending to an area of interest increases with its Salience, with Expectancy (how often information changes there, the event rate or bandwidth), and with Value (how important the information is to tasks), and decreases with Effort (the cost of moving gaze, or head and body, to get there). Salience and effort are bottom-up factors; expectancy and value are top-down. The 2003 Human Factors paper (Wickens, Goh, Helleberg, Horrey and Talleur) tested such attentional models on pilots flying simulated approaches with advanced display technology.

**Specifics.** The model is often written as P(A) = s*S - ef*EF + ex*EX + v*V with coefficients fitted to data. Wickens's group fitted it to scanning data in cockpit and driving studies. I could not confirm in this session the number of pilots, the specific correlations or the reported variance explained in the 2003 paper, so no figure is given. The model builds on a long line of work: Fitts, Jones and Milton (1950) recorded pilots' eye movements during instrument landing approaches and found that the attitude indicator (and the primary flight instruments clustered around it) was looked at most; Senders (1964) proposed that monitors sample each instrument at a rate that reflects its bandwidth (rate of change), which is expectancy; and Bellenkes, Wickens and Kramer (1997) found that expert pilots had shorter dwells and sampled more efficiently than novices. The standard "six pack" arrangement of airspeed, attitude, altimeter, turn, heading and vertical speed supports the model, since instruments are placed to reduce effort between the most-used ones. Horrey, Wickens and Consalus (2006, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied) applied it to driving. Wickens and McCarley (2008, Applied Attention Theory) give a textbook account.

**Criticism and status.** The model is descriptive: coefficients are fitted per task rather than predicted a priori, and it ignores details such as reading, scene context or the specific content of what is seen. In the cited studies expectancy and value tended to be stronger predictors than salience, which suggests that flashing alerts alone are weak at redirecting scans. It has been tested mostly in aviation and driving simulations, not on web pages.

**Use for LTF.**
- Instrument widgets should mimic the real panel layout, so expectancy and habits from the real cockpit drive the learner's scan.
- Make high-value, high-change items (airspeed, altitude, attitude) low-effort by putting them close together and central.
- Teach scanning on pages that match what the simulator shows so habits transfer.
- Do not rely on flashing to pull a scan toward a neglected area; build the habit through repeated practice.
- In fly mode the cost of looking away from the simulator counts, so minimize the effort of reading page content.

## Healey2012
**Core claims.** Healey and Enns survey vision science for visualization and computer graphics. They cover preattentive features (processed within a single glance, about 200 to 250 ms or less, without serial search), how features interact and interfere, and how attention and memory limit what viewers see, including change blindness and inattentional blindness. They draw design guidance for choosing visual features to encode data and to draw attention. An earlier paper (Healey, Booth and Enns 1996, ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction) tested high speed estimation with preattentive features.

**Specifics.** The survey lists features that support rapid detection, including hue, luminance, size, orientation, curvature, length, motion (direction, flicker), closure, intersection, and depth cues. The preattentive tasks it considers are target detection (is a unique item present), boundary detection (is there a texture boundary), region tracking, counting and estimating numerosity. Reaction time or accuracy in single-feature displays does not depend on the number of distractors, in displays shown for 200 ms or less. Features interact: variation in one feature can interfere with detecting a boundary in another (Callaghan 1984, luminance and hue), so a dominant feature can mask a secondary one. Search becomes harder as target-distractor difference falls and as distractor heterogeneity rises, which matches Duncan and Humphreys (1989). They recommend choosing perceptually discriminable values (using perceptually uniform color spaces), limiting the number of simultaneous data dimensions, and using motion sparingly since it is very attention-grabbing. On the memory side, only a few objects are retained across views and changes are detected only if attention is directed to the changed item. They also discuss feature hierarchies, such as the dominance of luminance and hue over shape for some boundary tasks. Specific numeric thresholds are not quoted from the paper here.

**Criticism and status.** The survey is well regarded and extensively cited in visualization. The preattentive claims come from controlled brief displays with a single feature difference; clutter, conjunctions, and heterogeneous distractors erode them, and Wolfe prefers "guiding feature" to "preattentive". Transfer to real interfaces needs testing.

**Use for LTF.**
- Code one key dimension per signal: color alone, or size alone, not color and shape and motion together.
- Limit simultaneous categories to those that remain discriminable, a handful of hues.
- Use motion rarely and deliberately since it dominates attention.
- Make differences large and test them in both Day and Night themes, where luminance and hue contrast change.
- Because of change blindness, highlight what changed when a value updates.
- When a chart or gauge uses several encoded dimensions, test it with a 200 ms flash: if a viewer cannot name the odd item in that time, the feature choice is not doing preattentive work and needs more contrast or fewer competing features.
- Treat feature interference as a design risk: a strongly varying background luminance in a widget can mask a hue-coded warning, so keep widget backgrounds flat.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** Every source in the lane agrees that attention is scarce and selective: people perceive what they attend to and little else. Bottom-up salience (Treisman, Itti, Healey and Enns) is real, but it is one of several factors (Wolfe and Horowitz), and task, expectation and value (Yarbus, Wickens, Benway and Lane) often outweigh it. Unattended things, even large ones, go unnoticed (Simons and Chabris, Rensink, Mack and Rock), and the more the primary task demands, the less spills over to everything else (Lavie). Real-page eye tracking (Nielsen and Pernice, Buscher and colleagues) shows that users scan more than they read, with early attention biased to the top left and the content column, little attention to the right third of a page, and avoidance of anything that looks like an advertisement. Clutter, measured as variability of color, contrast and orientation (Rosenholtz), makes search slower and is separate from item count.

**Where they conflict or qualify.** Early theory split perception into a preattentive stage and an attentive stage, but later work shows a continuum of search efficiency. Saliency models do reasonably in free viewing and poorly under a task, and Yarbus's claim that task determines gaze is partly replicated at a smaller effect than the famous figure suggests. The F-pattern is popular, yet its own reporter says it signals poor formatting, and the evidence is a vendor report. Perceptual load can protect a task from distraction or increase blindness to important events, depending on whether the event is the learner's goal. Several figures (Simons percentages, Wickens correlations, Benway proportions) could not be confirmed in this session and are not used as facts.

**Evidence quality.** The strongest evidence in the lane is laboratory work with simple displays (Treisman, Posner, Lavie, Rensink, Simons), replicated for decades but measured in milliseconds on sparse stimuli. The web-specific evidence is thinner: Buscher and colleagues is peer reviewed but small (20 participants, 2009 desktop pages), and the F-pattern and banner blindness reports are practitioner studies, later partly supported by eye tracking. Aviation scanning evidence (Wickens, Fitts) is the closest match to LTF's fly mode, yet it concerns real cockpits and simulators, not a web page beside a simulator. For design decisions, treat the lab results as direction, the web results as hypotheses to test, and run a quick eye-tracking or five-second test on the real screens where a decision matters.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Give one thing per screen a unique single-feature difference, and do not signal with feature conjunctions.
2. Quiet the rest: salience is relative to the surroundings and falls as comparable peaks multiply.
3. Reserve the accent color and motion for what is actionable, in both Day and Night themes.
4. Keep the positions of recurring elements fixed so expectancy and history guide the eye.
5. Front-load text and use real headings, because the first words of lines and the top left are read first.
6. Do not style important content like an ad or banner, and do not put essentials in the right third.
7. Mark changes with a local transient, since full-screen replacement hides them.
8. Critical alerts must not rely on peripheral noticing during a concentrated task.
9. In fly mode, minimize clutter, effort and motion, because the learner is in a high-load, memory-loaded state.
10. Match instrument layouts to the real panel, and test with tasks and a saliency or clutter measure, not free viewing.
