# Lane 01: Classical typography (craft tradition, 1764 to today)

Each section covers one work. Evidence labels: "craft treatise" means authority from practice, not experiment; "essay" means argued opinion by a practitioner or critic. Every figure or quotation is confirmed at the URL named in that section's last line.

## warde1955crystal

Beatrice Warde, "The Crystal Goblet, or Why Printing Should Be Invisible" (lecture 1930; collected in *The Crystal Goblet: Sixteen Essays on Typography*, London: Sylvan Press, 1955).

### Core claims

Warde argues that printing exists to carry thought from one mind to others, and that every typographic choice should be judged by whether it serves that transfer. Her image is a choice between two wine goblets, one of solid gold with exquisite ornament and one of thin, clear crystal. The connoisseur picks the crystal because it reveals the wine instead of hiding it. Type should likewise reveal the text. Her one-line version in the text I read: "Type well used is invisible as type, just as the perfect talking voice is the unnoticed vehicle for the transmission of words, ideas."

Three supporting claims follow. She separates readability from what an optician would call legibility: a page in 14-point bold sans serif is, according to laboratory tests, more "legible" than one in 11-point Baskerville, but a speaker who bellows is more audible without being a better speaker. She says the "mental eye focuses through type and not upon it." And she insists the work is hard: "There is nothing simple or dull in achieving the transparent page."

### Specifics

The essay is a lecture text with no sample, measures or data. It offers a typology and a list of named failure modes.

The book typographer builds a window between the reader and the author's words. A stained-glass window (her example is rich text Gothic) is something to look at, not through. A transparent window leaves no visual memory of the typography. A window of small leaded panes corresponds to what she calls fine printing, where you are conscious that someone enjoyed building it.

The failure modes are concrete. Margins are compared to the long thin stem of a wine glass, which keeps fingerprints off the bowl, so margins keep fingers off the type page. Setting that invites "doubling" of lines or reading three words as one keeps the reader subconsciously worried. A running headline "that keeps shouting at us," a line that looks like one long word, and capitals "jammed together without hair spaces" cause "subconscious squinting and loss of mental focus."

She defines "modernist" functionally: the man who first chose glass over clay asked "What must it do?" rather than "How should it look?", and "to that extent all good typography is modernist." In an anecdote the typographer T. M. Cleland set a specimen layout in Latin so the client would not read the text.

### Replication and criticism

Nothing here can be replicated; it is rhetoric. Its one testable assertion, the bold-sans comparison, cites unnamed "laboratory tests" that I could not identify, so I treat it as her recollection.

The best-known critique is Matthew Butterick's "Drowning the Crystal Goblet" (*Practical Typography*). He calls the metaphor "totally inapt": text is already invisible without typography, so typography cannot be the invisible part; helium in a balloon is a better analogy, since the balloon gives visible shape to something unseen. He concedes the modest point that typography should not diminish meaning, but argues that presentation adds meaning, as clothing and tone of voice do. Dating is also contested: Butterick's page says 1932, while the reader that reprints the text says October 1930, to the British Typographers Guild. I follow the reader.

The reader's headnote describes her as a Monotype publicist who worked with Stanley Morison, so she spoke for a manufacturer's tradition.

### Implications for LTF

1. Lesson pages are sustained reading, where Warde's rule applies: one calm text face, ordinary sizes, no decorative display type in running text. A useful test: can a learner recall the lesson but not describe the type?
2. Her own caveat undercuts "bigger and bolder is better." Laboratory legibility is not the whole of readability. For LTF this supports a split: lessons follow book-face discipline, while fly mode (a glance beside the sim) is a different task and can use large, heavy figures. That split is my design inference, not Warde's claim.
3. Butterick's counterpoint matters for tone: type can set a reassuring voice for beginners. Reconciled rule: never let type cause friction, but choose it deliberately.
4. Her "shouting" running headline maps to sticky headers: repeated furniture should be quiet.
5. Review layouts with real lesson text, never lorem ipsum; only real text exposes reading friction.

## morison1936first

Stanley Morison, *First Principles of Typography* (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1936; essay first printed 1929 and 1930). Read in the 1951 Cambridge reprint inside Paul A. Bennett (ed.), *Books and Printing: A Treasury for Typophiles*.

### Core claims

Morison's own note calls the piece an "essay towards a rationale of book-typography." His definition: "Typography may be defined as the art of rightly disposing printing material in accordance with specific purpose; of so arranging the letters, distributing the space and controlling the type as to aid to the maximum the reader's comprehension of the text." The corollary is blunt: typography is "the efficient means to an essentially utilitarian and only accidentally aesthetic end," so "any disposition of printing material which, whatever the intention, has the effect of coming between author and reader is wrong." In books meant to be read there is "little room for 'bright' typography."

The second claim concerns convention. Book typography rests on the nature of alphabetic writing and on the traditions of the society served, and demands "an obedience to convention which is almost absolute." The wider the audience, the stricter the limits: an experiment suits a 50-copy tract, not one printed 50,000 times. Hence "Type design moves at the pace of the most conservative reader."

### Specifics

The essay has seven numbered parts. Working rules:

- Imposition, the placing of the text block on the page, is "the most important element in typography." Margins should be proportionate to the text area, with "decent space for thumbs and fingers." The vertical oblong is the normal page because a horizontal oblong drives lines to an impossible length.
- Three composition principles: the eye cannot read many words with sharply contrasted thicks and thins with ease; lines beyond a certain length are disagreeable; letter size must relate to line length. "The average line of words which the reader's eye can conveniently seize is between ten and twelve." When small type is unavoidable, insert leads (extra line spacing) to prevent "doubling," the reading of the same line twice. He defends leading against purists.
- His summary formula (part IV): vertical oblong pages; paragraphs of "ten to twelve consistently spaced words"; type of "comfortable size and familiar design"; lines separated enough to prevent doubling; a running title; margins related to line length.
- Indent every paragraph after the first, by a space "sufficient to be noticeable." Abolishing indention is "plainly an undesirable practice."
- Title pages: no line larger than twice the text size ("If the book be set in 12-point, the title need be no larger than 24-point").
- Part V answers the charge of monotony: decoration belongs on the preliminary pages, not in the text, because "the typographer's only purpose is to express, not himself, but his author."

He also describes how publishers "drive out" a book to look longer, with wide spaces, blank pages and big initials.

### Replication and criticism

This is craft doctrine, not experiment. Its claims about contrast, line length and leading were later tested in legibility research (lane 02); I state no results here. Two tensions are internal. "Familiar design" as the standard makes legibility partly circular: Edward Johnston's 1906 footnote (johnston1906writing) warns that our view of legibility "is apt to favour custom and use unduly." And the ten-to-twelve-word line is asserted without a source, though Gill also gives "10-12 words per line" (gill1936essay), which looks like shared workshop lore rather than two measurements.

Matthew Carter's review of Robin Kinross's history places Morison and Bruce Rogers in a "new traditionalism" influenced by the Monotype Corporation, and presents Kinross's aim as critical reflection on such doctrine.

### Implications for LTF

1. The definition is a one-sentence brief for lesson text: aid comprehension, nothing else. Use it to veto decorative type in lessons.
2. Wider audiences demand stricter convention. Beginners cannot be asked to learn a reading idiom, so lessons should use familiar patterns: left-aligned text, spaced paragraphs, predictable headings.
3. Imposition before composition: set page width, margins and the reading column before choosing type details.
4. His defence of leading supports generous line spacing when a column must run wide.
5. Test for "doubling" on a narrow phone and a wide desktop: line returns should be easy.

## gill1936essay

Eric Gill, *An Essay on Typography* (London: Sheed & Ward, 1931; second, re-set edition 1936). Read in the Boston: David R. Godine photo-lithographic reprint of the 1936 text, with an introduction by Christopher Skelton.

### Core claims

Gill writes about typography "as it is affected by the conditions of the year 1931," when industrialism had won an almost complete victory over handicraft. His thesis is that two worlds now stand apart, the power of the machine and the humanity of the craftsman, and that each should stop imitating the other: "Handicrafts standards are as absurd for mechanised industry as machine standards are absurd for the craftsman." The book applies that idea to letters and books.

His best-known specific claim is in the chapter "The Procrustean Bed" (the composing stick that forces lines to equal length): even spacing between words matters more than equal line length. Lines of unequal length are not in themselves objectionable, uneven spacing is, so it is better to sacrifice the neat right edge. In the 1936 preface he admits that the 1930 text was imperfect and that he rewrote much of it.

### Specifics

The measure argument is the most usable part. Gill lists the faults of the extremes. A very wide measure involves too much movement of the eye and head and risks "doubling" unless lines are separated by leads. A very narrow measure, narrow in relation to the type, cuts phrases and words up, because "Practised readers do not read letter by letter or even word by word, but phrase by phrase." He reports that "the consensus of opinion favours an average of 10-12 words per line" and that at fifteen or more words equal length and even spacing can both be had, but "the best length for reading is not more than 12 words."

Because a ten-to-twelve-word line is short for a compositor, equal length and even spacing conflict. His resolution: choose even spacing, let the right edge be ragged, and compromise only so far as the spacing stays visibly even. He calls equal line length "a laudable appetite" that "has become somewhat of a Superstition," and says a page of equal lines "partakes too much of the ideas of those who regard books as things to be looked at rather than read."

The book is also an object lesson: Skelton's introduction says the 1936 edition was hand-set in Gill's own Joanna, in 12 point with 3 point leading, printed at Pigotts, and that it was the first use of Joanna. The first two pages of the justification chapter are set justified to demonstrate the argument, and the rest is unjustified, with ampersands, contractions such as "tho'" and paragraph marks instead of indents.

### Replication and criticism

Craft opinion, with no experiment. Gill's reasoning has been partly absorbed (ragged-right setting is common) and partly questioned. Skelton's introduction makes the most pointed assessment: the case for close word spacing "has long since been recognised," but Gill's own pages need as many word breaks as ordinary setting, "some of them very dubious," and "if Gill had used the same treatment with justified setting, one wonders whether any unevenness of word spacing would be noticeable." Skelton also notes Gill's habitual disparagement of the industrial system, a bias to bear in mind: his preference for the hand is a moral position as well as a typographic one.

The ten-to-twelve-word figure matches Morison's (morison1936first) without citation by either; treat it as workshop consensus. Whether ragged-right is more readable than justified is an empirical question for lane 02 and lane 03.

### Implications for LTF

1. Gill's complaint, uneven word spaces from forcing equal lines, is what justified text produces when hyphenation is limited (my inference), so lesson text should default to left-aligned, ragged-right.
2. Gill supplies a measure budget in words, not characters: roughly 10–12 words per line is a craft target for lesson columns, to be checked against lane 02 and lane 03 evidence before it becomes a rule.
3. His warning on narrow measures bears on phone layouts and side panels: when the column gets narrow, fewer words per line cuts phrases apart, so allow reflow rather than shrinking the type.
4. Even spacing matters most in short, high-stakes text such as checklist items and quiz stems; avoid forced justification and stretched spaces there.

Skelton's remarks, Joanna and 1936 printing details come from that edition's "Publishing History". Open Library work OL1178647W lists the Dent and Godine editions.

## updike1922printing

Daniel Berkeley Updike, *Printing Types: Their History, Forms, and Use. A Study in Survivals* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 1922; The Merrymount Press, Boston; two volumes).

### Core claims

Updike's book is a history of European and American printing types arranged by country and period, with a practical purpose stated in the chapter on choosing types. He calls it "a study in survivals" because it tries to show "not only what types have survived, but what should survive through their fitness for the best typography." Its method assumes that long survival signals fitness, and that studying type forms teaches "not only how to choose" but gives "courage to eliminate."

Its sharpest claim is a moral one about taste: "There are many ways of being wrong, but only one way of being right." He adds that if we do not judge types rightly, "they will judge us." The ideal composing room is built not from a list of "standard" faces accepted on others' say-so, but by printers who study and then add interesting fonts from sources that study has revealed.

### Specifics

The work has two volumes and, by the chapter numbers in the scan, runs to at least twenty-four chapters, covering Italian, French, Dutch, German, English and American types, with chapter XIX on "classical" types (Bodoni and the Didots) and later chapters on the revival of Caslon and on the English and American revival of early type forms. Chapter XXIII, "The Choice of Types for a Composing-Room," gives his working verdicts.

In that chapter he praises transitional types (the Baskerville and later Caslon letter between old style and "modern") as workable, since they do "all the work of an old style type" while being "more interesting" and "less bleak and commonplace" than a modern face. He then lists the types "we ought not to want," which "have no place in any artistically respectable composing-room": practically all types on "standard line," all condensed or expanded types, all "sans-serif" or (as he says "absurdly miscalled") "gothic" types, all fat-faced black-letter and fat-faced roman, all hair-line types, almost all "ornamented" types and types imitating engraving, and almost all shaded types. He also condemns the family variants of many standard series as "condensations, distortions, or exaggerations of the original letter," the "disreputable offspring of honest parents."

Chapter XIX on Bodoni records dated facts: the *Manuale Tipografico* appeared in a second and final edition in two quarto volumes in 1818, five years after Bodoni's death, completed by his widow and Luigi Orsi; only 290 copies were struck off, per Signora Bodoni's letter. Updike judges it "probably the most elaborate specimen that the world has ever seen," and "the acme of Bodoni's late, chilly, dry manner." He divides Bodoni's work into an early period of old style or transitional types with profuse decoration and a later period of his own designs and unadorned typography.

### Replication and criticism

Craft history and connoisseurship, written from within the Boston private-press tradition. It has no experimental content. The verdict against sans serif is a product of its date: Tschichold's *New Typography* would argue the opposite within six years of the book's appearance (tschichold1998new), and the later history Robin Kinross tells (kinross2004modern) is largely of sans serif and asymmetry becoming mainstream. Treat his lists as period taste, not findings.

The survival logic is also questionable: survival can reflect habit, cost and foundry inventory, not fitness. I read an error-prone OCR scan and did not check his historical attributions against later scholarship, so use the book for his judgments and check any historical claim elsewhere.

His verdict on hair-line types is echoed by a blogger's excerpt of Dowding that modern-face (Bodoni-style) types need generous leading and narrow measures (dowding1954finer).

### Implications for LTF

1. "Courage to eliminate" is a type-selection rule: LTF needs few families (one text face, one display or numeral face, one monospace if code appears), chosen deliberately.
2. His "disreputable offspring" is a rule against synthetic distortion: do not let the browser fake bold or italic, and never scale a widget label horizontally to fit.
3. Hair-line weights remain a risk. Updike condemns them in print; on screens at small sizes thin strokes are even more fragile (see lane 03 for the rendering evidence).
4. The transitional category (moderate contrast, sturdy serifs) is a sensible default for lesson serifs.
5. Updike's sans verdict should not bind LTF: instrument widgets and fly mode will use sans numerals for glanceability. Record this as a deliberate departure from a traditionalist source.

## devinne1910practice

Theodore Low De Vinne, *The Practice of Typography: Correct Composition* (New York: The Century Co., 1901; third edition 1910, the text read here).

### Core claims

*Correct Composition* is one volume of De Vinne's four-volume *Practice of Typography*. It is a manual for compositors and proof-readers on spelling, abbreviations, numerals, italic and capital letters, division of words, spacing, punctuation and proof-reading. Its central argument is that readers notice and demand consistency. In the preface he says printing that passed a tolerant inspection in 1850 "does not pass now," and that "the reader of to-day is more or less a critic of style in type-setting."

Because "there is no book of generally accepted authority that lays down a full code of explicit rules for orderly printing," each printing house that strives for consistency must make its own code, the "style-card," to guide new compositors and maintain uniformity. The book publishes such a code for others, while allowing that different houses and kinds of work need different rules.

### Specifics

The spacing chapter (XII) is the most useful part for design. Its rules, all for hand-set metal type:

- Composition is made more pleasing "when the spaces between words seem to be of the same width in all lines on the page." Lines thick-spaced at one side and thin at the other are "a disgrace to the printer."
- Absolute uniformity is impossible; the compositor must "be content with approximations to an ideal but unreached standard."
- To make spacing seem even, the physical spaces must differ. Round letters such as o and e fill about half the type body, ascending and descending letters about three fourths, and angled letters (A, L, Y, W) leave gaps that should be counted as part of the space, so a word ending in y followed by one beginning with W takes a thinner space. This discrimination is impractical at small sizes and "desirable and even necessary" above twelve point.
- For solid composition in lower case, the three-to-em space is the most used; the en quadrat suits wide-leaded matter. Em-quad word spaces ("pigeonholing") are "generally disapproved."
- Capitals, being about twice as high and wide as round lower-case letters, need word spaces "twice as wide" and leads "twice as thick"; the en quadrat is the least permissible word space in capitals.
- On division of words he says words "always appear better unbroken, but the breaking of words may not be so unsightly as the breaking up of a general uniformity of the spacing between words." He names "hounds'-teeth," irregular white gaps across lines, as "more offensive to the reader than any strangeness of division."
- Small capitals of nine point and smaller "are dense and not easily read" for running titles; hair-spacing helps.

He also quotes Horace Hart's rule for divisions at Oxford: the part left at line end should suggest the part carried over.

### Replication and criticism

Craft codification, from a leading American printer-historian. The rules are conventions, not findings, and many are tied to metal type and hand work. He is explicit that American and British houses differ (English printers divide "mo-ther," American printers "moth-er"). The book's standing was high among practitioners: the introduction to the 1936 edition of Gill's *Essay on Typography* says De Vinne's four-volume work, "full of sound detail," was probably known to Gill and Hague, and that Morison "regarded it highly."

De Vinne also records the fashions of his own time. A footnote notes that "several eminent disciples of the fifteenth-century school of typography" use only a thin space, reasoning from the early printers, and he calls em-quad-spaced running titles "too uncouth for long life." The same question, how wide a word space should be, got a different answer from Dowding half a century later (dowding1954finer), and neither rests on experiment.

### Implications for LTF

1. His "style-card" is the model for the LTF copy rulebook. A written code for units, abbreviations, capitalization, numerals and checklist wording is how consistency survives many authors. LTF's existing rules for US English, no em-dashes, and MSL/AGL labels are exactly this kind of code.
2. Readers notice inconsistency faster than they notice errors of substance. Drift in how a quantity is written across lessons will read as carelessness, which erodes trust.
3. Uppercase and small-cap labels need extra letterspacing and a bigger word space. That justifies CSS letter-spacing on all-caps widget labels and badges.
4. "Hounds'-teeth" are today's rivers in justified text, one more reason for ragged-right in narrow columns.

## johnston1906writing

Edward Johnston, *Writing & Illuminating, & Lettering* (New York: Macmillan; London: John Hogg, 1906; The Artistic Crafts Series of Technical Handbooks, edited by W. R. Lethaby).

### Core claims

Johnston's handbook argues that letters are best understood and made through the tool that formed them, the reed, quill or chisel, so the way to learn good lettering is to copy the best historical models with the proper tool rather than to "design" new alphabets. In his words, "To make good letters is not necessarily to 'design' them — they have been designed long ago — but it is to take the best letters we can find, and to acquire them and make them our own." He states the problem simply: "To make good letters and to arrange them well."

He names the "essential qualities of Lettering" as "legibility, beauty, and character." He complains that the recent revival of printing produced many attempts at "decorative" books that show "a vagueness of intention" and "an ignorance of Letter-craft."

### Specifics

The book is a practical manual, with sections on tools and materials (quill, desk, ink, parchment), on forming a formal hand, on the history of letters (Roman capitals, Uncials, Half-Uncials, Italic and the Roman small letter), on illumination, and a chapter on inscriptions in stone. A few points matter for typographers:

- Tool-first reasoning. Uncials "are true pen-forms," with "simple strokes and beautiful, rounded shapes which flow from the rightly handled reed or quill." Much of the character of historical scripts comes from how the pen is held (for example, "slanted-pen" writing).
- The Roman small letter was produced when Italian scribes went back about three hundred years for a model. Johnston uses this as the precedent for method without imitation: one may "follow a method without imitating a style."
- Spacing measured by the letters themselves: "The average space between two words is the width of the letter o," and writing lines are usually set about three times the height of the letter o apart, kept "as close together as is compatible with legibility," with descenders clearing ascenders of the line below.
- Rules are tools. "Rules are only Guides and ... Methods are suggested by the work itself"; too much fitting and planning "makes the work lifeless."

His preface footnote on legibility is the most reusable item: "our personal view of legibility is apt to favour custom and use unduly, for a quite bad, familiar writing may seem to us more readable than one that is far clearer in itself but unfamiliar."

### Replication and criticism

A craft handbook, not an experiment. Its historical account of letter origins reflects 1906 palaeography, so check dates and relationships of historical hands against later scholarship before reuse; I did not.

The legibility footnote anticipates what later reading research would discuss as the role of familiarity (lane 02 and lane 19 cover it); here it is craft observation only. The tool-first claim is carried forward and formalized in Noordzij's theory of the stroke (noordzij2005stroke).

### Implications for LTF

1. The legibility footnote is a standing warning to the LTF design team: what looks readable to people who already know the interface or the subject may not be readable to a beginner. Test lessons with true beginners, not with the team.
2. "The width of the letter o" is a yardstick that scales with the type, which suggests (my inference) em-based spacing in CSS rather than fixed pixels.
3. Descender and ascender clearance gives a minimum floor to line height for any text face, especially in dense instrument legends and chart labels.
4. "Rules are only guides" is a design-governance stance for a design knowledge base: treat each rule here as a default with a reason, and write down the reason so it can be overridden knowingly.
5. The preference for historical models fits a tradition-first choice of text faces for lesson pages, with the caveat that the tradition was formed on paper.

## bodoni1818manuale

Giambattista Bodoni, *Manuale tipografico* (Parma: Presso la Vedova, 1818; two quarto volumes, with a *Discorso* by his widow and a *Prefazione* by Bodoni). English translation of the preface: H. V. Marrot (London: Elkin Mathews, 1925).

### Core claims

The *Manuale* is a printer's specimen book that doubles as a manifesto. It displays the typefaces Bodoni cut and cast at Parma, and its preface states what he thought made type beautiful. Bodoni says there are "four different heads" under which the beauties of type are derived. I could confirm the wording of only the first two and part of a third from the translated preface as quoted on the Lawson Archive: regularity, "conformity without ambiguity, variety without dissonance"; "a second and not minor value ... from sharpness and definition, neatness and finish"; and the polish of a well-cast letter, which "should shine like a mirror on its face." A catalogue summary lists the four principles as uniformity of design, sharpness and neatness, good taste, and charm, but that is a paraphrase and I could not confirm the original wording of the last two.

He also praises "the beautiful contrast as between light and shade" produced by a well-cut pen, which explains the high thick-to-thin contrast of his roman letters.

### Specifics

Bodoni died in 1813; the 1818 edition was published five years later. Daniel Berkeley Updike, reading it in 1922, records that it was completed by Bodoni's widow and Luigi Orsi, who had been Bodoni's foreman for twenty years, that only 290 copies were printed, and that the widow wrote she could not sell them for less than 120 francs. The first volume holds roman and italic Latin types over 144 pages, running from small to very large bodies, sometimes with as many as fourteen varieties of the same body in different weights of line. Updike doubts so many near-identical kinds were needed, but approves the finely graduated sizes, which let Bodoni "place on his pages, not approximately, but exactly, the size of type he wished to employ." The second volume holds Greek, exotic, German and Russian types, borders, mathematical and astronomical signs and music.

Updike's judgments: the work is "probably the most elaborate specimen that the world has ever seen" and "the acme of Bodoni's late, chilly, dry manner." He distinguishes an early period of old-style types with profuse decoration from a later one of his own designs and "unadorned typography." He traces the "classical" look to Grandjean's thin straight serif, to Baskerville's modelled types and to enthusiasm for antiquity.

### Replication and criticism

The preface is a statement of taste by a printer selling his skill, so it has no empirical standing. Critics have attacked both its claims and its type. An unattributed web essay I fetched calls the *Manuale* "a brilliant and circumstantial sales talk," faults Bodoni's faces for "excessive contrast" and "hairline serifs" that needed wide spacing, extra leading and the best paper, and calls him a creature of fashion. I could not identify its author, so treat that account as low-weight. Morison's rule that the eye cannot easily read many words made of strongly contrasted thicks and thins (morison1936first) and Updike's own list that bans hair-line types (updike1922printing) point the same way, as does a web excerpt of Dowding saying modern faces need generous leading and narrow measures (dowding1954finer). All three are craft opinion, not measured results. The related experimental questions on contrast and legibility sit in lanes 02 and 03.

### Implications for LTF

1. "Regularity" as conformity without ambiguity and variety without dissonance is a compact statement of what a component library should do: consistent rhythm with controlled variation.
2. The graduated size range, exact sizes rather than approximate ones, supports an explicit type scale in the design tokens, with each size chosen for a purpose rather than adjusted ad hoc.
3. High-contrast "Didone" faces carry luxury and fashion connotations and have fragile hairlines; for a training product used on many screens and in dim rooms, restrict them to large display or avoid them.
4. "Sharpness and definition" is a rendering requirement: icons, gauge ticks and hairline rules must remain crisp at 1x pixel densities; test them there.

## tschichold1995new

Jan Tschichold, *The New Typography: A Handbook for Modern Designers* (Berkeley: University of California Press, 1995; translated by Ruari McLean, with an introduction by Robin Kinross). German original *Die neue Typographie*, Berlin, 1928.

### Core claims

The book is the programmatic statement of the New Typography. Its opening position, in the excerpt I read: "The essence of the New Typography is clarity." That clarity is set against an old typography whose aim was "beauty," and it is justified by the volume of print competing for attention: the "extraordinary amount of print" demands "the greatest economy of expression." Tschichold's method is that visible form should be developed "out of the functions of the text," with "form" created from function as in technology and nature. He names the function of printed text as communication, emphasis ("word value") and the logical sequence of contents.

From that follow his two main rules. Reject the central axis, which he calls an "artificial, invisible backbone" imposed from outside and illogical because readers start at one side of a line. And prefer asymmetry: "Asymmetry is the rhythmic expression of functional design," more logical and "far more optically effective than symmetry." Ornament is rejected as "an ignorant tendency that our century must repress."

### Specifics

The reader excerpt (Armstrong, *Graphic Design Theory*, pages 35 to 38) gives these concrete points:

- Hierarchy comes from content: "Every part of a text relates to every other part by a definite, logical relationship of emphasis and value, predetermined by content." The typographer expresses it through type size and weight, arrangement of lines, color and photography, and must study "how his work is read and ought to be read."
- Asymmetry is not chaos: "A striving for order can, and must, also be expressed in asymmetrical form." It gives unlimited scope for variation, unlike the single central-axis form.
- The New Typography "encourages 'standardization' in the construction of units, as in building."
- Objectivity is not poverty. A letterhead with no ornament and legible type is called "meagerness," and its failure shows "the hollowness of the old principles."
- A redesign example: a newspaper advertisement criticized for ornaments, seven kinds of type and sizes and centered design is redone with no ornament and "only 5 different types," with "good legibility."

The reader's headnote adds context. Tschichold, then twenty-one, saw the 1923 Bauhaus exhibition at Weimar, advocated the new typography for a decade, and was later imprisoned by the Nazis and escaped to Basel. The headnote says he then "turned back to the classical typography of his early training" and sensed "an element of fascism" in the movement's purifying order.

### Replication and criticism

A manifesto and handbook, not an experiment. Its claims about optical effectiveness are assertions. Tschichold himself revised them: a digest of *The Form of the Book* reports that in later essays he said book typography has no "new" rules, only centuries-old principles (tschichold1991form). Another detail from that digest conflicts with Eye's dating of the same essays, so treat essay dates there with caution.

Robin Kinross, via Matthew Carter's review in Eye, calls the chapters on the new typography "the heart of the book," records Tschichold's programme "Standardization, instead of individualism," and corrects a common assumption: "the Bauhaus had no fully fledged typographers" (Tschichold, with Renner and Trump, taught in Munich). For the opposing craft view, Updike's list of types to avoid includes all sans serif (updike1922printing), and Morison demands near-absolute obedience to convention in book typography (morison1936first).

### Implications for LTF

1. Ask of every screen what its communication, emphasis and sequence are, then make size, weight and position carry exactly that. This is a usable design-review question.
2. App screens, dashboards and widgets resemble the advertising and display work in Tschichold's examples more than book pages. His rules (asymmetry, left alignment, no ornament, few sizes) fit those surfaces better than lesson prose; this is my judgement.
3. "Standardization" is the rationale for design tokens and shared components.
4. His warning about meagerness applies to minimal dashboards: removing everything is not clarity.
5. The five-types limit in the redesigned advertisement is not an LTF rule, but a small, deliberate set of sizes is.

## tschichold1991form

Jan Tschichold, *The Form of the Book: Essays on the Morality of Good Design*, translated by Hajo Hadeler, edited with an introduction by Robert Bringhurst (Point Roberts, WA: Hartley & Marks, 1991; UK edition London: Lund Humphries, 1991). German essays first collected in Basel by Birkhäuser, 1975.

### Core claims

The essays record the later Tschichold, who after the New Typography argued for the traditional book. Kinross's review in *Eye* (no. 7, Summer 1992) summarizes the stance: typography is a modest, self-effacing art that serves the reader, and it joins practice, aesthetics, ethics and politics. Book pages should follow "natural" geometric proportions used since the medieval period, not arbitrary formats. Kinross reports that Tschichold treats indenting paragraph openings as essential, because omitting indents sacrifices meaning for superficial neatness.

The contrast with his 1928 programme is the main historical point of the book: the same author who dismissed ornament and symmetry as obsolete (tschichold1995new) here defends convention for continuous reading. He gives no experimental basis for either position.

### Specifics

Essays named in Kinross's review: "Consistent correlation between book page and type area," the major essay, known to British readers since 1936 as "Non-arbitrary proportions of page and type area"; "Typesetting superscript numbers and footnotes"; "Ten common mistakes in the production of books"; and short pieces on quotation marks, dashes and the "consequences of tight typesetting." The editor left out two essays on signature marks and spine titling.

The proportions material is the part most often cited. Kinross says the 2:3 (octavo) and 3:4 (quarto) are the "natural" pair, and the Wikipedia account of page canons states that the Van de Graaf construction gives margins of one-ninth and two-ninths of the page, inner margin half the outer, and for a 2:3 page the proportions 2:3:4:6 for inner, top, outer and bottom. Tschichold is the main popularizer of these canons; the page canons article notes he speculated the method "may be older" than its discovery, that its source article by Van de Graaf (1946) was "not examined" by the writer, that his own medieval framework gives margin proportions of 1:1:2:3, and that the golden-ratio version he called "hardly useful today."

A web digest of the essays, which I treat as a secondary account that disagrees with Eye on essay dates, adds these rules: lines of about eight to twelve words; word spacing "three-to-em or even more compressed"; indented paragraphs, usually one em; roman capitals always letterspaced; legibility and clarity before a smooth page contour; and a printer's stock of old-style roman with italics from 6 to 72 point.

### Replication and criticism

Craft authority, not experiment. Kinross's review is mixed on the edition: the American edition is larger than the 1975 original, uses imitation laid paper, has a square back that contradicts Tschichold's own guidance, and some translations overstate the German (häßlich rendered as "repulsive"). He also finds the collection arbitrary and repetitive, and the editor and translator too attached to "fine writing and fine printing," when Tschichold's own concerns were more ordinary. The page canons article records the historical reservations: that the Van de Graaf canon is a reconstruction of a method that "may have been used," and that a measured Gutenberg page (ratio 0.690) is closer to 2:3 than to the golden ratio. These canons are a craft heritage, not proof that proportions drive readability.

### Implications for LTF

1. Proportion is a rule for page margins in print. On screens the equivalent decision is the reading column's width relative to the viewport and to the type size. LTF can borrow the relational idea (margins derived from one ratio) without borrowing book measurements.
2. His indent rule has a web counterpart: mark paragraph beginnings clearly by either an indent or paragraph spacing, but not neither. LTF should pick one and apply it everywhere.
3. Uppercase letterspacing and eight-to-twelve-word lines agree with other lane sources (devinne1910practice, gill1936essay), which strengthens them as defaults, with the caveat that each is craft opinion until lane 02 evidence is checked.
4. Treat his "morality of good design" as an explicit design value: serve the reader, resist novelty for its own sake. That aligns with a beginner-training product.
5. Because the essays disagree with his earlier manifesto, write LTF guidance as defaults per content type (prose, UI, display), not as one universal doctrine.
