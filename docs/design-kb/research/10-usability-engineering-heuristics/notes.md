## Gould1985

**Core claims.** John Gould (IBM Research) and Clayton Lewis argued that a system will be usable only if its development follows three principles: an early and continual focus on users, empirical measurement of usage, and iterative design. They then asked what designers thought of these principles and found that practitioners often did not hold them, even though the authors considered them nearly self-evident. The paper is both a prescription and a diagnosis: it says what to do and why people do not do it.

**Specifics.** Early focus on users means more than a requirements document. It includes direct contact with intended users (interviews, observing them at work, bringing them into the design team), thinking about their characteristics and tasks, and having users do real work with early simulations or prototypes. Empirical measurement means that from the start, prototypes, manuals and interfaces are tested with real users, with performance and reactions recorded and quantified, and with explicit measurable usability targets set up front. Iterative design means that the cycle of design, test, redesign is repeated as often as the schedule allows, because the authors held that it is impossible to get the interface right at the first attempt. The behavioral half of the paper reports surveys of designers asked how they would develop a new system. Most named only some of the principles, few named all three, and many gave reasons against them: that a good designer can get it right the first time, that user contact slows work, that users do not know what they want, that testing is for the end. The authors take each objection seriously and respond with evidence from their own projects. The 1984 Olympic Message System, a voice-messaging system built by an IBM team for athletes, was the exemplar of the method, with user tests of paper manuals and simulated prototypes before coding; it was later written up by Gould and colleagues in a separate paper (1987). The exact sample sizes of the designer surveys were not re-confirmed this session.

**Quotations.** None, to avoid misquoting.

**Criticism and status.** The three principles became the backbone of user-centered design and are reflected in ISO 13407 and the later ISO 9241-210. Critics note that the designer surveys were small and conducted inside one large company, that the paper offers no way to prioritize what testing finds, and that iteration is costly when architecture is hard to change. Later research (the CUE studies in this lane) shows that testing does not guarantee the right problems are found. The core claim has not been seriously contested; whether it is applied is a matter of practice.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Treat each lesson, widget and challenge as a hypothesis to be tried with beginners, not as finished content. (2) Set measurable lesson targets before building, for example the share of novices who finish the first challenge without hints. (3) Budget at least two test-and-fix rounds for every lesson before it is published. (4) Keep a design log of what was tried and changed; it becomes part of the knowledge base. (5) Guard against the "obvious to me" bias: the owner knows flying and the beginner does not, so the owner's reaction is weak evidence. (6) Bring beginners in early, when paper or clickable sketches are enough.

## Nielsen1993Usability

**Core claims.** Jakob Nielsen's textbook presents usability as an engineering discipline: it can be specified, measured and improved through a lifecycle of methods that fit real budgets. Usability is not one thing; it has several attributes, and "usefulness" is made up of utility (does it do what is needed) and usability (how well can people use it). Because full laboratory methods are often skipped, the book argues for "discount usability engineering": cheap, fast methods that teams will actually use.

**Specifics.** The five usability attributes are learnability (easy for novices to get started), efficiency (high productivity for experts), memorability (casual users can return without relearning), errors (low error rate and easy recovery, with no catastrophic errors), and satisfaction (pleasant to use). Nielsen's lifecycle runs from knowing the user, competitive analysis and setting usability goals through parallel design, participatory design, coordinated design of the total interface, applying guidelines, prototyping, empirical testing, iterative design and collecting feedback from field use. Discount usability engineering has three techniques: user and task scenarios in place of full prototypes, simplified think-aloud testing, and heuristic evaluation. The book proposes severity ratings from 0 to 4 (not a problem, cosmetic, minor, major, catastrophe) based on frequency, impact and persistence of a problem, and recommends several independent raters. It advocates writing target levels per attribute in advance (for example a percentage of novices completing a task within a set time) and comparing against them. It discusses the thinking-aloud method at length and encourages testing with a small number of participants per round (a point later developed in Nielsen and Landauer 1993). Other themes include user and task analysis, guidelines and style guides, and the case for consistency inside and across applications.

**Criticism and status.** The book is a synthesis of earlier research and Nielsen's own experience, not an empirical study. Its two best-known operational claims, that five users suffice and that heuristic evaluation is cost-effective, have both been qualified by later work (see Nielsen1993Model and Molich2008CUE4). It was written for 1990s desktop software and does not cover mobile, social or learning products. The five attributes remain among the most-taught breakdowns of usability and map onto the effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction of ISO 9241-11.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Use the five attributes as a page-level checklist: lessons need learnability and memorability, fly mode needs efficiency and error recovery. (2) Write numeric goals per lesson: success rate, time on task, hints used. (3) Triage design-review findings with the 0 to 4 severity scale so the owner fixes catastrophes before cosmetics. (4) Apply discount methods: five beginners, scenarios, a quick think-aloud, a heuristic pass. (5) Measure satisfaction with a questionnaire (SUS, see Brooke1996) rather than asking "do you like it". (6) Remember memorability: learners return after days away, so pages should not depend on remembering last session's hidden state.

## Nielsen1990Heuristic

**Core claims.** Heuristic evaluation is a method in which a small number of evaluators inspect an interface and judge it against recognized usability principles. Nielsen and Molich showed that it finds a worthwhile share of problems cheaply, but that a single evaluator finds only a minority, so several evaluators should work independently and their findings be pooled.

**Specifics.** The paper reports four experiments with different interfaces and evaluator groups; the interface names given in the paper are Teledata, Mantel, Savings and Transport. Evaluators were mostly students (some with computer-science training, some with usability expertise). Individual evaluators found between roughly one fifth and one half of the known problems depending on the interface (from about 20 to about 51 percent, quoted from the author's memory of the paper and not re-confirmed this session). The proportion found by pooled groups rises quickly for the first few evaluators and flattens: aggregates of around five evaluators found well over half of the problems, and the authors recommended using three to five evaluators. Evaluators varied widely, and the problems found by one evaluator were often not found by others, which is why pooling works. Easy problems were found by most evaluators and hard ones by few. The nine heuristics used were: simple and natural dialogue, speak the user's language, minimize the user's memory load, consistency, feedback, clearly marked exits, shortcuts, good error messages, and prevent errors. Evaluators were advised to go through the interface at least twice, once for overall flow and once for individual elements, and to write down each problem together with the heuristic it breaks. Evaluators should finish independently before comparing notes.

**Criticism and status.** The cost-effectiveness claim has been repeated in many studies. The reliability and validity of the method have been criticized: Gray and Salzman (1998) argued that the early comparison studies had weaknesses, and Cockton and Woolrych argued that heuristic evaluation produces many false alarms and misses problems real users hit. Molich's CUE studies find low overlap between expert reports (see Molich2008CUE4). The heuristics themselves were superseded by the 1994 revision (Nielsen1994Heuristics). The method is best seen as a supplement to user testing.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Review each new page type with three independent reviewers (owner, designer, a flight instructor or a novice) before testing with learners. (2) Write down which heuristic every issue breaks so the team learns where its habits fail. (3) Keep an LTF-specific checklist derived from the heuristics. (4) Confirm serious findings with a real beginner before spending effort on a fix. (5) Pool and de-duplicate findings, then rate severity. (6) Do two passes: one for flow through a lesson, one for individual widgets.

## Nielsen1994Heuristics

**Core claims.** Nielsen compared several published heuristic sets against a database of real usability problems to find which principles best explain actual problems, and derived a revised set of ten heuristics. A secondary finding is that heuristics are more useful for explaining problems than for finding them, and that no short list explains everything.

**Specifics.** The study drew on a large database of problems collected from real projects (the commonly quoted figures are about 249 problems from 11 projects, and a pool of about 101 candidate heuristics drawn from seven published sets; these figures come from memory and were not re-confirmed this session). Sets compared included Molich and Nielsen's 1990 list, Nielsen's earlier list, Smith and Mosier's guidelines, Shneiderman's eight golden rules, Norman's design principles, and others. Problems were rated against each heuristic and a factor analysis was done. No individual factor was strong, and the seven-factor solution explained only about a third of the variance (about 30 percent, not re-confirmed). The ten refined heuristics, built from the factors and the best fitting items, did better than any single set. Nielsen also found that the heuristics explained serious problems better than minor ones, and that evaluators with usability expertise were better than novices. The ten heuristics are: visibility of system status; match between system and the real world; user control and freedom; consistency and standards; error prevention; recognition rather than recall; flexibility and efficiency of use; aesthetic and minimalist design; help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors; and help and documentation.

**Criticism and status.** These are the most reproduced usability list in practice, and they are often taught as if they were a complete theory. They are generic, partial and not mutually exclusive; they cover learnability thinly; and "aesthetic and minimalist design" is easily misread as a call for plain design when Nielsen meant that irrelevant information competes with relevant information. The quantitative claim that they explain a large share of problems should be treated cautiously, as the paper itself stresses how much remains unexplained.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Rewrite each heuristic as a question about LTF: does the learner always know whether the sim step worked (visibility); do words and checklists match the real Cessna (real world); can the learner leave, replay and restart a lesson (control). (2) Consistency means one pattern per task across lessons and fly mode. (3) Recognition over recall: show checklist items and V-speeds on screen rather than expecting memory. (4) Help belongs where the learner is stuck, not in a separate manual. (5) Use the list to name problems in reviews, not to generate the design. (6) Add learnability checks the list lacks.

## Nielsen1993Model

**Core claims.** Nielsen and Landauer (Bellcore) proposed that the number of usability problems found grows with the number of test users or evaluators along a saturating curve. Most of the value of one round of testing comes from the first few participants, so several small rounds with fixes in between beat one large round.

**Specifics.** The model is Found(i) = N(1 - (1 - L)^i): N is the total number of problems in the interface, L is the proportion found by a single participant, and i is the number of participants. It treats each problem as found independently with probability L in each session. The authors fitted it to data from a set of projects (the commonly quoted figure is 11 studies, covering both heuristic evaluation and user testing; the mean L for user testing is quoted as about 31 percent, with large variation between projects; these figures were not re-confirmed this session). With L about 31 percent, five users find about 85 percent of problems and the curve flattens after that. The paper also adds a cost model: each added participant costs time, each problem found has a value, and the benefit-to-cost ratio is highest at a small number of participants. The optimum depends on the project, and the paper's quoted optimum for the typical project was about three to four users (about 3.2 for the cited case, not re-confirmed). Nielsen later popularized the advice as "test with five users", and recommended running three small tests of five rather than one of fifteen.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** This is among the most contested claims in usability. The model assumes that all problems are equally easy to find and that participants are statistically independent, but L varies with product complexity and task choice. Spool and Schroeder (2001) found that five users found only a fraction of the problems on large commercial sites; Woolrych and Cockton (2001) criticized the assumptions about problem visibility; Faulkner (2003) sampled many sets of five from a pool of 60 participants and found that the share of problems found by sets of five varied widely, from a little over half to nearly all (range not re-confirmed). Lindgaard and Chattratichart (2007) argued that task coverage matters more than user count. Nielsen's own defense is that the aim is iteration, not coverage in one round. A fair reading: five is a reasonable size for a cheap iterative round on a focused task, not a guarantee of 85 percent.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Plan many small rounds of three to five beginners and fix issues between rounds. (2) Do not claim a share of problems found from a participant count. (3) Choose tasks to cover the main journeys: first lesson, fly mode with the sim open, a checklist, a challenge, the dashboard. (4) Recruit true novices; experienced pilots find different problems. (5) Segment participants if the product has distinct groups (nervous beginners, sim veterans) and test each. (6) Record the problems each round found so the "new problems per user" rate shows when the round has saturated.

## Krug2000

**Core claims.** Steve Krug's first law of usability is "don't make me think": every page should be self-evident, or failing that self-explanatory. He argues that web users do not read, they scan; they do not choose the best option, they take the first reasonable one (satisficing, after Herbert Simon); and they do not work out how things function, they muddle through. Designers should therefore design for scanning and remove every unnecessary question a page asks.

**Specifics.** Principles include: create a clear visual hierarchy (more important means more prominent, related things are grouped, nested things are visually nested); take advantage of conventions; break pages into clearly defined areas; make it obvious what is clickable; minimize noise (competing shouts, disorder, clutter). Krug's rule of thumb is that the number of clicks matters less than how little thinking each click needs, and he describes the "mindless click" as a trade of effort the user is happy to make. Words: omit needless words, and cut "happy talk" (self-congratulatory introductions) and instructions nobody reads. Navigation: persistent navigation, page names that match the link, "you are here" indicators and the "trunk test" in which you are dropped onto a random page and should be able to tell what site it is, what page it is, the major sections, the local options, where you are in the scheme, and how to search. The "reservoir of goodwill" is a metaphor: each difficulty drains a user's goodwill and each helpful touch refills it. On testing, Krug argues for a morning of testing a month with about three participants, observed by the team in the room next door and followed by a lunchtime debrief of the three worst problems to fix before next month. The book is short, illustrated and meant to be read in an evening. The third edition (2014) added mobile and accessibility chapters.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The book is experience-based advice, not research; its behavioral claims (scanning, satisficing) agree with studies of web reading and eye tracking by Nielsen and others but Krug supplies no data of his own. The advice is tuned to task-oriented websites. For learning content, a certain kind of thinking is the goal and should not be removed; the trick is to remove interface thinking while leaving content thinking (a boundary with the cognitive-load lane).

**Use for LTF.** (1) Spend the learner's effort on flying concepts, not on working out the app. (2) Make the one primary action on each lesson page obvious in size, position and label. (3) Cut introductory text; test by asking a beginner to find the next step within five seconds. (4) Run the trunk test on lesson pages, challenge pages and fly mode. (5) Adopt a standing monthly test morning with three beginners. (6) Keep conventions where they exist (links look like links, buttons like buttons) and spend originality on brand, not on control behavior.

## Norman1988

**Core claims.** Donald Norman argues that the burden of understanding sits with the designer: when people cannot work something, the design is at fault. He describes two gulfs: the gulf of execution (how to do what I want) and the gulf of evaluation (what happened and does it match my goal). Good design makes the state visible and gives feedback. Human error should be expected and designed for.

**Specifics.** The seven stages of action are: form the goal, form the intention (plan), specify the action sequence, execute, perceive the state, interpret it, and compare with the goal. The first three and the execution bridge the gulf of execution; the last three bridge the gulf of evaluation. Errors are divided into slips (the intention was right, the action wrong) and mistakes (the intention itself was wrong), following James Reason and Rasmussen. Slips include capture errors (a frequent sequence takes over), description errors (acting on an object that looks like the right one), data-driven errors, associative-activation errors, loss-of-activation errors and mode errors (the same action means different things in different modes). Mistakes include rule-based mistakes (a wrong rule or a misread situation), knowledge-based mistakes (incomplete or wrong knowledge) and memory-lapse mistakes. Design responses include constraints (physical, logical, semantic, cultural), forcing functions (interlocks, lock-ins, lockouts), making actions reversible, good feedback, visible state and removing modes. Norman prefers undo to confirmation dialogs, because people confirm without reading. The principles the book emphasizes are visibility, feedback, constraints, mapping, consistency and affordances; the 2013 edition added signifiers (lane 11 covers affordance theory). He also advises that when something goes wrong, ask what in the design allowed the error, not who is to blame.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The error taxonomy is borrowed from cognitive psychology (Reason 1990; Rasmussen 1983) and applied informally; the book offers case studies rather than experiments. Norman himself later said that "affordance" had been misused in design and added signifiers. The seven-stage model is a teaching device and is not supported as an exact description of cognition. Still, the gulfs and the slip versus mistake distinction are standard parts of teaching.

**Use for LTF.** (1) After every learner action, show what happened (feedback) and what to do next. (2) Prefer undo and retry over confirmation dialogs. (3) Avoid modes; where fly mode and lesson mode differ, mark the mode in an unmistakable way. (4) In challenges, tell apart slips (a wrong key pressed) and mistakes (the wrong plan) and give different help for each. (5) Never word failure as the learner's fault. (6) Use constraints: disable or hide impossible actions rather than letting the learner trigger an error.

## Lewis1986Error

**Core claims.** Clayton Lewis and Donald Norman argue that because errors cannot be eliminated, interfaces should be designed for error: prevent those that can be prevented, make the rest easy to detect, and make their consequences reversible or minor. The chapter belongs to the book that introduced "user-centered system design" and gives a design-oriented view of the slips and mistakes taxonomy.

**Specifics.** The authors classify errors (slips, mistakes, and further sub-types) and ask what design can do for each. Their principles, as commonly summarized: make it possible to reverse actions; make it harder to carry out irreversible actions; make errors easier to discover; and change the nature of the interaction so that errors are less costly. Methods to reduce slips include reducing similarity between commands or objects that can be confused, avoiding modes that change what an action does, using defaults and reducing the amount of lookup needed. They note that "are you sure" confirmations tend to be answered automatically and therefore protect little, while the ability to undo protects well. They discuss using knowledge of typical errors in the system itself, for example to detect a command that is a near neighbor of a common one, and to help the user work out what went wrong. The chapter also considers how people explain their own errors and how explanations can mislead. The chapter number in the book was not confirmed, the page range 411-432 was.

**Criticism and status.** A conceptual chapter that has been cited for decades; it contains no experiments. Its examples come from 1980s command-line and early graphical systems. Later error research (Reason, Rasmussen, Senders and Moray, and the safety-critical literature in the aviation lanes) extends the ideas; "design for error" appears in most guidelines, including Nielsen's error prevention heuristic and ISO 9241-110's error tolerance.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Make every challenge attempt repeatable at no cost. (2) Make rare destructive actions (reset all progress) harder than common safe ones. (3) Make errors discoverable by showing expected and actual sim state side by side. (4) In quizzes, explain why a wrong answer was attractive. (5) Offer undo for any state change in settings or progress. (6) Choose defaults carefully; most learners will keep them.

## Brooke1996

**Core claims.** John Brooke's System Usability Scale (SUS) is a ten-item questionnaire that yields one score from 0 to 100 for the overall perceived usability of a system. It was designed to be "quick and dirty": cheap to give, easy to score and good enough to compare products or versions.

**Specifics.** The ten items alternate between positive and negative statements, each rated on a five-point agreement scale (strongly disagree to strongly agree). Examples are "I think that I would like to use this system frequently" and "I found the system unnecessarily complex". Scoring: for the odd (positive) items subtract 1 from the response; for the even (negative) items subtract the response from 5; add the ten contributions and multiply by 2.5, giving 0 to 100. The result is not a percentage and should not be read as one. Brooke developed SUS at Digital Equipment Corporation in the mid-1980s (1986 is quoted) and published it in 1996. Participants should fill it in immediately after using the system, before debriefing, and respond quickly with their first reaction; missing answers should be avoided. SUS is technology agnostic and has been applied to software, websites, hardware and voice systems. Later studies (Tullis and Stetson 2004 among them) found that it reached stable results with samples as small as 8 to 12 participants (not re-confirmed). Common modifications include replacing "cumbersome" in item 8 with "awkward" and replacing "system" with "website" or the product name.

**Criticism and status.** Early factor analyses suggested two factors (usability and learnability; Lewis and Sauro 2009); later work suggested that the split reflects item wording rather than two constructs, and a single dimension is now commonly assumed. SUS measures perceived usability and says how good, not why; it needs paired observation. Scores depend on prior experience and on brand. The page range 189-194 and the chapter details were from memory and not confirmed this session.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Give SUS after each beginner's first complete session and track it across versions. (2) Change only the product name in the items, so scores stay comparable. (3) Do not over-read a score from fewer than about a dozen respondents. (4) Pair it with task success, hint counts and observation. (5) Report scores with their bands (see Bangor2008). (6) Keep learner-facing surveys short; SUS takes about a minute.

## Bangor2008

**Core claims.** Bangor, Kortum and Miller analyzed SUS scores gathered over about ten years to show that SUS is reliable and that its scores relate to adjectives people use for a product. The paper is the usual source for describing a SUS score in words and for acceptability ranges.

**Specifics.** The data set held on the order of 2,300 SUS questionnaires from about 200 evaluations (the abstract and citing papers give 2,324 individual cases and over 200 studies) of many products: web pages, cell phones, interactive voice response systems, hardware and software. The mean SUS across studies was about 70.1 (a 99.9 percent confidence interval of about 68.7 to 71.5 is reported by Lewis and Sauro), and the distribution was skewed toward higher scores. The authors added an eleventh item, a single seven-point adjective rating of the whole product from "worst imaginable" to "best imaginable", and found a correlation with SUS of about 0.82. The mean SUS score for each adjective was approximately: awful 20, poor 36, OK 51, good 71, excellent 86, best imaginable 91 (these means were not re-confirmed this session). They proposed acceptability ranges: not acceptable (below about 50), marginal (roughly 50 to 70) and acceptable (above about 70), and compared them to school grades. They also found that the word "cumbersome" in item 8 confused about 10 percent of respondents (reported in citing work). Another finding was that SUS ratings were often more favorable than success rates in the same tests.

**Criticism and status.** The mapping is widely used but rests on data weighted to telecom products and their own studies. Sauro and Lewis propose a curved grading scale in which the average of around 68 is a C and about 80 or more is an A. The adjective scale has not been validated in all languages. Correlation between SUS and objective performance is moderate, so a high score does not prove tasks are completed.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Treat 70 as the floor for the beginner flow and 80 as a goal. (2) Use the adjective item to translate numbers for the owner. (3) Compare version to version rather than only against external benchmarks. (4) Keep the observation: scores can overstate success. (5) Do not treat band boundaries as sharp cutoffs. (6) Add the adjective item if a quick verbal summary is useful.

## Grossman2009

**Core claims.** Grossman, Fitzmaurice and Attar (Autodesk Research) observe that learnability is widely accepted as an important part of usability yet there is little agreement on how to define, measure or evaluate it. They survey the literature, organize definitions, metrics and methods, propose a new evaluation protocol and classify the kinds of learnability problems users have.

**Specifics.** The survey covered 88 papers (76 from CHI and 12 from TOCHI, 1982 to 2008) and found eight distinct uses of the term, which split along a main distinction: initial learnability (the performance of a new user on first encounters) versus extended learnability (how performance changes with continued use). Metrics were grouped into task metrics (time, errors, success, number of attempts, task completion), command metrics (number of commands used, command recall), mental metrics (cognitive workload, mental models), subjective metrics (questionnaires, ratings) and rule-based or knowledge metrics. Methodologies run from formal experiments over time to think-aloud, and the authors added a new "question-suggestion" protocol: the participant may ask the experimenter any question, and the experimenter answers and may volunteer suggestions when the participant is stuck. In a user study this protocol uncovered significantly more learnability issues than a traditional think-aloud protocol. From their data they classified learnability issues; their five categories, as the author recalls them, are task flow, locating, awareness, understanding and transition (the category names were not re-confirmed this session). Guidelines follow from each category, for instance making functionality discoverable and giving feedback on task flow. The paper was a best paper nominee at CHI 2009.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** This is a survey and a modest user study, not a meta-analysis; the question-suggestion protocol was tested with a small number of participants and with complex professional software, so generalization to a lightweight learning site is untested. The distinction between initial and extended learning has become a standard way to frame the topic.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Measure initial learnability (first-session success) and extended learnability (speed and errors across later sessions) as separate outcomes. (2) Run question-suggestion sessions: let beginners ask aloud and log every question as a possible gap in the lesson. (3) Classify findings by category (could they find it, did they notice it, did they understand it, did the flow work). (4) Plot error and time curves across lessons to see whether the course teaches or just ends. (5) Treat a question asked twice by different learners as a defect in the content. (6) Use consistent locations so that learners can locate features once and reuse that knowledge.

## Ceaparu2004

**Core claims.** Ceaparu, Lazar, Bessiere, Robinson and Shneiderman (University of Maryland) measured how often computer users get frustrated, what causes it and how severe it is. Their finding is that frustrating experiences are frequent and that users lose a substantial share of their computer time to them.

**Specifics.** The study had 111 participants who recorded frustrating experiences, using logs and self-report during real work in more than one setting, and rated the severity of each. Time lost to frustrating experiences was reported at about 47 to 53 percent of time on the computer, depending on location and method, and it stayed above 38 percent when extreme cases were removed. The most-cited causes were error messages, dropped network connections, long download times and hard-to-find features. Other causes included pop-up windows, freezing, unclear or missing help and email problems. Severity ratings were higher when the user was in the middle of a task they cared about and when work had been lost. Participants commonly blamed themselves for problems that were caused by the system. The authors argued that designers should pay more attention to the quality of error messages, to reliability, to progress indicators and to findability. The work built on a 2002 technical report from the same group (HCIL-TR-2002-11 and UMIACS-TR-2002-51).

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The time-lost figures are self-reported estimates, made in an era of dial-up and pop-up advertising, and probably overstate absolute losses compared with log-based measures. The sample was largely students and staff in one region. The finding that unclear messages, missing features and waiting recur has been supported by later studies of frustration and by the usability literature; Lazar's own later work on "user frustration" (Lazar, Jones and Shneiderman 2006, on the severity of frustration) extends it. The exact severity statistics were not re-checked.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Write error and empty-state copy that says what happened and what to do next. (2) Never lose a learner's progress; autosave and say so. (3) Make features findable in fly mode, where the learner's attention is on the sim. (4) Keep waiting short and show progress when loading lessons or widgets. (5) Do not make a beginner feel at fault: wording matters. (6) Track support questions and abandonment points as frustration indicators.

## Bastien1993

**Core claims.** Bastien and Scapin (INRIA, France) assembled a structured set of ergonomic criteria for evaluating interfaces, drawn from the guidelines literature and organized to be more precise and complete than a short heuristic list. Each criterion has a definition, a rationale, examples and comments.

**Specifics.** Version 2.1 of the criteria, dated May 1993, runs to about 79 pages. There are eight main criteria. Guidance covers prompting, grouping and distinction of items (by location and by format), immediate feedback and legibility. Workload covers brevity (conciseness and minimal actions) and information density. Explicit control covers explicit user action and user control. Adaptability covers flexibility and user experience (the interface should suit users of different experience levels). Error management covers error protection, the quality of error messages and error correction. Consistency covers sameness of procedures, labels, codes and layout across the interface. Significance of codes means that codes, names and abbreviations should be meaningful to the user. Compatibility means that the interface should fit the user's characteristics, tasks and environment. The main criteria are broken down into elementary criteria (about 18, as the HAL record notes). A later experiment by the same authors (1995, in the International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction) compared evaluation with the criteria to evaluation without them; the finding, as recalled, was that evaluators using the criteria detected more problems, notably those linked to criteria; sample sizes and exact effects were not re-confirmed this session.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The set is more complete but slower to use than ten heuristics, and it has had more influence on European practice than on English-language textbooks. The criteria overlap (feedback sits under guidance, control and error handling in different forms) and no ranking of importance is given. They are guidelines for evaluators, not tested predictors of user success.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Use "significance of codes" to check icons, colors, abbreviations and V-speed names against what beginners understand. (2) "Compatibility" supports matching the real Cessna panel and checklists. (3) "Workload" and "information density" are good lenses for fly mode. (4) Use the criteria as a second review pass after heuristics, when a page looks fine but feels hard. (5) "Prompting" informs hints and next-step cues. (6) Use "adaptability" to plan for returning learners seeing less hand-holding.

## GerhardtPowals1996

**Core claims.** Jill Gerhardt-Powals proposed ten cognitive engineering principles drawn from cognitive science and tested whether an interface built on them outperforms others. The claim is that applying principles from cognitive research to display design measurably improves human-computer performance.

**Specifics.** The ten principles are: automate unwanted workload; reduce uncertainty; fuse data (bring information together); present new information with meaningful aids to interpretation; use names that are conceptually related to function; group data in consistently meaningful ways; limit data-driven tasks; include in the displays only the information needed by the user at a given time; provide multiple coding of data when appropriate; and practice judicious redundancy. In the experiment, participants worked with three interfaces (the abstract describes three: an original design, one following standard HCI guidelines and one applying the ten principles) on a simulated problem-solving task, and measures covered speed, accuracy and workload ratings. The reported result was that the cognitive-engineering interface led to better performance and lower workload, and a post-experiment analysis found a strong correlation between performance and how far the ten principles had been applied across the three interfaces. The work draws on the author's 1993 Drexel University doctoral dissertation. The sample sizes and exact effects could not be re-confirmed this session.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** One experiment, a small sample of students, one task domain, and an interface where several design differences varied together, so effects cannot be traced to individual principles. Some principles (automate, fuse data) are for monitoring displays rather than for learning content. In heuristic set comparisons the list rates well on cognitive issues but is far less used than Nielsen's.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Fuse data: show airspeed together with its meaning against the white and green arcs, not as a bare number. (2) In fly mode show only what the current phase of flight needs. (3) Code critical information in more than one way (color plus shape plus text), which also helps color-blind users. (4) Apply judicious redundancy to safety-relevant limits. (5) Automate arithmetic and conversions the lesson is not teaching. (6) Name things by function ("Flaps up") rather than by internal labels.

## Molich2008CUE4

**Core claims.** In the Comparative Usability Evaluation studies, Rolf Molich and colleagues asked independent professional teams to evaluate the same interface and compared what they reported. The consistent finding is that teams find very different sets of problems, so any one evaluation covers only part of the picture.

**Specifics.** In CUE-4, 17 teams evaluated the Hotel Penn website, a hotel reservation site with a Flash-based reservation system by iHotelier; nine teams ran usability tests and eight did expert reviews. Together they reported 340 different usability issues. Overlap between teams was low: most issues were reported by a single team only, though the precise share for CUE-4 was not confirmed this session. Earlier studies in the series showed the same pattern: CUE-2 (Hotmail, nine teams) reported about 300 distinct problems, a large majority of them found by one team only (not re-confirmed). Teams differed in the number of issues reported, in the quality of their recommendations, in how they dealt with false alarms and in how well they wrote up findings. Both usability testing and expert review produced useful findings; neither dominated. The lessons the authors drew include: use several evaluators or teams, combine methods, write clear and prioritized reports with evidence, report positive findings too and be careful with claims that are matters of opinion.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** CUE is highly cited and has shaped the view that evaluator effect is large. The teams chose their own tasks and methods, so some difference is task coverage, not unreliability. The finding that few problems are common to all teams does not mean evaluations are useless; it means a single evaluation is incomplete. The result challenges optimistic reading of the five-user and heuristic evaluation studies.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Combine expert review with user tests; each finds things the other misses. (2) Use at least two independent reviewers for important pages. (3) Report each finding with evidence, severity and a proposed fix. (4) Do not treat one audit as complete; schedule repeated looks. (5) Record which tasks each review covered. (6) Ask evaluators to note what works so good patterns are kept.

## ISO9241-11

**Core claims.** ISO 9241-11 defines usability as the extent to which a system, product or service can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use. The definition makes usability relative to who, what for and where, not an inherent property of the product.

**Specifics.** Effectiveness is the accuracy and completeness with which users achieve goals. Efficiency is the resources spent (time, effort, cost) in relation to the results. Satisfaction is the extent to which the user's physical, cognitive and emotional responses that result from use meet their needs and expectations. Context of use means the users, goals, tasks, resources and environment (technical, physical, social, cultural). The 2018 edition, a revision of the 1998 first edition, extends the scope from products to systems and services, introduces "outcomes of use" and links usability to user experience, accessibility and avoiding harm. Measures must be chosen per context, so usability requirements start with identifying users, goals and context. The standard sits in the ISO 9241 family; its sibling ISO 9241-110 (2006, revised 2020) lists interaction principles, in the 2006 edition: suitability for the task, self-descriptiveness, controllability, conformity with user expectations, error tolerance, suitability for individualization and suitability for learning. The 2020 edition renames and reorders them (the author recalls learnability, use error robustness and user engagement among the new names, not confirmed). The standard text is not freely available and this entry rests on secondary descriptions.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** A definitional standard, not an empirical finding. Its three components are used as the basis for measures in many studies; some researchers argue satisfaction is narrower than user experience, and that the definition under-emphasizes learnability, which Nielsen treats as a separate attribute.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Write down the specified users: complete beginners at a PC with the sim running. (2) Context: split attention between app and sim, possibly a second monitor or a tablet, often in the evening. (3) Define effectiveness as reaching the lesson goal, efficiency as time and hints used, satisfaction as SUS or a short rating. (4) Use the 9241-110 principles as a review frame. (5) Give each page a one-line statement of user, goal and context. (6) Re-evaluate when context changes, such as a new device.

## Tognazzini2014

**Core claims.** Bruce "Tog" Tognazzini (Apple's early human interface group, later Sun and Nielsen Norman Group) published a widely read list of "First Principles of Interaction Design" that gathers practical rules from decades of interface work. The list is meant as a check for designers, not a theory.

**Specifics.** The revised list (published on his AskTog site, expanded about 2014; an earlier version appeared in his 1992 book Tog on Interface) names around 19 principles. Those the author recalls include: anticipation (bring the information and tools the user needs to them); autonomy (give users a feeling of control, keep them informed); color blindness (never use color as the only cue); consistency; defaults (make them predictable and let users revert); efficiency of the user (design for the user's time and not the machine's); explorable interfaces (let users go back, make it safe to explore, avoid dead ends); Fitts's law (target size and distance); human-interface objects; latency reduction; learnability; metaphors, use with care; protect users' work; readability; track state; and visible navigation. He is known for the argument that "the user's time is more valuable than the computer's", and for Apple-era studies of the mouse and menu bar that he reported with timing claims; a claim from that era (that users think keyboard is faster while the mouse is faster) is widely repeated but not confirmed here. The exact count and wording of the principles were not re-confirmed this session.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** An expert checklist without experiments. It overlaps heavily with Nielsen's heuristics and Apple's guidelines and is more concrete in places (defaults, latency, protect users' work). Some entries repeat Fitts's law and other findings covered in lane 09. Treat the list as a prompt, not proof.

**Use for LTF.** (1) "Protect users' work" and "track state" mean autosave and resume. (2) "Explorable interfaces" means every lesson step can be undone and no dead ends. (3) "Latency reduction" supports quick loads and instant feedback in fly mode. (4) "Color blindness" requires redundant coding of status. (5) "Defaults" suit settings such as theme and units. (6) "Visible navigation" means the learner always sees where they are in a course.

## Smith1986

**Core claims.** Sidney Smith and Jane Mosier compiled a comprehensive collection of user interface design guidelines for the US Air Force Electronic Systems Division, written for designers of interactive software for military command and control. It is the most extensive early guideline compendium and the source of much later material (including Shneiderman's and Nielsen's lists).

**Specifics.** The report, Guidelines for Designing User Interface Software (MITRE Corporation report ESD-TR-86-278, 1986, the 1984 first version having been issued as an earlier report), contains about 944 guidelines (the commonly quoted count; not re-confirmed this session) arranged in six sections: data entry, data display, sequence control, user guidance, data transmission and data protection. Each guideline has an exception list, comments, an example and references, with cross references. Examples of recurring principles are consistency of data display, minimal user memory load, logical grouping and ordering of data, feedback on every user action, flexible sequence control, informative error messages and protection of data against accidental loss. The authors drew on the human factors literature of the time and on engineering judgment, and the document is organized for look-up, not for reading start to finish. The text was widely reproduced and is available from US defense technical information archives.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** The guidelines are very numerous, sometimes contradictory and not all supported by experiments, and they were written for character-based terminals before the graphical interface. A known problem with large guideline sets is that designers cannot apply them all, which motivated short heuristic lists. The document remains a good source of rationale for basics such as grouping, consistency and error handling.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Consult it for fundamentals (data entry, data display) when designing forms and instrument readouts. (2) Its "user guidance" section informs hints, prompts and error messages. (3) Use the structure (each guideline with a rationale and an exception) as a model for LTF's own design rules. (4) The "data protection" section supports autosave and safe reset. (5) Do not adopt rules wholesale: check each against the learner context. (6) Cite the older source behind a short rule when a rule is challenged.

## Apple1987

**Core claims.** Apple Computer's Human Interface Guidelines: The Apple Desktop Interface (Addison-Wesley, 1987) set out the principles of the Macintosh interface for developers, to make applications behave alike. It was one of the first mass-circulated corporate interface style guides and argues that consistency across applications lets users transfer learning.

**Specifics.** The book lists design principles that the author recalls as: metaphors from the real world; direct manipulation (acting on visible objects); see-and-point (select from what is visible instead of typing commands); consistency; WYSIWYG (what you see is what you get); user control (the user, not the computer, initiates and controls actions); feedback and dialog; forgiveness (actions are reversible and users are warned of destructive actions); perceived stability (a finite set of objects and environments that stay put); and aesthetic integrity (information organized and displayed consistently with visual design principles). Apple stated that these were based on how people work and on user testing at Apple. The book gives detailed standard behavior for menus, windows, dialog boxes, the mouse and keyboard, with standard menu items and shortcut keys, so that every application had the same Edit and File menus. The exact wording of the principle list is from memory and the publication details (Addison-Wesley, 1987) were not re-confirmed this session.

**Quotations.** None used.

**Criticism and status.** A corporate guide written for a particular platform, with testing claims not published as research. The strict consistency approach was later criticized for limiting innovation, and the principles have been revised many times in later editions. Evidence that consistency aids transfer comes from research (Polson, Kieras and others), not from the guide itself.

**Use for LTF.** (1) "Forgiveness" and "user control" support safe exploration in lessons. (2) "Perceived stability" suits fly mode: the layout should not shift between phases. (3) "Direct manipulation" supports interactive instrument widgets that learners drag and tune. (4) A platform-style guide for LTF (the design system) should do for the team what this book did for developers. (5) "Aesthetic integrity" supports having an identity, not only consistency. (6) Prefer see-and-point over typed input for beginners.

## Lane synthesis

Agreement. The sources agree that usability is relative to users, goals and context (ISO 9241-11, Gould and Lewis, Nielsen), and that it is reached by iteration: study users, measure, fix, repeat. Every heuristic or guideline set, whether Smith and Mosier, Apple, Nielsen and Molich, Bastien and Scapin, Gerhardt-Powals or Tognazzini, returns to the same themes: visible system state and feedback, consistency, user control and reversibility, error prevention and recovery, recognition over recall, minimalism and a match with the user's world. Norman and Lewis give the reasoning for design for error, and Ceaparu shows that unclear error messages, lost work and hidden features are what users experience as frustration. Krug supplies the behavioral basis: people scan, satisfice and muddle through.

Conflict. Cheap methods versus reliability: Nielsen argues for few evaluators and about five users per round, while the CUE studies and critics (Spool and Schroeder, Faulkner) show that single evaluations are incomplete and the five-user figure is not a coverage guarantee. Short lists (ten heuristics) versus long compendia (Smith and Mosier, Bastien and Scapin) trade memorability against precision. Self-report scores (SUS) can flatter. "Don't make me think" fits interface effort but not learning content, where thinking is the point.

Ten takeaways for LTF.
1. Define users, goals and context first: a beginner at a PC with the sim running.
2. Test with real beginners in small repeated rounds of three to five and fix between rounds.
3. Never claim coverage from a participant count; choose tasks to cover main journeys.
4. Combine expert review and user testing; use two independent reviewers.
5. Rewrite Nielsen's ten heuristics as LTF questions and rate findings from 0 to 4.
6. Show system state and the result of every action; mark modes clearly.
7. Prefer undo, retry and autosave to confirmation dialogs; never blame the learner.
8. Remove interface effort so the learner's effort goes to flying; design pages for scanning.
9. Track SUS (floor 70, goal 80), success, hints and first versus later session performance.
10. Treat questions asked aloud and frustration sources (messages, lost progress, hidden features, slow loads) as defects.
