## Ryan2000

**Core claims.** Self-determination theory (SDT) holds that people have three basic psychological needs: autonomy (acting from one's own volition), competence (effectively dealing with the environment) and relatedness (connection with others). Social contexts that support these needs foster intrinsic motivation and healthy internalization of values and regulations. Contexts that thwart them produce amotivation, controlled motivation and poorer well-being. Intrinsic motivation is doing an activity for its inherent interest and satisfaction. Extrinsic motivation is not one thing: the paper describes a continuum of regulation styles from external regulation (acting for rewards or to avoid punishment), through introjection (acting to avoid guilt or protect ego), identification (valuing the goal personally), to integration (the value is assimilated into the self). The more internalized the regulation, the better the persistence, learning quality and well-being.

**Specifics.** The article is a theory overview, not a single experiment. It presents several mini-theories. Cognitive evaluation theory predicts how events affect intrinsic motivation through two routes: perceived locus of causality (did I choose this?) and perceived competence (am I effective?). Controlling events such as deadlines, surveillance, evaluation pressure, threats and contingent tangible rewards move the perceived locus outward and lower intrinsic motivation. Informational events, such as positive competence feedback, choice and acknowledgment of the person's perspective, raise it, but only if the person also feels autonomous, so praise delivered in a controlling tone can backfire. Organismic integration theory describes how a non-interesting activity becomes self-endorsed: the person needs a meaningful rationale, acknowledgment of the feelings about the task, and a sense of choice rather than pressure. Autonomy support therefore means offering choice, giving a rationale, using non-controlling language (avoiding "should", "must", "have to"), and acknowledging negative affect. Competence support means optimal challenge, clear structure and expectations, and informational feedback. Relatedness support means warmth and genuine interest from the teacher or community. The authors also cite cross-cultural work showing that need satisfaction predicts well-being in both individualist and collectivist samples, which is why they call the needs universal.

**Quotation.** None, to avoid misquoting.

**Criticism and status.** SDT is among the most heavily researched motivation theories, with studies in education, work, sport, health and games. Critics point out that the list of three needs is somewhat arbitrary (why not meaning or achievement?), that the regulation "continuum" is better treated as distinct types than a strict ordering, that many findings rely on self-report and correlational designs, and that the theory is so broad it risks being unfalsifiable. Replication of the need-satisfaction to well-being link is generally good; the size of the reward-undermining effect is contested (see Deci1999). Whether needs are strictly "basic" and universal is debated in cross-cultural work.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Test every feature against the three needs. Autonomy: can the learner pick the next lesson, skip ahead, or set the pace? Competence: is there an achievable challenge and honest feedback? Relatedness: is there an instructor voice or optional community?
2. Write copy as information rather than control. "You held the glide within 5 knots" is informational; "You must hold speed" is controlling. Avoid "should", "must" and "don't forget" in learner-facing text.
3. Give a rationale for necessary but dull content such as checklists and memory items: tie it to what a real pilot faces, which is how internalization happens.
4. Treat streak pressure, countdowns and nagging notifications as controlling events that risk eroding the interest the app depends on.
5. Remember that a learner who wants to fly is already intrinsically driven; the design job is to protect that motivation, not to replace it with extrinsic hooks.
6. Offer choices that matter (airport, weather, which lesson next) rather than cosmetic choices, since only meaningful choice supports autonomy.

**Worked example for LTF.** A learner stuck on the radio-call lesson can be offered a choice between reading the phraseology first or listening to a real exchange first (autonomy), told why the calls are standardized (rationale), and shown which phrases they already got right (competence). Each of these is an informational, need-supporting move; none requires a reward.

## Deci1999

**Core claims.** A meta-analytic review of 128 experiments asks whether extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation. The conclusion, confirmed in the search record of the abstract, is that tangible rewards overall significantly undermine intrinsic motivation, and that the effect depends on the type of reward and how it is made contingent. Engagement-contingent, completion-contingent and performance-contingent rewards all reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation. Verbal rewards (positive feedback) tended to enhance intrinsic motivation, especially for college students, and this is the informational route described in SDT.

**Specifics.** The authors coded 128 studies and used two outcome measures of intrinsic motivation: free-choice behavior (time spent on the target activity during a period after the reward is gone, when the participant can do something else) and self-reported interest or enjoyment. Effect sizes reported for tangible rewards on free-choice behavior, as given in the abstract: engagement-contingent d of about -0.40, completion-contingent about -0.36, and performance-contingent about -0.28. These three categories are defined by what a person must do to earn the reward: just work on the task, finish the task, or reach a stated standard. Unexpected tangible rewards and task-noncontingent rewards (paid regardless of what you do) did not produce a significant undermining effect, which matches Lepper1973. The undermining effect was larger for children than for college students. Interest in the task mattered: for uninteresting tasks there is little intrinsic motivation to undermine, and rewards did not hurt. The authors argue the mechanism is that contingent rewards are experienced as controlling, shifting perceived causality outward, so the same dollars delivered as informational acknowledgment of competence do less harm.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** Cameron and Pierce (1994) published a competing meta-analysis concluding that the undermining effect was limited to narrow conditions (expected, tangible, engagement- or completion-contingent rewards given for interesting tasks) and that rewards often have no negative effect or are positive for performance. Deci, Koestner and Ryan responded that the narrow conditions are in fact the common ones in real life and that the Cameron analysis pooled categories in a way that diluted the effect. The shared ground between the camps is that expected, tangible, engagement-contingent rewards on interesting tasks can reduce later free-choice interest, and that verbal, informational feedback is safe or helpful. Most studies are brief lab tasks with children or undergraduates. How well the result carries to long-running consumer apps is not established by this paper.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Do not pay points, coins or loot per lesson opened or per minute of use: engagement-contingent rewards are the worst case in this evidence.
2. Competence-affirming feedback ("landed within 200 feet of the aim point") is informational and safe. Keep feedback concrete and about the learner's performance.
3. If badges exist, tie them to demonstrated skill (performance-contingent against a meaningful standard) and consider unannounced recognition rather than advertised targets.
4. Never let the reward become more prominent than the flying task; the interesting activity should stay the focus of the screen.
5. Run a removal test in review: if points and badges vanished, would the learner still come back? That is the intrinsic-motivation check.
6. Learners new to a dull topic (regulations, weather codes) can be supported with rewards without the same undermining risk, but still prefer rationale over prizes.

## Lepper1973

**Core claims.** The overjustification hypothesis: when a person is given a salient extrinsic reward for an activity they already enjoy, they may infer that they are doing it for the reward, and their later interest in the activity drops once the reward is removed. The same reward delivered without being expected does not produce the effect, which shows that the harm comes from the expectation that the activity is a means to an end.

**Specifics.** The participants were preschool children (the paper's sample, to my recollection, was in the order of 50 children; the exact count is not confirmed here) who showed high baseline interest in drawing with felt-tip markers during free play. Children were assigned to one of three conditions. Expected award: the child was shown a "Good Player" certificate with a ribbon and told they could get it for drawing. Unexpected award: the child drew without any mention of a prize and was then given the same certificate. No award: the child drew and received nothing. One to two weeks later, observers watched through a one-way mirror to see how much time the children spent drawing during free play. The expected-award children spent clearly less time with the markers than the other two groups, which did not differ from each other. The paper also had judges rate the quality of drawings made in the experimental session, and the expected-award drawings were rated lower. The precise percentages of free-play time that I recall (roughly 9, 17 and 18 percent) are not confirmed in this session and should be checked against the paper before quoting.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The effect has been replicated many times and is the basis for the reward literature summarized in Deci1999. Boundary conditions were found later: no undermining for unexpected rewards, for rewards perceived as recognition of competence, for uninteresting tasks, or when the reward is conveyed as informational feedback. The original sample is small, the task a single drawing activity, and the follow-up one-time, so the paper alone cannot tell us about long-term apps. Some classroom field studies found small or no undermining when rewards were tied to quality of work, and critics argue the effect size depends strongly on how the reward is framed.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Treat promised rewards as risky; recognition delivered after the fact carries less overjustification risk than a pre-announced prize.
2. Do not open a lesson with "Complete this to earn a badge". Let the task stand on its own interest.
3. If a milestone must be announced in advance, present it as a mark of competence (a standard to reach), not as an inducement.
4. Unexpected-and-informative recognition (a quiet "That was your smoothest approach yet") fits the evidence better than a visible checklist of prizes.
5. Pay particular attention to learners who already love flight simulation: they have the most intrinsic interest to lose.
6. When writing research plans for LTF, include a delayed free-choice measure (do learners return unprompted a week later?) as a test of any reward feature.

## Csikszentmihalyi1990

**Core claims.** Flow is a state of optimal experience in which a person is fully absorbed in an activity whose challenge matches their skill. It arises when both challenge and skill are high and in balance. If challenge greatly exceeds skill, the person feels anxiety; if skill greatly exceeds challenge, boredom. The first full model appeared in Beyond Boredom and Anxiety (1975); the 1990 book Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience gave the popular account, and later versions of the diagram split the space into more regions (for example apathy, worry, arousal, control, relaxation) rather than the original three channels.

**Specifics.** The book draws on interviews with rock climbers, chess players, surgeons, dancers, factory workers and others, plus the Experience Sampling Method (ESM). In ESM, participants carry a pager and, when signaled at random times during the day, report what they are doing, where, with whom and how they feel. The conditions for flow listed in the book are clear goals, immediate feedback on how well one is doing, and a balance between perceived challenges and perceived skills. The experiential features are: a merging of action and awareness, deep concentration on the task, a sense of control, loss of self-consciousness, a distorted sense of time, and an autotelic quality, meaning the activity is rewarding in itself. A finding he stresses is the "paradox of work": people reported more flow, and better mood and motivation, in challenging structured work than in passive leisure, yet said they wished to be at leisure. The balance is about perceived challenge and perceived skill, not objective measures, and it is dynamic: as skill grows, the same task becomes boring, so flow requires ever-higher challenge. The book also argues for goals one can pursue, rules that structure the activity, and feedback that arrives inside the action rather than after it.

**Quotation.** Not quoted; I am not certain of exact wording and page.

**Criticism and status.** The qualitative richness is widely admired, but the challenge-skill balance prediction has only mixed empirical support: in ESM data, high challenge with high skill predicts the best experience, though some studies find that challenge alone, or skill alone, explains much of the effect. Measurement is a recurring difficulty, because asking a person about flow interrupts it, and self-report scales vary (the flow state scale, ESM items). Flow research is largely correlational. In game research, flow is often treated as one component of engagement, and claims that any matched-difficulty design guarantees flow are over-reached. Dynamic difficulty adjustment studies show mixed benefits, and some players dislike feeling manipulated.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Offer difficulty tiers (for example the same approach with calm wind, then crosswind) and suggest the next tier after steady success, while letting the learner override the suggestion.
2. Give immediate feedback inside the practice, for instance a live readout in fly mode, because immediacy is a named condition of flow.
3. State one clear goal for every challenge before it starts, so the learner always knows what counts as success.
4. Protect concentration in fly mode: no pop-ups, no animated badges, no notification dots while the learner is flying.
5. Avoid layering evaluative timers on every task, since self-consciousness is the opposite of flow.
6. Do not claim that the app produces flow; claim only that it removes known obstacles to it (unclear goals, delayed feedback, mismatched difficulty).

**Worked example for LTF.** A pattern-practice challenge with a stated goal, live altitude and speed readouts and a one-tap retry keeps goal clarity and feedback immediacy high. If the learner fails the same approach three times, the app can offer an easier variant with lighter wind instead of repeating the same demand, reducing anxiety without removing the challenge.

## Bandura1977

**Core claims.** Self-efficacy is a person's belief in their ability to execute the behavior required to produce a given outcome. It differs from an outcome expectancy (a belief that the behavior will lead to a result): a person may think a landing will be rewarded but doubt they can fly it. Bandura argues that efficacy expectations determine whether a person will attempt a behavior, how much effort they will spend, and how long they will persist against obstacles and aversive experiences.

**Specifics.** The paper proposes four sources of efficacy information. Performance accomplishments (mastery experiences) are the strongest because they are based on personal evidence of success, and repeated success raises efficacy while early failures lower it, particularly before efficacy is firmly established. Vicarious experience means seeing similar others succeed, which is weaker than one's own performance but useful when the observer has little experience. Verbal persuasion, such as encouragement from a credible source, is easy to apply but gives weaker, more fragile effects. Emotional and physiological arousal: people read their own tension, sweating and fear as signs of vulnerability, so reducing arousal or reinterpreting it raises efficacy. Efficacy beliefs vary on three dimensions: magnitude (how difficult a task one feels able to do), generality (how far the belief transfers) and strength (how firmly held). The evidence came from clinical research on phobia, where guided participant modeling, in which a therapist demonstrates and then helps the client perform the feared behavior step by step, produced the largest gains in both efficacy and approach behavior in snake-phobic adults compared to modeling alone or desensitization. Within this treatment, efficacy ratings predicted which tasks the person would approach. Bandura's 1997 book Self-Efficacy: The Exercise of Control extended the theory to education, work and health.

**Criticism and status.** Meta-analyses find moderate positive relationships between efficacy and performance (I recall Multon, Brown and Lent 1991 on academic outcomes and Stajkovic and Luthans 1998 in work settings, each in the region of r = .3 to .4, though those figures are not confirmed here). The relationship is reciprocal: success raises efficacy and efficacy raises effort. Critics argue that efficacy measured right after a task partly just restates performance, that overconfidence can hurt calibration (a pilot who overrates their skill is dangerous), and that the four-sources model underplays other influences. The core idea is widely accepted and used in education and health behavior change.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Engineer an early real success: the first lesson should end with the learner doing something they can see they did (holding a heading, a smooth climb) rather than just reading.
2. Provide vicarious examples of similar beginners attempting and improving, such as annotated first-landing replays, to supply modeling.
3. Normalize arousal: "Most students find the flare tense at first" changes how the body signals are read.
4. Keep encouragement credible and specific; generic "Great job!" carries little persuasive weight and may be discounted.
5. Avoid punishing failure screens in the first lessons, because early failure lowers efficacy most.
6. Show history ("your last five approaches got steadier") as mastery evidence, and calibrate it so learners do not become overconfident about unsupervised real flight.

**Worked example for LTF.** The first lesson might end with a straight and level exercise that the learner can actually complete, followed by a replay overlay showing their steady heading line. That is a mastery experience with visible evidence, and it is worth more than any amount of encouragement text placed around it.

## Locke2002

**Core claims.** Goal-setting theory states that specific, difficult goals lead to higher performance than vague goals ("do your best") or easy goals, provided the person is committed and has the ability. Goals work by directing attention to relevant activities, energizing effort, increasing persistence and encouraging the discovery of task strategies. The paper summarizes about 35 years of research by the authors and others and tries to build a practically useful theory.

**Specifics.** The key moderators are goal commitment (higher when the goal is important, when the person has self-efficacy, and when the goal is public or participatively set), feedback (goals work best when people can see their progress against the goal), task complexity (the effect is stronger on simple than on complex tasks), and ability. The authors discuss mediators: choice of direction, effort, persistence and strategy. They note that on complex tasks where people lack strategies, a performance goal can produce hurried or poor strategy, so a learning goal (for example, discover how to do the task, or master three strategies) works better than a high outcome goal. Goals also mediate the effect of incentives: money improves performance mainly by changing the goals people set. Assigned and self-set goals produce similar results when a rationale is given and the assigning person is trusted. Satisfaction is tied to goal attainment, which creates a high-performance cycle: demanding goals, performance, rewards, satisfaction, and commitment to new goals. The paper lists conditions under which goals can fail, including conflicting goals and too little feedback. The count of studies behind the claims is large (I recall more than a thousand, not confirmed here).

**Criticism and status.** Goal-setting is one of the most replicated results in organizational psychology. Critics include Ordonez, Schweitzer, Galinsky and Bazerman (2009, "Goals Gone Wild"), who catalogued harms from narrow goals: neglect of unmeasured aspects, risk-taking, unethical behavior and loss of intrinsic motivation. The research base is largely workplace and lab tasks of short duration. In learning, performance-goal orientation can reduce willingness to take on hard tasks, which is the contrast with mastery goals in achievement goal theory.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Give each challenge one specific, measurable goal (hold 3,000 feet MSL within 100 feet; touch down in the first third of the runway).
2. In the first lessons prefer learning goals ("find out what the nose does when you add power") to score goals.
3. Show feedback relative to the goal at the end of each attempt, so the goal is meaningful.
4. Let learners adjust a goal, which preserves autonomy and still gives specificity.
5. Do not stack many simultaneous numerical targets; the research supports a focused goal.
6. Avoid goals that reward only the measured aspect (a smooth landing score that ignores the checklist) so the app does not teach learners to game the metric.

**Worked example for LTF.** A challenge titled "Steady climb" could state: hold 80 KIAS and climb from 2,000 to 3,000 feet MSL, speed within 5 knots. At the end, show the achieved speed range against the stated one. Commitment is increased if the learner chose the challenge, and feedback closes the loop. Resist adding five more numeric targets to the same attempt, which would dilute focus.

## Amabile2011

**Core claims.** Of all the events that influence people's inner work life (their emotions, motivations and perceptions), the most important is making progress in meaningful work. The authors call this the progress principle: on days when people perceive progress, even small wins, they feel better and are more motivated, and on days with setbacks they feel worse, with setbacks hitting harder than wins help.

**Specifics.** The book is based on a multi-year diary study, with about 12,000 daily diary entries from about 238 employees across 26 project teams in 7 companies, whose work called for creativity (the figures are as I recall them and I could not confirm them in this session). Participants completed a short daily web questionnaire on mood, motivation and perceptions of work, plus an open field asking them to describe one event that stood out that day. Researchers coded these events and found that progress, in the form of small steps toward a meaningful goal, was the most common event on days of best mood and motivation, and setbacks were common on worst days. The authors identify two kinds of support: catalysts (clear goals, autonomy, adequate resources and time, help with the work, learning from problems and successes) and nourishers (respect, encouragement, emotional support, a sense of affiliation). The book also reports a survey of managers who ranked progress low among motivators compared with recognition and incentives, which the authors present as a blind spot. They recommend regular visible progress checks and a protected focus on meaningful goals.

**Criticism and status.** The core results appeared in peer-reviewed papers (for example Amabile and colleagues on affect and creativity, 2005), while the book and the 2011 Harvard Business Review article are popularizations. The data are self-reported diaries from knowledge workers, correlational, so causation is inferred from timing. Extending the principle to voluntary learners is plausible, as the same need for competence is invoked in SDT, but it is not directly tested. Some of the cited percentages are not confirmed here.

**Use for LTF.**
1. The dashboard should answer "what moved forward since last time" first, not total points or minutes.
2. Break big goals (a first solo-quality traffic pattern) into visible smaller wins, and name them in plain language.
3. After a setback such as a poor landing, pair the feedback with one concrete next step; setbacks weigh more than wins.
4. Prefer progress with meaningful content ("you now hold altitude within 50 feet") to anonymous counters.
5. End each session with one clearly stated step forward, even on a bad day.
6. Do not manufacture trivial wins (a badge for opening the app); the principle depends on progress being meaningful.

## Kivetz2006

**Core claims.** Clark Hull's goal-gradient hypothesis (1932), originally shown in rats that ran faster as they neared food, also holds for humans: the nearer a person is to a reward, the more effort they invest. The paper adds that the gradient responds to perceived progress, so even illusory advancement speeds people up.

**Specifics.** The authors combine a field study and experiments. Field study 1 used a coffee shop loyalty card (buy a set number of coffees, get one free): customers shortened the time between purchases as they moved closer to the free coffee. Field study 2 used an online music-rating reward program in which members rated songs to earn gift certificates: rating frequency rose as members approached the reward threshold. Another study tested illusionary progress by giving some customers a card with extra stamps already applied but also more required, so that actual remaining purchases were identical to a plain card while perceived progress was greater, and these customers purchased faster. They also found a post-reward reset: after earning the reward, people slowed down, buying less often until they began approaching the next one, which is the mirror image of the gradient. The authors also relate the gradient to retention: customers further along in a program were more likely to remain. Perceived distance (for example "3 of 10 stamps" versus "7 to go") rather than absolute work is what matters. The exact sample sizes and speed-up magnitudes are not confirmed here.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The evidence is from consumer loyalty contexts with discretionary purchases, not learning. The goal-gradient pattern is consistent with a broader literature on goal proximity, but boundary conditions exist: when a goal looks unreachable, proximity can reduce effort, and some learners disengage after a near miss. Effort can fall after reward receipt, which matters for course designers because a completed module is a natural place to quit. Illusory progress that is revealed as inflated can feel manipulative and may damage trust (see ethics concerns in the dark patterns literature, lane 20).

**Use for LTF.**
1. Show a visible fraction for multi-step modules ("3 of 5 steps"); effort tends to rise as the end approaches.
2. Cut long courses into short chapters so learners often feel close to some finish line.
3. Expect a dip right after completion and use a pull toward what comes next (a preview of the next lesson, a prompt to try the maneuver in the sim today).
4. Count only genuine learning milestones towards progress; padding the bar with trivial steps defeats the purpose and erodes trust.
5. Make "distance remaining" honest and estimated in minutes where possible, so the perception matches reality.
6. Track whether learners stall right at completion points; that is where the post-reward slump will appear in your data.

## Nunes2006

**Core claims.** Giving people artificial advancement toward a goal, framed as already earned, increases their persistence and the likelihood that they finish. The effect, which the authors call endowed progress, occurs because people who see themselves as having started are more motivated than those at zero, even when the remaining work is identical.

**Specifics.** The central field experiment took place at a car wash. Customers were given a loyalty card. In the endowed condition the card needed eight stamps for a free wash but came with two already stamped (so ten spaces, two filled, framed as a bonus). In the control condition the card needed eight stamps with eight empty spaces. Both groups needed to buy eight washes, the same effort. Over the following months the endowed group redeemed cards at a substantially higher rate and took less time between visits. The widely quoted numbers are roughly one third of endowed customers completing versus about a fifth of controls (I recall 34 percent and 19 percent from 300 customers, but these figures are not confirmed in this session, so treat them as approximate). Lab studies in the same paper tested the mechanism: the head start worked when it was presented with a reason (for example, a reward for a prior purchase or a reason for the bonus) and when it was perceived as progress toward the same goal, and it was less effective when it looked like a shorter card without the sense of having begun. The authors link this to the goal-gradient and to the "illusion of progress" described in Kivetz2006.

**Criticism and status.** Later work refined the effect. Some studies find that endowed progress works best when the head start is relevant to the goal and framed as earned, and that it can backfire if people feel the progress is meaningless. Related work argues that the effect partly reflects a commitment or "sunk cost" frame, and Hsee and colleagues (2013) proposed that related reward effects stem from "medium maximization". The original field sample is small and from one business. The paper does not test learning contexts, and replications in education are limited.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Greet new learners with an honestly earned head start: "You connected your simulator and answered four questions, so Module 1 is 20 percent complete."
2. Give a reason for the head start in the copy, since justification is central to the effect.
3. Treat onboarding steps (account, aircraft selection, controls calibration) as the first completed steps of the course.
4. Do not inflate: a progress bar full of trivial clicks is both unhelpful and, if discovered, manipulative.
5. A/B test the framing with small cohorts, since transferability from a car wash to a flight course is unproven.
6. Consider the ethical line described in the dark-patterns literature: head starts are fine when they are true and help learners, not when they only trap them in a funnel.

**Worked example for LTF.** A new learner's course map shows Module 1 with four steps. Two (create profile, connect the simulator) are completed during onboarding, so the map opens at two of four with a note saying why. The remaining work is real, the head start is earned and the reason is given, which meets the conditions that the paper found necessary for the effect.

## Hamari2014

**Core claims.** Hamari, Koivisto and Sarsa reviewed the empirical literature to ask whether gamification works. Their conclusion: gamification generally produces positive effects, but the effects depend on the context in which gamification is implemented and on the users who encounter it.

**Specifics.** The review covered 24 peer-reviewed empirical studies of gamification, found by searching major databases for the term and related variants. They classified studies by context (education and learning was the largest group; others included commerce, intra-organizational systems, health and exercise, and sustainability), by the motivational affordances implemented (points, leaderboards, achievements and badges, levels, stories and themes, clear goals, feedback, rewards, progress, challenge), and by the outcomes measured (behavioral and psychological outcomes). The most common affordances were points, leaderboards and achievements or badges. Most studies reported mostly positive effects, but some reported mixed or null results, and the authors identified methodological problems: many studies were short, so effects might reflect novelty; samples were small; control groups were often missing; and the studies seldom linked their design to theory. They also noted that users differ, so the same affordance can motivate one user group and not another. The overall message was that the field lacked rigor, and they called for more controlled, longer-term, theory-driven studies.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** This is an early review (HICSS 2014), superseded for quantitative claims by meta-analyses such as Sailer2020. Its mostly positive headline has often been cited without the caveat that the underlying studies were weak. It remains the clearest statement of why early positive findings should not be trusted as proof. Related critics include Deterding and colleagues (2011), who define gamification as game design elements in non-game contexts and warn against reducing games to points and badges, and Nicholson (2015) who argues for meaningful rather than reward-based gamification.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Treat points, leaderboards and badges as hypotheses, not best practice.
2. Plan evaluation over weeks and months; novelty can inflate early results.
3. Make elements optional or low in prominence so that different learners can ignore what does not suit them.
4. Prefer elements with a functional role (clear goals, feedback, visible progress) over decoration.
5. Write down the learning or motivation need each element is meant to serve; remove elements that cannot be justified.
6. Collect usage data per element so you can see which are used and which are ignored.

## Sailer2020

**Core claims.** A meta-analysis of experimental and quasi-experimental studies finds that gamification of learning has positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioral learning outcomes. The abstract, confirmed by search, reports that cognitive effects were stable when only high-rigor studies were included, while motivational and behavioral effects were less stable. Inclusion of game fiction and social interaction were significant moderators of behavioral outcomes, and competition combined with collaboration might also help motivational outcomes.

**Specifics.** The meta-analysis, published in Educational Psychology Review volume 32 (2020), pages 77 to 112, synthesized studies comparing gamified learning conditions with non-gamified controls. The authors separated outcomes into cognitive (knowledge, skills), motivational (interest, engagement, attitudes) and behavioral (activity, participation). They coded the game design elements used, the kinds of fiction, and the social structure (cooperation, competition, none), and tested moderators with a theory-driven framing, arguing that different elements work through different psychological needs. The search result also reported an overall figure (an r of about .31 with a wide interval) that I cannot attribute to a particular outcome, so I do not give per-outcome effect sizes here; the g values I had recalled earlier are not confirmed and have been removed. Heterogeneity was high and the number of rigorous studies was limited. The authors state that gamification is a bundle of elements, so the findings cannot be assigned to single features.

**Criticism and status.** Later meta-analyses (for example by Bai, Hew and Huang, 2020, and Zainuddin and colleagues, 2020) report small to moderate positive effects and also high heterogeneity, so the broad direction is replicated, although I did not verify those papers' figures here. Publication bias, short interventions, and the novelty effect remain concerns. The paper cannot say whether a given element such as a badge helps, only that categories of design, such as fiction or social interaction, moderate outcomes.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Narrative framing (a flight school story, a recurring instructor character) has support as a moderator of behavior, so consider story before points.
2. Prefer cooperative or team-versus-team structures to pure individual competition.
3. Expect clearer benefits for knowledge outcomes (recall, quizzes) than for sustained motivation.
4. Do not rely on gamification alone to hold motivation; combine with competence and autonomy supports.
5. Evaluate one element at a time and keep an event log.
6. For each element, record the psychological need it serves, in line with the authors' call for theory-based design.

## Hanus2015

**Core claims.** In a real course over a whole semester, a gamified design (leaderboard, badges, point system) did not raise motivation or performance. Compared with a non-gamified version of the same course, students in the gamified version showed lower intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time and did worse on the final examination. The authors read the result through self-determination theory: external feedback structures can be experienced as controlling and can trigger unfavorable social comparison.

**Specifics.** As I recall, the study followed roughly 80 undergraduates in a communication course across a term of about 16 weeks, comparing a gamified section with a non-gamified section in which the content was the same. The gamified condition awarded points for assignments, and showed leaderboards and badges. Measures taken at several time points were intrinsic motivation, social comparison, satisfaction, effort and academic performance, and an analysis traced how gamification influenced exam scores through motivation, satisfaction and perceived autonomy. The direction of results: gamification lowered intrinsic motivation, satisfaction and empowerment, and the lower final exam scores and reduced effort were linked with those decreases. The sample size, course length, and the exact effect sizes are not confirmed in this session and should be checked before quoting. The explanation offered is that leaderboards and badges can make rewards salient and contingent on performance, which reduces autonomy, and that learners who see themselves near the bottom lose a sense of competence.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** One course, one implementation, modest sample, and a single university context; the finding should not be generalized into "gamification fails". It is a counterexample to naive positivity and it is consistent with Deci1999, though later studies that used non-competitive, optional or mastery-oriented badges report neutral or positive effects. Sailer2020 suggests that competition paired with collaboration may fare better than pure competition. The study is useful mainly as a warning that competitive public rankings can backfire for those who do not rank highly.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Avoid public leaderboards, especially for beginners who will cluster near the bottom and read the board as a verdict on ability.
2. Make any competitive feature opt-in and off by default.
3. Present badges as feedback on mastery rather than rank among peers.
4. Segment retention data by performance level; harms may concentrate among weaker performers, and averages can hide them.
5. Let learners hide streaks, points and comparisons from their screen.
6. If social features exist, favor cooperative goals (a study group's collective progress) over individual rankings.

**Worked example for LTF.** Consider a first-week learner who completes the climb lesson with a slightly sloppy altitude hold. A leaderboard would show them below dozens of peers, who may have flown for years; the information is useless to them and discouraging. A private skill card showing their own altitude error shrinking across three attempts gives the same performance data in a form that supports competence. The evidence reviewed here suggests that the second design preserves motivation, and it costs nothing extra to build.

## Przybylski2010

**Core claims.** Video games hold players because they satisfy basic psychological needs. The authors propose a motivational model of game engagement in which games provide need satisfaction for competence, autonomy and relatedness, and this, together with presence (immersion) and intuitive controls, predicts enjoyment, the desire to play again and short-term well-being. The paper introduces the Player Experience of Need Satisfaction (PENS) measure.

**Specifics.** Four studies are reported. The first developed and validated PENS with subscales for competence (feeling effective and challenged), autonomy (feeling free to choose and act), relatedness (feeling connected to others in or around the game), presence or immersion (feeling inside the game world) and intuitive controls (controls that are easy to master and become transparent). Later studies applied the model to diverse games and included experimental manipulations in which games or game conditions differing in need support were compared. Findings: need satisfaction predicted enjoyment and intentions to play again, and competence in particular was linked to the structure of challenge and feedback; autonomy was supported by meaningful options and a sense of volition; relatedness by interactions with other players or non-player characters. Intuitive controls were important because they let competence and presence arise without struggle against the interface. The authors discuss how games can supply need satisfaction that people may lack in daily life, but also note that need-thwarting experiences (feeling controlled or incompetent) reduce play motivation. I did not confirm sample sizes here, and the samples were largely young adult gamers.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** The PENS measure is widely used, although its factor structure varies by study, and results are partly correlational and based on self-report. Samples are skewed to young adults familiar with games. Some researchers question whether games are intrinsically motivating mainly through need satisfaction or through other routes such as challenge, narrative and curiosity (compare Malone and Lepper 1987, not included here). Related work by Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski (2006) applied the theory earlier to computer game motivation.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Audit each screen against competence, autonomy and relatedness, the same checklist used for game features.
2. Treat interface friction as a motivation cost: intuitive controls let learners focus on the flying task. Keep fly mode very light.
3. Give meaningful choices (airport, weather, time of day, difficulty) to support autonomy.
4. Make mastery visible with skill indicators tied to actual maneuvers, which supports competence.
5. Support relatedness with a consistent instructor voice or optional community, without forcing social participation.
6. Because LTF is used beside a real simulator, the simulator itself provides presence; the app should not compete for attention at the moment of flight.

**Worked example for LTF.** A fly-mode screen beside the simulator can be audited quickly with the PENS subscales. Does the learner feel effective (a live indicator that confirms the target was met)? Do they feel free (can they skip the checklist step they already know)? Do they feel connected (does the instructor voice acknowledge their last attempt)? Is the interface transparent (large targets, no typing during flight)? Any no is a design defect with a motivational cost, not just a usability issue.

## Lally2010

**Core claims.** Habit formation, defined as increasing automaticity of a behavior through repetition in a consistent context, follows an asymptotic curve. It takes much longer than the popular claim of 21 days, and it varies greatly between people and behaviors.

**Specifics.** Confirmed by search: 96 volunteers chose a daily behavior linked to a cue (such as drinking a glass of water at lunch or doing 50 sit-ups after breakfast) and tried to perform it daily for 84 days, rating how automatic it felt each day using items from the Self-Report Habit Index. The paper modelled automaticity as an asymptotic curve. The median time to reach 95 percent of the individual asymptote was 66 days, with a range from 18 to an extrapolated 254 days. This median applied to a subset (reported as 39) whose data fit the model, and about half of participants had not reached a high enough automaticity score by day 84 to count as a habit. Simple behaviors such as drinking became automatic faster than complex ones such as exercise. Missing a single opportunity did not materially affect the process, while repeated misses slowed it. The biggest automaticity gains came from early repetitions with diminishing returns later. The 21-day myth is commonly traced to a plastic surgeon's observations (Maltz, 1960) rather than research.

**Criticism and status.** The sample was small, self-selected and made up mostly of university-related volunteers, the behaviors were simple health behaviors, and automaticity was self-reported. The median of 66 days applies to a modelled subset and should not be quoted as an average for everyone. Wood and Neal (2007) provide a theoretical account in which habits depend on stable contexts. Whether the same timeline holds for practicing a skill like flying, which is effortful and varied, is untested.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Support a consistent cue: let learners choose a day and time and send reminders at that slot.
2. Make streaks forgiving: one missed day should not wipe out progress, and the evidence supports that a single miss does not undo habit formation.
3. Design for a long arc of weeks to months; do not judge the habit loop on first-week data alone.
4. Keep the recurring task small (a ten minute drill) so repetition is easy and automaticity builds faster.
5. Never write "it takes 21 days to build a habit" in copy; if a number is cited, say the average was about two months and varied widely.
6. Treat habit as a tool for showing up, not a replacement for deliberate practice during the flying itself.

**Worked example for LTF.** A learner who chooses Tuesday and Saturday evenings for a thirty minute practice and receives a reminder at that time is building a cue-behavior link. Counting the streak in weeks rather than days, and forgiving one missed session, matches the finding that a single lapse does not materially change habit formation. A message after a long gap should welcome the learner back and offer a short drill rather than reproach them.

## Sisk2018

**Core claims.** Two meta-analyses test the claims of growth mindset research (Dweck): one on the correlation between mindset and academic achievement, and one on the effect of mindset interventions on achievement. The authors conclude that the overall effects are weak, with stronger effects in specific groups such as students at academic risk and from low socioeconomic backgrounds, and they call for caution about broad claims.

**Specifics.** The first meta-analysis pooled correlations between self-reported mindset and achievement from a large number of studies and found a small positive relationship, on the order of r of about .1 (figure approximate and not confirmed in this session). The second pooled intervention studies and found a very small average effect on achievement, around d of 0.08 (also not confirmed here), with larger effects among groups described as at risk or low in socioeconomic status. The authors discuss bias, noting that studies by authors with a stake in the theory and small studies may show larger effects. The complementary large trial by Yeager and colleagues (2019, Nature), which I recall as a preregistered randomized study of around 12,000 US ninth graders with a short online intervention, found small improvements in grades for lower-achieving students; details are not confirmed here and that paper is not a source in this lane.

**Criticism and status.** Dweck and Yeager argue that Sisk and colleagues combined heterogeneous interventions and treated implementation quality poorly, and that small average effects can be meaningful at scale when targeted to those who benefit. Independent replications of mindset interventions (for example in the UK) found small or null effects. Popular summaries overstate the construct, and "false growth mindset" (praising effort with no strategy) is unhelpful. The honest position is that mindset effects are small, moderated and context dependent.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use mindset-flavored copy sparingly and make it substantive: "Landing is a skill that builds with practice; here is the next drill" instead of slogans.
2. Pair praise for effort with feedback on strategy so that effort is linked to improvement.
3. Do not rely on a mindset message as a retention mechanism.
4. Take the anxiety and failure experiences of the most struggling learners seriously, since the effects appear strongest there.
5. Avoid labeling people ("natural", "gifted") or suggesting that flying ability is fixed.
6. Where claims about mindset appear in LTF copy, stay within what the evidence supports: small average effects, larger for those who struggle.

**Worked example for LTF.** After a hard landing, copy such as "Everyone bounces early on; your next drill is a stable approach speed" gives a strategy and normalizes difficulty. Copy such as "You just need to believe you can improve!" gives neither. The first fits the evidence on useful growth messages (specific, tied to a strategy); the second is the form most criticized as false growth mindset.

## Lane synthesis

The sources converge on a consistent picture. Lasting motivation comes from satisfying competence, autonomy and relatedness (Ryan2000, Przybylski2010), and competence is built through mastery experience, clear goals and informational feedback (Bandura1977, Locke2002, Amabile2011). Rewards are not uniformly harmful, but expected, tangible, engagement-contingent rewards for tasks people already enjoy reduce later interest, while informational recognition of competence is safe or helpful (Lepper1973, Deci1999). Gamification evidence is positive but modest and heterogeneous, stronger for cognitive than for motivational or behavioral outcomes, and public competition can backfire (Hamari2014, Sailer2020, Hanus2015). Progress devices such as the goal gradient and endowed progress raise completion, but the evidence is from commerce and carries an ethical edge (Kivetz2006, Nunes2006). Habits form slowly and variably, and one missed day does not break them (Lally2010). Flow is a useful design target for challenge and feedback, though its measurement is shaky (Csikszentmihalyi1990). Mindset claims should be held loosely (Sisk2018).

Where they conflict: Cameron and Pierce versus Deci on the size of reward effects; whether gamification works at all, which depends on the design and the audience; and whether progress cues are help or manipulation. Several figures I recalled could not be confirmed this session and are flagged in the notes.

Ten actionable takeaways for LTF:
1. Treat the learner's wish to fly as the engine and protect it from controlling design.
2. Engineer a real early success in the first session.
3. Give each challenge one specific goal with feedback relative to it.
4. Make progress visible, honest and made of small named wins; support the step after a setback.
5. Use informational feedback about skill instead of points for activity.
6. No public leaderboards by default; keep social features cooperative and optional.
7. Use badges as acknowledgments of demonstrated skill, never for time spent.
8. Use progress meters and justified head starts, and never fake progress.
9. Offer forgiving streaks and learner-chosen reminders, expecting habits to take months.
10. Keep fly mode free of interruptions and celebratory pop-ups, and evaluate each game element separately over weeks.
