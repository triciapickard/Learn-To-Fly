## Simon1955
**Core claims.** Classical economics modeled the chooser as omniscient: all alternatives known, all consequences computed, a stable utility function maximized. Simon argues that this is not a description of any real organism. Real choosers have limited information about the world and limited computational capacity to process it, so they behave according to simplified models of the situation. The decision problem is not "which alternative is best" but "which alternative is good enough", and the chooser needs a procedure for searching and a stopping rule. The companion paper, Simon 1956 (Psychological Review 63(2), "Rational choice and the structure of the environment"), is where the word satisficing is introduced and where he argues that behavior is shaped jointly by the mind and the structure of the environment. His later image for this is a pair of scissors: you cannot understand how the cutting is done by looking at one blade only.

**Specifics.** The 1955 paper is a formal but mostly verbal model. It replaces a full ordering of outcomes with a simple payoff scheme (in the simplest case, outcomes are satisfactory or not, or are placed in a small number of ordered classes). Alternatives are not given all at once; they are discovered sequentially, so the order in which options arrive matters. The chooser holds an aspiration level. Search continues until an alternative meets it. Aspirations are not fixed: they rise when satisfactory options are found easily and fall when search is long and fruitless, so the standard is adapted to the environment. Simon also argues that the chooser evaluates only a subset of the consequences of each action, and that attention, not information, constrains the process. The consequence for prediction is that you cannot derive behavior from an objective payoff table; you need to know what the person attends to, what they search, in what order, and when they stop. Simon extends the point to organizations, where routines and divided attention stand in for global optimization. The paper was written when economics was formalizing rational choice, and it is the origin of the field now called bounded rationality, for which Simon received the 1978 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics.

**Criticism and status.** This is a theoretical model, not an experiment with an effect size, so there is nothing to replicate in the usual sense. Its descriptive claim (people stop searching at good enough) has strong support across consumer and job search studies, and it underlies the heuristics programs of Gigerenzer (see Gigerenzer2011) and of Payne, Bettman and Johnson's adaptive decision maker. Critics from within economics argued that satisficing can be rewritten as optimization with search costs; Simon's reply was that this requires the chooser to compute the optimal stopping point, which presupposes the very omniscience at issue. Later individual-difference work (the maximizer versus satisficer scale of Schwartz and colleagues, 2002) is not part of this paper and has mixed validity, so it should not be presented as established.

**Use for LTF.**
- Treat learners as satisficers: they will take the first reasonable next step they see. Make the first visible option on the lesson page the one you want them to take.
- Show one recommended next lesson, with alternatives secondary, so that the stopping rule "good enough" is met by the right choice.
- Because aspiration levels adjust, calibrate early milestones so a beginner's bar for "good" tracks real progress (for example, "first stable climb" before "perfect landing").
- Order matters in sequential search: list lessons in teaching order, not alphabetically or by popularity.
- Design for the whole situation (a learner with a joystick in one hand, the sim on another screen), not only for the mind in isolation.

## Simon1971
**Core claims.** In an information-rich world, the scarce resource is not information but the attention of its recipients. Simon writes that information consumes attention, so a surplus of information produces a shortage of attention, and the design problem becomes allocating attention efficiently among the many sources that might consume it. He argues that an information-processing system should be judged by whether it reduces what reaches the recipient to what the recipient can use, that is, by its filtering, not by how much it supplies.

**Quotation.** "A wealth of information creates a poverty of attention" (p. 40 in the chapter; page not confirmed this session).

**Specifics.** The chapter appears in Martin Greenberger (ed.), *Computers, Communications, and the Public Interest* (Johns Hopkins Press, 1971), and was prepared for a conference on the impact of computers on society. It is an essay, not an empirical study, and contains no sample sizes or effect measurements. Its main moves are these. First, organizations are information processors with limited serial capacity, so the organization must decide what to attend to. Second, the value of an information-processing system is the benefit of decisions it improves minus the cost of the attention consumed, so adding messages can reduce net value. Third, the most useful systems act as filters and summarizers that lower the number of items a person must process; Simon says that designers should be willing to remove more information than they add. Fourth, because humans process information roughly serially and hold only a small amount in short-term memory, interfaces that push many items at once cause queues and neglected items. He also argues that managers should think in terms of the rate at which an organization can process information when designing its structure, and that decentralization and the creation of specialized sub-units are ways of dividing attention. The passage on a poverty of attention is the most quoted line, and it is the intellectual ancestor of the attention-economy literature.

**Criticism and status.** There is nothing to replicate, because the text is conceptual. Its practical claim is supported by later cognitive load and attention research (see the multimedia learning lane). The sentence is often quoted out of context as a slogan; the chapter's actual recommendation is about designing filters and about organizational structure. Page range of the chapter is commonly cited as 37-72, but this was not confirmed this session and has been removed from the bibliographic record.

**Use for LTF.**
- Treat every element on a lesson screen as a withdrawal from a small attention budget the learner also needs for the simulator.
- In fly mode, show only what the current phase of flight needs; move everything else behind a deliberate gesture.
- Summaries, checklists and progress bars are filters; judge them by whether they reduce items to process.
- Count a feature's cost in attention (seconds of looking away from the sim), not in pixels.
- When in doubt, remove information from the screen before adding any.

## Tversky1974
**Core claims.** Probability and frequency judgments under uncertainty rely on a small number of heuristic principles that reduce complex inference to simpler operations. These are often useful but lead to systematic and predictable errors. The paper names three: representativeness (judging likelihood by similarity to a stereotype), availability (judging frequency by ease of retrieval), and adjustment and anchoring (starting from an initial value and adjusting insufficiently).

**Specifics.** Representativeness is linked to neglect of prior probabilities (base rates), insensitivity to sample size, misconceptions of chance (the gambler's fallacy), insensitivity to predictability, the illusion of validity and misconceptions of regression. Availability is illustrated by the classic letter question: people judge words that begin with K to be more common than words with K in third position, although the latter are more numerous, because the former are easier to retrieve. Imaginability and illusory correlation are also covered. The best known experiment is anchoring. Participants watched a wheel of fortune stopped at a number between 0 and 100, then said whether the percentage of African nations in the United Nations was higher or lower than that number and gave an estimate. Median estimates differed by anchor (commonly quoted: about 25 for a low anchor of 10 and about 45 for a high anchor of 65; not re-confirmed this session). Offering payoffs for accuracy did not remove the anchoring effect. A second demonstration asked students to estimate within a few seconds a product written as a descending or ascending sequence of numbers from one to eight; the descending sequence yielded a much larger median estimate (commonly quoted: about 2,250 versus about 512; the true value is 40,320). The explanation given is insufficient adjustment from a starting point that is partly computed. The paper also notes that experts such as statisticians were subject to some of the same errors.

**Criticism and status.** Anchoring is one of the most robust phenomena in judgment research and has replicated in large multi-laboratory projects, though the size of the effect varies with the task and anchor plausibility. The heuristic labels are more contested: Gigerenzer and colleagues argue that "representativeness" and "availability" are vague descriptions that do not predict when errors will appear, and that errors depend on question format (natural frequencies reduce base-rate neglect). The heuristics-and-biases program also faces the criticism that the errors are shown in artificial vignettes rather than natural environments. Kahneman's later book contains chapters on social priming that did not replicate; this 1974 paper is not part of that problem.

**Use for LTF.**
- First numbers anchor: show honest typical lesson duration and typical beginner scores rather than a flattering low figure.
- Avoid anchoring learners on perfect scores; show the realistic range of first attempts.
- For safety content, vivid accidents distort availability; prefer frequencies and base rates over anecdotes.
- Present risk as natural frequencies ("about 3 of 10 first attempts") rather than percentages where possible.
- When setting a challenge target, remember that whatever number is displayed first becomes the reference.

## Kahneman1979
**Core claims.** Expected utility theory fails as a description of choices under risk. Prospect theory replaces it with three features: (1) outcomes are evaluated as gains and losses relative to a reference point, not as final states of wealth; (2) the value function is concave for gains, convex for losses and steeper for losses than for gains (loss aversion); (3) probabilities are transformed into decision weights that overweight small probabilities and underweight moderate and high ones.

**Specifics.** The paper reports many choice problems answered by university students and faculty in Israel, Sweden and the United States, using hypothetical gambles with monetary stakes. The certainty effect: people overweight outcomes that are certain relative to probable ones. In the best known pair, a large majority preferred a sure 3,000 to an 80 percent chance of 4,000 (commonly quoted: about 80 percent; not re-confirmed this session), though the gamble has the higher expected value. The reflection effect: when the same options are posed as losses, preferences reverse, so people prefer a gamble to a sure loss. The isolation effect: people simplify a choice by ignoring the components that options share, so different decompositions of the same problem produce different choices. The paper also treats editing operations (coding, combination, segregation, cancellation) that people apply before evaluation. The mathematically fuller cumulative prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman 1992, Journal of Risk and Uncertainty) estimated a loss aversion coefficient of roughly two for money and a diminishing sensitivity parameter below one; these parameter estimates vary by study and should not be used as universal constants.

**Criticism and status.** The qualitative patterns (certainty effect, reflection, reference dependence) are broadly replicable and have been reproduced across many countries (Ruggeri and colleagues, 2020, Nature Human Behaviour, report broad replication across a large international sample; details not confirmed this session). The general claim that losses loom larger than gains is contested: Gal and Rucker (2018, Journal of Consumer Psychology 28(3), 497-516) argue that evidence for loss aversion as a general principle is weaker than commonly assumed and that findings often reflect inaction or attention rather than a higher weight on losses. Other work finds loss aversion is weaker for small stakes. Stimuli are hypothetical, though real-stakes studies show similar patterns.

**Use for LTF.**
- Streak and progress-loss mechanics exploit loss framing. Use them sparingly and never to coerce attendance (see Luguri2021).
- Describe mistakes as information gained rather than points lost.
- Certainty is overvalued: "you will finish this lesson in 10 minutes" reads stronger than "probably about 10 minutes".
- Do not build a fixed "losses count double" rule into scoring or reward design.
- Use reference points deliberately: show the learner's own earlier performance as the reference, not a stranger's.

## Tversky1981
**Core claims.** Choices depend on how a decision problem is framed, even when the frames are logically equivalent. People construct a mental representation of acts, outcomes and contingencies, and the representation depends on wording, on the reference point the wording sets, and on what is coded as a gain or a loss. Because people are risk averse in the domain of gains and risk seeking in the domain of losses, changing the frame can reverse a preference. This violates the invariance principle that normative theories require.

**Specifics.** The Asian disease problem asks respondents to imagine that the United States is preparing for an outbreak expected to kill 600 people, with two programs. In the gain frame, Program A saves 200 people for sure while Program B has a one-third chance of saving all 600 and a two-thirds chance of saving nobody. In the loss frame, Program C means 400 people die for sure, while Program D has a one-third chance that nobody dies and a two-thirds chance that 600 die. The outcomes are identical, yet the majority chose the sure option in the gain frame and the gamble in the loss frame (commonly quoted: about 72 percent versus 28 percent in the gain frame, and about 22 percent versus 78 percent in the loss frame, with samples of roughly 150 per group; not re-confirmed this session). The paper also introduces mental accounting through the jacket and calculator problem: far more people said they would travel to another store to save a fixed amount on a cheap calculator than the same amount on an expensive jacket (commonly quoted: about 68 percent versus about 29 percent for a saving of 5 dollars on 15 versus 125 dollars; not re-confirmed this session), although the absolute saving is the same. Further sections cover the framing of transactions as insurance versus a sure loss, sunk costs and the framing of outcomes in the context of a reference account, together with the distinction between "minimal", "topical" and "comprehensive" accounts. The paper argues that frames are usually not noticed and that people rarely reconcile different framings of the same problem.

**Criticism and status.** The gain/loss framing effect has replicated, including in the Many Labs 2 project (Klein and colleagues, 2018), though effect sizes are usually smaller than the original and depend on wording. A meta-analysis by Kuhberger (1998) found a reliable framing effect of small to moderate size that varies with the type of problem. Effects shrink when decisions are consequential, when respondents are experts, or when the options are made transparent. Not every wording choice in a user interface is this effect: the original manipulation changes only the description of identical outcomes, not the information provided. Studies with differing information (for example, "90 percent fat free" versus "10 percent fat") are a related attribute-framing effect and are generally more robust than risky-choice framing.

**Use for LTF.**
- State pass rates and risks in a consistent frame ("9 of 10 learners complete this lesson") and avoid switching frames to sway behavior.
- For safety items, present the honest frame and both the relative and absolute numbers.
- Mental accounting suggests learners judge effort relative to the whole: 5 minutes feels small in a 2-hour course and large in a 10-minute challenge; indicate where a task sits in the whole.
- Do not frame skipped checklist items as "losing" points to push compliance; teach why the item matters.
- When comparing options, put them in a common frame (same units, same direction).

## Johnson2003
**Core claims.** Defaults have large effects on consent to organ donation, and the policy design of the default may be among the most consequential choices in the system. People tend to leave a default in place because deciding requires effort, because the default may signal what the choice architect recommends, and because the default becomes the reference point for what is lost.

**Specifics.** The article (Science, 2003) is a short report that has two parts. The first compares European countries by the share of the population that is an effective consenting donor under opt-in or opt-out (presumed consent) legislation. The two best-known cases, which were confirmed this session through coverage of the paper, are Germany, an opt-in country where about 12 percent of people were registered, and Austria, an opt-out country with a rate of about 99.98 percent. Other countries in the comparison (for example, Denmark, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, Belgium, France, Hungary, Poland, Portugal and Sweden) are reported in the paper's figure, but the individual percentages were not confirmed this session and are not reproduced here. The second part is an online experiment in which participants imagined moving to a new state and were assigned to an opt-in, opt-out or neutral (forced choice) condition; the opt-out condition produced much higher consent than opt-in, with the neutral condition close to opt-out (exact percentages and sample size not confirmed this session). The authors argue that the gap is too large to be explained by cultural differences alone and propose that a change in the default could substantially increase the donor pool; their practical estimate is that each donor can supply organs for several transplants.

**Criticism and status.** The country comparison is correlational and confounded by culture, religion, law and health-system design. Registration rates are not donation rates: real donation depends on family consent practices, intensive care capacity and transplant infrastructure, and later analyses found that opt-out countries do not uniformly outperform opt-in countries in actual donation. A 2025 paper in PNAS Nexus (Gunturkun and colleagues) argues that opt-out defaults for deceased donation may crowd out living donation. The study is most useful as a vivid demonstration that defaults matter, not as an estimate of a policy effect. For better quantification see the meta-analysis in Jachimowicz2019.

**Use for LTF.**
- Defaults decide for most learners: set beginner-appropriate defaults for assistance level, units and display, and explain them.
- Choose the default that serves the learner rather than the engagement metric.
- Pre-checked consent boxes and pre-selected paid plans are dark-pattern defaults; do not use them.
- Follow the system theme preference as the default for Day and Night modes.
- Make every default visibly changeable; a default that cannot be inspected becomes coercion.

## Jachimowicz2019
**Core claims.** Defaults have, on average, a substantial effect on choices, but the size varies greatly with context. The authors propose three channels through which a default operates: endorsement (the default signals what the choice architect thinks is best), endowment (the default becomes the reference point, so leaving it feels like a loss) and ease (staying with the default requires the least effort).

**Specifics.** The meta-analysis pooled 58 studies with a total sample of 73,675 participants. The overall effect was Cohen's d about 0.68, with a 95 percent confidence interval of roughly 0.53 to 0.83; a Columbia press release quotes 0.63 to 0.68, so quote the paper rather than secondary summaries. Heterogeneity was substantial and two studies showed negative effects. Moderators: defaults were stronger in consumer domains and weaker in environmental ones. The authors coded the studies for how strongly each of the three mechanisms was likely to be present and found partial support, with the strongest support for endowment and ease and the endorsement result less clear. They also report that defaults were more effective when decision makers had weaker preferences, lacked knowledge, or faced a choice with high effort, and that explicit endorsement or an active choice request changed the effect.

**Criticism and status.** This is the largest quantitative synthesis of default effects, authored by the researchers who built the field, which may bias interpretation. Given the later evidence on publication bias in nudge research (Maier2022, DellaVigna2022), d = 0.68 is probably an upper bound. Field studies of pension enrolment find large effects, whereas studies in which participants have strong prior preferences find smaller effects. Defaults can backfire when people reject the implied endorsement or when the default is seen as manipulative.

**Reading the number carefully.** A pooled d near 0.68 is large by the conventions of social science, yet it sits on top of very different outcome measures (enrolment rates, donation consent, product choices, energy tariffs) that were converted to a common metric. The heterogeneity means a designer cannot read the figure as a forecast for a specific screen. A more defensible reading is ordinal: defaults usually beat information and exhortation in the same setting, and defaults matter most when people are indifferent, uninformed or short of time. A beginner flight learner meeting a settings screen for the first time is close to that condition on all three counts, which is why default choices for assistance level, units and checklist style deserve explicit design attention and testing rather than being left to whatever the framework ships with. The authors' three-part framework also gives a practical checklist: ask whether the default signals a recommendation, whether leaving it feels like keeping something, and how much effort a change would take.

**Use for LTF.**
- Defaults are the most reliable choice-architecture lever; decide them explicitly in onboarding and settings.
- A default implies endorsement, so label recommended options honestly ("Recommended for your first flight").
- Expect smaller default effects for learners with strong prior preferences, such as experienced pilots.
- Record which defaults learners change; frequent changes show the default is wrong for that group.
- Because defaults are strong, review them as an ethical decision, not only a conversion decision.

## Iyengar2000
**Core claims.** Providing more choices can demotivate: people presented with an extensive set of options are less likely to choose at all, and are less satisfied and less confident in the choice they make. The authors argue that extensive choice, which looks attractive, carries costs of cognitive effort and anticipated regret.

**Specifics.** Three studies. Study 1 was a field experiment at an upscale grocery store, where a tasting table displayed either 24 jams or 6 jams on alternating days. More passers-by stopped at the larger display, but a far smaller proportion of those who stopped there went on to buy (commonly quoted: about 60 percent versus 40 percent stopping, and about 3 percent versus 30 percent buying; not re-confirmed this session). Study 2 was a classroom experiment where students were offered either 6 or 30 optional essay topics for extra credit; those given fewer options were more likely to write the essay and wrote better essays as rated by graders. Study 3 was a laboratory study with chocolates: participants choosing from a limited set (6) reported more satisfaction with their choice and less regret than those choosing from 30, and were more likely to pick chocolate as payment over cash. The paper distinguishes between enjoyment of having many options in the abstract and the experience of choosing.

**Criticism and status.** The result is not robust. A meta-analysis of 50 experiments (Scheibehenne and colleagues 2010) found a mean effect near zero with wide variation, and Chernev and colleagues 2015 identified conditions under which overload does appear. The grocery study was conducted in a single store, with a small number of days and no independent replication that matches its numbers. Barry Schwartz's "paradox of choice" popularized the claim as a general law, which the evidence does not support. Specific numbers such as "six options is optimal" are not a finding of this paper.

**How the studies differ.** Study 1 measured a behavioral outcome (purchase) in the field, but the participants were shoppers who chose whether to stop at the display, so the two conditions were self-selected after the first step. Studies 2 and 3 used students and measured effort, quality and satisfaction under assignment to condition. Together they argue for two separate effects: a motivational one (not choosing at all) and an experiential one (being less satisfied after choosing). Later work has found the second more reliably than the first, and has found that the demotivation shows up when choosers lack a clear idea of what they want. The study is therefore best used to motivate a question for testing in LTF, namely whether a long list of lessons or challenges stops beginners from starting, and not to prescribe a number of items. The authors themselves framed the result as a limit on the assumption that more choice is always better for motivation, not as a rule that choice should be minimized.

**Use for LTF.**
- Do not cite this study to justify an arbitrary option limit; test the real choice sets in the app.
- Beginners with unclear preferences are the condition where overload is most plausible; curate their choices.
- Support expert users with search and filters so a larger catalog does not cost them.
- Measure completion, not clicks, when comparing menu designs.
- Offer a recommended default when a choice set is large and complex.

## Scheibehenne2010
**Core claims.** Across the empirical literature there is no reliable main effect of choice overload. The mean effect is virtually zero, with considerable variance between studies: in some experiments more options lead to worse outcomes, in others to better ones. The authors argue that the oft-cited claim that too many options always harm choice is not supported, and that overload occurs only under specific, not yet well-defined conditions.

**Specifics.** The meta-analysis (Journal of Consumer Research 37(3), 409-425) covers 63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments with a total of 5,036 participants. The pooled mean effect is close to zero in effect size terms. The authors examine candidate moderators: the size of the choice set, the complexity of the options, the difficulty of the decision, the uncertainty of preference, the presence of a justification requirement and time pressure, and whether the dependent measure is satisfaction, regret, purchase or choice deferral. They find that choice overload tends to be more probable when decision makers have no clear preferences, when the choice set is complex and when options are hard to compare, but no condition was sufficient to produce overload reliably. Analyses showed that the "more is better" pattern occurs especially when people have clear prior preferences. The authors suggest that earlier demonstrations (such as Iyengar and Lepper 2000) may have been idiosyncratic to their stimuli and that some idiosyncratic moderators may explain when overload appears.

**Criticism and status.** Chernev, Bockenholt and Goodman (2015) argue that the first meta-analysis combined incomparable studies and that overload is a conditional phenomenon that appears under identifiable circumstances; Scheibehenne and colleagues replied in 2011 (commentary in the same journal). The two meta-analyses are best read together. Neither supports the popular statement that fewer options always improve choice, and neither supports the opposite. File-drawer problems affect any meta-analysis, and unpublished experiments were included to reduce that.

**Why the paper matters for practice.** The review changed the status of a popular claim from a finding to a question. Before it, many design articles stated that more options reduce conversion as if it were settled. After it, the defensible statement is that option count alone does not predict the outcome, and that features of the chooser and the choice set do. For a product team the useful move is to define the situation: who is choosing (beginner or expert), what the goal is (find the best option or start somewhere), and how hard the options are to compare. The authors also remind readers that the null average does not mean no one is ever overloaded; averaging across opposite effects can hide real cases. In a learning app, a mean effect near zero in published experiments is no reason to ignore a high abandonment rate on a long menu, which is a direct local measurement.

**Use for LTF.**
- Never write "too many choices is bad" as a rule in the design knowledge base; state the conditions.
- A/B test menu size and structure with the actual learners.
- When learners have clear goals (practice a specific maneuver), a large catalog is acceptable.
- Pair large catalogs with strong structure (categories, ordering, search).
- Treat the "paradox of choice" as a hypothesis for beginners, not a law.

## Chernev2015
**Core claims.** Choice overload is real but conditional. A larger assortment can produce negative outcomes (deferral, switching, lower satisfaction, lower confidence) when specific conditions hold, and can produce positive outcomes otherwise. The authors propose a conceptual framework in which assortment size affects outcomes through the cognitive effort of comparing options, and identify four moderators that determine when the effect appears.

**Specifics.** The meta-analysis (Journal of Consumer Psychology 25(2), 333-358) coded 99 observations from 53 or so prior studies, with a combined sample of 7,202 participants. The four moderators: choice set complexity (more attributes, more similar options, weaker structure, no dominant option); decision task difficulty (time pressure, need to justify, many attributes to trade off); preference uncertainty (people who do not know what they want, or lack expertise, are affected more); and decision goal (a goal of finding the best option and a goal of browsing differ, with maximizing goals increasing overload). When these moderators are taken into account, assortment size has a significant effect on overload outcomes, in contrast with the near-zero mean in Scheibehenne2010. The paper distinguishes antecedents (set size, set complexity), the underlying mechanisms (cognitive load, decision difficulty, regret and demotivation) and consequences (choice deferral, switching likelihood, reduced satisfaction and reduced confidence). It also distinguishes the impact of an assortment on the decision process from its impact on the outcome. Effect sizes are heterogeneous and the pooled overall effect, before moderation, is small.

**Criticism and status.** The moderators were identified partly after looking at the data, and the authors had earlier published a commentary arguing against the first meta-analysis (Chernev 2010 commentary, same journal as Scheibehenne), so there is an advocacy dimension. The sample of 99 observations is larger than but overlaps with the earlier one. Both meta-analyses are consistent with a view that overload exists in a subset of conditions and that the average across all published studies is small. A reasonable summary for practitioners is "conditional and context dependent".

**Relation to the first meta-analysis.** The two papers used different inclusion rules and different outcome codings, which is one reason the headline numbers differ. A careful reader should avoid picking the paper that fits a preferred design. What both support is that the interaction between assortment size and the four moderators is where the information lies. In practice this means a team should place its own situation on each moderator: for an onboarding lesson list, complexity is moderate, difficulty is low, preference uncertainty is high and the goal is to start. That profile points toward guided choice, with one recommended path, and toward allowing exploration later, once preferences form. It also suggests that structure can change the effective complexity of a long list, so grouping lessons into phases of flight can reduce overload without removing content.

**Use for LTF.**
- Treat new learners as the high-risk group: unclear preferences, complex set, difficult task.
- Reduce set complexity through structure (categories, sensible ordering, clear differences), not only by showing fewer items.
- Use a recommended default when the goal is to choose what to do next; allow free browsing when the goal is exploration.
- Make options differ along few, clearly labeled dimensions (duration, topic, difficulty).
- Allow comparison without memorizing (side-by-side summaries).

## Mertens2022
**Core claims.** Choice architecture interventions (nudges) have, on average, a small to medium effect on behavior across domains, and the effect depends on the type of intervention and the behavior targeted. The paper is the largest synthesis to date of the empirical nudge literature.

**Specifics.** The meta-analysis (PNAS 119(1), e2107346118, published online 30 December 2021) covers more than 200 studies reporting over 440 effect sizes, with a combined sample of about 2.15 million participants. The overall effect was Cohen's d = 0.45 (95 percent confidence interval 0.39 to 0.52). The authors classify interventions into decision information (for example labeling, reminders), decision structure (for example defaults, changes in effort or ordering) and decision assistance categories. Decision structure interventions had the largest effects. Domain also moderated: food-related behavior was most responsive, with effects up to about 2.5 times larger than in other domains, while financial, environmental and prosocial domains showed smaller effects. The authors report evidence of moderate publication bias, and note that their conclusion is that nudging is effective on average but that there is considerable heterogeneity.

**Criticism and status.** This paper is the starting point of an important dispute. Maier and colleagues (Maier2022) applied robust Bayesian meta-analysis and found no remaining evidence of an effect once publication bias was modeled. A separate PNAS letter by Szaszi and colleagues (2022) argues that the adjusted average falls between d of about 0.03 (under severe publication bias) and 0.31 (under moderate bias), and that heterogeneity is so large that an average is not informative. DellaVigna and Linos (2022) found much smaller effects in unselected field trials. Mertens and colleagues responded that the evidence is not uniformly negative. Treat d = 0.45 as an upper bound, not a forecast.

**What the moderator results suggest.** The finding that decision structure interventions outperform information interventions is consistent with the default literature and with common sense: changing what is easiest to do is more reliable than asking people to care. The size of the food-domain effect, which is largely about choosing between items in a physical or menu environment, shows why transfer to other domains is uncertain. A learning app deals with sustained, effortful behavior rather than a single menu choice. The paper also reports the share of studies from different domains and the dominance of laboratory and online samples, so ecological validity is a further concern. The authors state that effect sizes should be interpreted with caution because of heterogeneity and bias, which is the cautious reading this knowledge base adopts.

**Use for LTF.**
- Plan on small effects: a few percentage points on a behavior, not 30 percent.
- Structural nudges (defaults, reducing effort, ordering) have stronger support than reminders and messages.
- Do not cite "nudges work, d = 0.45" in the knowledge base; cite specific interventions with evidence and caveats.
- Run in-app experiments with enough learners to detect small effects, and report nulls.
- Choose interventions by domain similarity: effects in food and finance may not transfer to learning.

## Maier2022
**Core claims.** After adjusting for publication bias with robust Bayesian meta-analysis (RoBMA), the evidence for an overall nudging effect disappears, and the data are in fact most consistent with strong publication bias and a small or null average effect. The letter argues that the headline conclusion of Mertens2022 depends on a choice of ignoring publication bias.

**Specifics.** The authors (Maier, Bartos, Stanley, Shanks, Harris and Wagenmakers) reanalyzed the Mertens data set. RoBMA averages across a family of models that include or exclude an effect, heterogeneity and publication bias, weighting each model by how well it predicts the data, so it avoids a binary decision about whether publication bias is present. Their result was strong evidence for publication bias and, after adjustment, no evidence for an effect; the paper's title says so. The letter was published in PNAS 119(31), e2200300119 (July 2022) and the data and code are on the Open Science Framework (osf.io/svz6e). In a later rejoinder the authors accepted that a more careful title would be "No Overall Evidence for Nudging After Adjusting for Publication Bias", clarified that the Bayes factor for an overall effect is undecided rather than evidence against nudging, and reported that after correcting for bias about 21.5 percent of academic nudge effects are larger than d = 0.3.

**Criticism and status.** Data Colada criticized the analysis for pooling very different interventions, arguing that an average across defaults, labels and reminders hides specific effects such as defaults that are probably real. Others noted that RoBMA is itself model dependent. The consensus is that the nudge literature is affected by selection, that the average effect is lower than published numbers, and that specific interventions require their own evidence. The finding is a caution about aggregate evidence, not a refutation of defaults.

**How to use this exchange.** The exchange between Mertens, Maier, Szaszi and others is an instructive example of how meta-analytic conclusions depend on modeling choices, and the lane on evidence quality covers the methods. For design decisions the relevant lesson is procedural. First, a pooled effect size from a heterogeneous literature is a weak basis for a specific design choice. Second, publication bias is expected wherever small positive results are rewarded, which applies to many published behavioral interventions. Third, the strongest evidence for a specific intervention is a well-powered, preregistered trial in a comparable setting, such as the nudge-unit trials in DellaVigna2022. A team that cannot run such a trial should prefer interventions with a plausible mechanism, low cost and low downside, such as removing friction, over those that depend on a large behavioral effect to be worthwhile.

**Use for LTF.**
- Treat aggregate "nudge works" claims as unreliable; require intervention-specific evidence.
- Pre-register app experiments and report null results internally.
- Prefer boosting competence (teaching skills) over relying on nudge effect sizes.
- Where the knowledge base cites a nudge effect, give the range and the criticism.
- Do not interpret a null average as proof that defaults or friction changes do nothing.

## DellaVigna2022
**Core claims.** Published academic nudge studies substantially overstate the effects of nudges deployed at scale. Trials run by government nudge units show effects roughly one-fifth the size of those in the academic literature, mainly because of publication selection and low statistical power.

**Specifics.** The paper (Econometrica 90(1), 81-116, January 2022) analyzes every randomized trial run by two large US nudge units (the Office of Evaluation Sciences and Nudge/Behavioral Insights Team North America): 126 randomized controlled trials covering more than 23 million individuals. The average effect of a nudge on take-up (for example enrolment, form completion, payments) was 1.4 percentage points, about an 8.0 percent relative increase over control. In contrast, the average published academic nudge in a comparison sample of 26 papers raised take-up by 8.7 percentage points, a 33.5 percent increase. A meta-analysis model attributes about 70 percent of the gap to selective publication of academic results, exacerbated by low statistical power, with the rest explained by differences in nudge characteristics and settings. The authors also surveyed experts and found that predictions overstated the effects of the trials. Typical nudges included reminder letters and emails, simplified forms and social comparisons.

**Criticism and status.** The sample is unusually complete, so selection bias is limited, which is its main strength. The limits: government settings, mostly one-time communications and behaviors with a concrete observable outcome, so generalization to continuing engagement with a learning product is indirect. The gap between 1.4 and 8.7 points has become a standard reference for expected effect sizes.

**What counts as the unit of evidence.** The unit here is the trial, not the paper, and every trial from the two units is included, whether or not it succeeded, which is what makes the sample unusual. The comparison with the academic sample shows that the problem is not that nudges have no effect but that published effects are inflated. The authors note that predictions by academics and practitioners about the trials were too optimistic, which is a reminder that expert intuition about nudge size is poorly calibrated. For product design, a 1.4 point lift in a completion rate on a large user base can still be worth having, and a realistic power calculation should assume an effect of that order. This also means that small tests of a few hundred learners will usually fail to detect a real nudge effect, so decisions about nudges often have to rest on cost, reversibility and ethics rather than on measured lift.

**Use for LTF.**
- Assume that a nudge raises a behavior rate by a point or two, and budget experiments accordingly.
- Reminders and simplification still have value at scale: 1.4 points across a large user base matters.
- Run randomized tests inside the app, with sample sizes large enough for small effects.
- Be suspicious of any claim of a dramatic effect from a single study.
- Prioritize removing friction (simpler forms, fewer steps) over persuasive messaging.

## Mathur2019
**Core claims.** Dark patterns, user interface designs that benefit the service at the expense of the user, are widespread on shopping sites, can be found through systematic measurement, and are sometimes sold as third-party services. The paper supplies the first large-scale empirical taxonomy and prevalence estimate.

**Specifics.** The study (Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction 3 (CSCW), Article 81, November 2019) crawled about 11,000 shopping websites and about 53,000 product pages, using automated detection of text-based patterns followed by manual review. It found 1,818 instances of dark patterns, classified into 15 types in 7 broader categories (sneaking, urgency, misdirection, social proof, scarcity, obstruction and forced action). Instances were present on about 1,254 of the roughly 11,000 sites, about 11 percent. A subset of 234 instances across 183 websites was classified as deceptive, including fake countdown timers and fabricated activity or low-stock messages. The authors also identified 22 third-party entities that provide dark patterns as turnkey tools, such as plugins that display fabricated purchase notifications. Dark patterns were more prevalent on higher-ranked (more popular) sites. The paper's taxonomy builds on earlier work by Brignull and Gray and colleagues and adds attributes (asymmetric, covert, deceptive, information hiding, restrictive) describing how each pattern operates.

**Criticism and status.** The coverage is shopping sites only, English-language, and the detection is conservative, so prevalence is a lower bound. Counts measure presence, not harm to users; the paper does not test whether each pattern changes behavior (Luguri2021 does). The taxonomy is widely used in regulation and research.

**Method and limits in more detail.** The authors built a crawler that visited product pages, segmented page text into elements and used clustering and manual inspection to find recurring text patterns associated with dark patterns, then confirmed them by hand. This approach finds patterns that are expressed in text, such as countdown notices or "only 2 left" messages, and misses patterns that depend on layout, color or interaction flow, such as a visually dominant accept button, which are harder to detect automatically. The paper therefore understates prevalence of the visual and interactive types. The attributes the authors propose (asymmetric, covert, deceptive, information hiding, restrictive) help explain why each type is a problem, and make it possible to ask of a new feature whether it hides information, restricts choice or deceives. These questions are more portable than the list of 15 types.

**Use for LTF.**
- Audit LTF against the 15 types before release; no fake timers, fabricated activity, confirmshaming or hidden costs.
- Vet third-party scripts, since the paper shows that dark patterns can arrive as plugins.
- Treat social proof and scarcity claims as factual statements that must be true.
- Use the taxonomy as a checklist in design review for any motivation feature.
- Keep an internal log of any pressure mechanic and its justification.

## Luguri2021
**Core claims.** Dark patterns are effective at changing consumer behavior, and the aggressive versions generate backlash while the mild ones do not, which makes the mild ones the more insidious. Susceptibility is higher among less-educated people. The authors argue that the legal system (for example, US unfair and deceptive practices law) should treat many such designs as unlawful.

**Specifics.** Two large online experiments on census-weighted samples of American adults (Journal of Legal Analysis 13(1), 2021). In Study 1, participants completed a survey, were told they had been enrolled in an identity-theft protection plan and were asked whether to accept it. Participants were randomly assigned to a control, a mild dark pattern condition or an aggressive dark pattern condition, which varied the steps needed to decline. The mild group was roughly twice as likely to accept the dubious plan as the control group, and the aggressive group nearly four times as likely (the exact acceptance percentages could not be confirmed this session and are not reproduced). Aggressive patterns produced strong negative reactions (anger, lower willingness to do business again), while mild patterns did not. Less-educated respondents were significantly more susceptible to mild patterns. Study 2 tested individual techniques: hidden information, trick questions and obstruction (making it hard to decline) worked well, while "must act now" messages did not significantly raise uptake, and the study found that decision architecture, not the price, drove uptake.

**Criticism and status.** Strong randomized design but online hypothetical stakes; independent replication in other countries and products is limited. The distinction between mild and aggressive is by design and not a continuum measured in the field.

**Why the finding matters for design ethics.** Two points from the paper matter beyond the numbers. First, the effect of the manipulation was driven by the structure of the choice (what it took to decline), not by the price or by the appeal of the product, so the same offer yielded very different uptake under different interfaces. Second, the interface that produced the most conversions also produced the most anger, whereas the interface that produced a moderate lift left people unaware, which means that satisfaction surveys will not catch the mild version. A team using only conversion and satisfaction metrics would see a gain without a cost. The authors therefore recommend regulation that looks at the design itself. For LTF, the implication is that the review of motivation and sign-up flows should be done by inspection against a checklist, not by watching metrics alone.

**Use for LTF.**
- Mild patterns are the real risk because they convert and are not noticed; commit to avoiding even gentle tricks.
- Never use trick wording, hidden costs or obscured cancellation.
- Beginners and less-experienced users are the more susceptible group, so the audience raises the ethical bar.
- Ensure declining takes no more steps than accepting.
- Keep a record that LTF engagement features pass this test.

## Sunstein2022
**Core claims.** Sludge is friction that makes it harder for people to get what they want or do what they intend: excessive paperwork, waiting, complexity, confusing forms and unnecessary steps. It is the counterpart of nudging, and organizations should regularly audit their processes, count the costs and cut sludge unless it is justified.

**Specifics.** The article (Behavioural Public Policy 6(4), 654-673, issue of October 2022, first published online January 2020) proposes sludge audits: structured reviews, which may be quantitative or qualitative, that identify steps, measure time, money and psychological burden, and compare them with benefits. The TSA PreCheck program is used as an example of reducing sludge in a specific setting. The paper argues that sludge falls hardest on vulnerable and disadvantaged people, that it can be deliberate or the result of neglect, and that some frictions have legitimate purposes (preventing fraud, ensuring informed consent), which requires a cost-benefit judgment. Related works: Thaler's 2018 Science essay "Nudge, not sludge" and Sunstein's 2021 book on sludge. A later literature compares sludge audits with dark pattern audits.

**Criticism and status.** This is a policy framework and not an empirical test, and it relies on subjective valuation of friction costs. It is also not clear where sludge ends and legitimate friction (a safety check) begins. Sunstein co-authored Nudge, and the evidence for nudging is now disputed, but removing friction is a less contested intervention than adding persuasion.

**Practical shape of an audit.** An audit begins by listing every step a person must take to complete a goal (for example, from landing page to first lesson, or from first lesson to deleting an account), then recording for each step the time, the information required and whether it can be skipped or deferred. Each step is then compared with the benefit it provides, either to the person or to a legitimate purpose such as security. Steps that provide no benefit, or whose benefit goes entirely to the organization, are candidates for removal. Sunstein also stresses that sludge is not neutral across users: those with less time, less education or less confidence bear more of the cost. In an app whose audience includes complete beginners, a form that experienced users complete without noticing may be a real barrier, so the audit should be done from the beginner's point of view.

**Use for LTF.**
- Audit sign-up, progress sync, settings and account deletion for sludge: count steps, time and required fields.
- Distinguish productive friction (a checklist read-back that teaches) from sludge (an account wall before a free lesson).
- Make cancellation and data deletion as easy as sign-up.
- Report the audit result to the team as a number of steps and seconds.
- Remove steps before adding persuasion.

## Gigerenzer2011
**Core claims.** Heuristics are not just inferior substitutes for optimization; in uncertain environments simple rules can match or beat complex ones. The key concept is ecological rationality: a heuristic is good or bad relative to the structure of the environment in which it is used. The review (Annual Review of Psychology 62, 451-482) summarizes the research program of the "adaptive toolbox" and contrasts it with the heuristics-and-biases approach, which it criticizes for vague labels and for judging errors against logical norms.

**Specifics.** Heuristics reviewed include the recognition heuristic (if one of two objects is recognized, infer it has the higher value), take-the-best (use the first cue that discriminates), tallying (count cues, ignoring weights), and 1/N (divide resources equally); the review discusses a study showing that 1/N performed competitively with optimizing portfolio models, and fast-and-frugal decision trees used for medical triage and legal decisions. The "less-is-more" effect says that in some conditions partial ignorance improves accuracy. The bias-variance tradeoff explains why simple models can predict better out of sample: they sacrifice a little bias to lower variance, especially with small, noisy samples. The article also describes how to study heuristics formally, by specifying them as computational models that can be tested against data.

**Criticism and status.** The recognition heuristic and the less-is-more claims have been challenged by other researchers (a 2011 debate in the journal Judgment and Decision Making), who find the heuristic is used less consistently than claimed. The program also faces the criticism that its strongest results concern prediction tasks with specific structure. Gigerenzer and colleagues have also criticized nudging as treating people as flawed; Hertwig and Grune-Yanoff (2017) propose "boosts" that build competence as an alternative.

**Why it matters beyond the lab.** A central argument is that the accuracy-effort tradeoff is not a simple line: with limited data or in noisy environments, ignoring information can improve prediction. This is relevant to the design of instrument displays and checklists, where adding more readouts or more steps does not necessarily improve decisions and can reduce them by diverting attention. Fast-and-frugal trees, which ask a short sequence of yes-no questions and exit at the first decisive answer, are a format already familiar to pilots in the form of go/no-go criteria, and are easy to render as simple diagrams. The review is also a reminder that many "biases" disappear or shrink when information is presented in formats that match how people learn from experience, such as natural frequencies, which supports presenting risk and performance statistics in counts rather than probabilities.

**Use for LTF.**
- Pilots use simple rules (checklists, flow patterns, "if-then" go/no-go rules); teach rules that fit the environment.
- Draw fast-and-frugal trees as diagrams for go/no-go decisions such as weather and fuel.
- Prefer boosting (teaching a skill or a rule) to nudging in an app whose purpose is competence.
- State the conditions in which a simple rule is valid.
- Remove cues from a decision display if they add noise.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** Decisions are made under limits of attention, time and information (Simon1955, Simon1971). Presentation matters: anchors, frames and defaults change what people choose (Tversky1974, Tversky1981, Johnson2003, Jachimowicz2019). Defaults are the most dependable lever in the choice-architecture toolbox. Dark patterns are real, measurable and effective (Mathur2019, Luguri2021), and friction should be audited and reduced where it is not serving the user (Sunstein2022). All sources treat the environment, not just the mind, as the object of design.

**Where they conflict.** Kahneman and Tversky read behavior as biased relative to a rational norm; Gigerenzer reads the same behavior as often well adapted and calls the biases vague. The size of nudge effects is the major open number: d = 0.45 in Mertens2022, near zero after bias adjustment in Maier2022, 1.4 percentage points in the field in DellaVigna2022. Choice overload: a near-zero mean (Scheibehenne2010) versus a conditional effect (Chernev2015). Loss aversion as a general law is contested (Gal and Rucker 2018). Claims from social priming and from some behavioral-economics popularizers do not hold: the 2012 PNAS paper on signing at the top of forms to increase honesty (Shu and colleagues) was retracted in 2021 after its field data were shown to be fabricated, and should not be cited as support.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Attention is the scarce resource; subtract before you add.
2. Learners satisfice, so make the first reasonable option the right one.
3. Set defaults deliberately and label them honestly as recommendations.
4. Expect small nudge effects, a point or two, and measure them in the app.
5. Do not cite choice overload as a law; for beginners, reduce complexity with structure and a single recommended next step.
6. First numbers anchor, so show honest durations and realistic score ranges.
7. Keep frames consistent and honest; do not use loss-framed pressure for engagement.
8. Audit flows for sludge; productive friction such as checklists is not sludge.
9. Never use dark patterns, including mild ones that convert and go unnoticed.
10. Prefer boosting (teach decision rules and skills) over nudging, because the product's goal is competence.
