# Lane 20: Judgment, decision making and dark patterns. Research notes

## tversky1974judgment
Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Judgment under uncertainty: Heuristics and biases. Science, 185(4157), 1124-1131.

### Core claims
People assess probabilities and predict values by relying on a small number of heuristic principles that turn hard judgments into simpler ones. The authors say these heuristics are quite useful but sometimes lead to severe and systematic errors. The paper names three: representativeness, availability, and adjustment from an anchor. The errors are predictable, so they reveal which shortcut produced them. This is an experimental and review article, and it launched the heuristics-and-biases research program.

### Specifics
Representativeness: when judging whether an object belongs to a class, people ask how similar it is to the stereotype of that class. This ignores factors that should matter. One is prior probability (base rate). In the engineer and lawyer experiment, subjects read brief personality descriptions allegedly drawn from a group of 100 professionals. In one condition the group was described as 70 engineers and 30 lawyers, in another as 30 engineers and 70 lawyers. Bayes' rule says the odds should differ by a factor of 5.44, yet the subjects' probability judgments were essentially the same across the two conditions. Sample size is also neglected: in the hospital problem (about 45 versus about 15 births per day, asked which records more days with over 60 percent boys), the paper reports that most subjects answered that the two were about equally likely, though the smaller hospital is more likely to.

Availability: frequency or probability is judged by the ease with which instances come to mind. In the paper's word examples, people judge words beginning with a given letter to be more common than words with that letter in the third position, because they search memory by first letter.

Adjustment and anchoring: estimates start from an initial value and adjustments are typically insufficient, so different starting points yield different estimates biased toward the start. In the wheel of fortune demonstration, a number from 0 to 100 was determined by spinning a wheel in the subjects' presence. Subjects said whether the number was higher or lower than the true value (the percentage of African countries in the United Nations), then adjusted from it. The median estimates were 25 and 45 for groups that received 10 and 65 as starting points. The paper states that payoffs for accuracy did not reduce the anchoring effect. A second demonstration had high school students estimate a product within 5 seconds: the median estimate for 1x2x3x4x5x6x7x8 was 512, for the descending version 2,250, while the correct answer is 40,320.

### Replication and criticism
The evidence is a set of questionnaire experiments with small samples, reported tersely. Anchoring is among the best-supported effects in the program; the Many Labs 2 project (Klein et al., 2018, Advances in Methods and Practices in Psychological Science) reports that anchoring and framing findings were among those reproduced, though the summary I could read said the framing effect was about half as strong as originally reported (psychologicalscience.org summary; I did not read the anchoring table). Criticism has come from the fast-and-frugal tradition (see gigerenzer2011heuristic), which argues that the program labeled heuristics as error-prone without specifying them as testable models, and that deviations from logic or probability theory are not necessarily irrational in an uncertain world. Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier note the program's influence from this 1974 paper onward, and the paper itself is careful to call heuristics "quite useful" in general. The specific demonstrations are robust in questionnaires; the broader claim of general irrationality is contested.

### Implications for LTF
Anchoring matters wherever LTF shows a number first. A challenge briefing that opens with a target value (for example a pitch or altitude target in a sim exercise) will pull the learner's own estimates toward it, and the anchor can be arbitrary. Quiz writers should avoid putting a misleading number in the question stem or in distractor order. Availability means vivid stories (an accident narrative) will inflate perceived frequency, so lessons that use incident examples should pair them with rates or context. Base-rate neglect argues for showing "how often" next to "what happens" in any risk-related lesson. On dashboards, a first displayed benchmark becomes an anchor for the learner's self-judgment, so show benchmarks as ranges. In fly mode, avoid decorative numbers in the display that are not decision-relevant, because anything numeric can anchor.

## tversky1981framing
Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1981). The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice. Science, 211(4481), 453-458.

### Core claims
Preferences between options can reverse when the same options are described differently. The authors define a decision frame as the decision maker's conception of the acts, outcomes and contingencies tied to a choice, controlled partly by how the problem is formulated and partly by the norms, habits and characteristics of the decision maker. They argue that choices involving gains are often risk averse and choices involving losses are often risk taking, and that people also evaluate outcomes inside mental accounts, so the same money can be treated differently depending on the account it is charged to. This is a set of questionnaire experiments, not a field study.

### Specifics
The respondents were students at Stanford University and the University of British Columbia who answered short questionnaires in class, and each problem reports its N and the percentage choosing each option.

The Asian disease problem: a disease expected to kill 600 people. In Problem 1 (N = 152), Program A saves 200 people for sure, Program B has a one-third probability that 600 are saved and a two-thirds probability that none are. 72 percent chose A and 28 percent chose B. In Problem 2 (N = 155), a different group saw the same consequences stated as deaths: Program C means 400 people will die, Program D has a one-third probability that nobody will die and a two-thirds probability that 600 will die. Only 22 percent chose C and 78 percent chose D. The outcomes are identical; only the wording (saved versus die) changed.

Problem 3 (N = 150) paired a sure gain of $240 against a 25 percent chance of $1,000 (84 percent chose the sure gain) with a sure loss of $750 against a 75 percent chance of losing $1,000 (87 percent chose the gamble).

Mental accounting: Problem 8 (N = 183) asked whether people would still pay $10 for a play ticket after losing a $10 bill; 88 percent said yes. Problem 9 (N = 200) said the ticket itself was lost; only 46 percent said they would buy another. The authors propose that the second purchase is entered in the account opened by the first, making the show cost $20. Problem 10 asked if people would drive 20 minutes to save $5: 68 percent would for a $15 calculator, 29 percent for a $125 calculator (groups of 93 and 88), although the saving is the same $5. The authors name the minimal account (just the $5 saving and the inconvenience) and the more inclusive account (the saving against the item price).

### Replication and criticism
The Asian disease result is one of the most cited demonstrations in decision research, and the paper is the source of "framing effect" as the field uses it. Later work shows the effect is sensitive to details. A Many Labs 2 summary reports that the framing effect was reproduced but at about half its original strength (psychologicalscience.org summary; I did not read the paper's per-effect table). So the safe reading is that wording changes choices reliably but the original magnitude is an overestimate. A standing objection is that "200 will be saved" can be read as "at least 200", so the two versions may not be identical in everyday language. The mental accounting examples are hypothetical survey answers, not tests with real money.

### Implications for LTF
Framing is the lowest-cost lever in an instructional app and the easiest to misuse. Wording such as "most learners who briefed first hit the target" versus "many learners who skipped the brief missed it" describes one fact two ways and will move behavior differently, so LTF should choose the frame that matches the true message and hold it across a lesson, not pick whichever frame converts better. Quiz stems should not switch between "correct" and "incorrect" framings of the same fact in the same set, because the switch alone changes answers. Progress dashboards should avoid loss frames ("you will lose your streak"); a gain frame ("3 of 12 lessons done") does not manufacture risk. Price or plan screens, if they ever exist, must present the full cost in one account, since the calculator problem shows a $5 difference matters differently depending on what it is compared with. Aviation numbers are not taken from this source.

## johnson2003defaults
Johnson, E. J., & Goldstein, D. G. (2003). Do defaults save lives? Science, 302(5649), 1338-1339.

### Core claims
The paper argues that the effect of a default (what happens if the person does nothing) on a high-stakes decision is very large, using organ donation as the test case. Countries that ask citizens to opt in to being a donor show much lower rates of agreement than countries where consent is presumed and citizens must opt out. The authors read this as evidence that preferences about donation are often constructed at the moment of the question and are therefore shaped by its form. Evidence type: a short report combining cross-country registry data (a natural experiment) with an online experiment.

### Specifics
I read the longer companion article by the same authors, "Defaults and Donation Decisions" (Transplantation, 2004, 78:1713-1716), which reports the same programme. Its online experiment asked 161 respondents whether they would be donors under one of three questions. In the opt-in condition they were told to assume they had moved to a new state whose default was not to be a donor, and could confirm or change it. The opt-out condition was identical except that the default was to be a donor. A neutral condition required a choice with no default. Changing the answer took one mouse click, which limits an effort explanation. Revealed donation rates were about twice as high when opting out as when opting in, and the opt-out condition did not differ significantly from the neutral condition, so the opt-in condition was the outlier. The article reports 79 percent stated donation in the neutral condition.

Across European countries, effective consent rates (people who had opted in, or had not opted out) separated opt-in and opt-out countries by nearly 60 percentage points with no overlap in the two distributions. The Netherlands mailed more than 12 million letters in a country of 15.8 million and the effective consent rate did not change. For actual donations per million, a regression controlling for infrastructure, education and religion found a significant default effect: 14.1 rising to 16.4 (a 16.3 percent increase), and a separate cross-sectional study cited there reported 10.8 rising to 16.9 (56.5 percent). The article lists three routes by which defaults act: the default as implied recommendation, the effort of changing it, and status quo plus loss aversion. It also gives other natural experiments: in auto insurance, 79 percent of New Jersey drivers kept the limited right to sue (the state's default) while 70 percent of Pennsylvania drivers kept the full right (theirs); in a retirement plan, raising the default contribution from 0 to 3 percent lifted the share of new employees saving anything from 31 to 86 percent, though it reduced the number choosing more than 3 percent.

### Replication and criticism
The consent-rate comparison measures agreement, not donations; the 2004 companion adds donation data separately. A Behavioral Science and Policy review (Steffel, Williams and Tannenbaum) found higher consent, donation and transplantation rates under presumed consent but cautions that it is one factor among many (secondary account via search). On breadth, Jachimowicz and colleagues' meta-analysis of default effects in Behavioural Public Policy covers 58 studies (pooled n = 73,675) and reports d = 0.68 (95 percent CI 0.53 to 0.83), with wide heterogeneity; defaults worked better when they signaled endorsement or reflected the status quo, and less well in environmental domains (via search; I did not read the full text). So the default effect is real and large on average but not constant, and the headline cross-country gaps overstate what a design change alone would do.

### Implications for LTF
Every pre-selected control is a decision LTF makes for the learner. Defaults should serve the learner: reminder emails, public profile visibility, and data sharing should default to the more private option, and any default that benefits LTF at the learner's expense is the preselection dark pattern (see gray2018dark and ftc2022bringing). Defaults that help are legitimate: a pre-checked "review this lesson tomorrow" reminder that is clearly visible and one click to remove, or fly mode defaulting to a readable high-contrast theme. A fly-mode setup screen should start from tested settings, since most beginners will keep what they are given. Quizzes should not default-select an answer. When a default carries a recommendation, say so in the interface; the endorsement route means a hidden default can look like advice.

Confirmed this session: Crossref query returned 10.1126/science.1091721 (Science 302(5649), 1338-1339); full text of the 2004 companion paper read from dangoldstein.com/papers/JohnsonGoldstein_Defaults_Transplantation2004.pdf (161 respondents, 60-point gap, Netherlands mailing, 14.1 to 16.4, 31 to 86 percent, Virginia 24 percent); meta-analysis and review figures from web search results (collaborate.princeton.edu, behavioralpolicy.org).

## simon1955behavioral
Simon, H. A. (1955). A behavioral model of rational choice. The Quarterly Journal of Economics, 69(1), 99-118.

### Core claims
Classical economics assumes an "economic man" who knows his environment, has a stable and well-organized preference system, and has the skill to compute which available action reaches the highest point on that scale. Simon proposes to replace that global rationality with a kind of rational behavior compatible with the information and the computational capacities that organisms, including humans, actually possess. The paper offers a mathematical model of choice for organisms of limited computational ability. It is a theoretical paper that rests on what Simon calls casual empiricism, not on experiments, and it is the origin of the idea of satisficing.

### Specifics
Simon lists the elements any rational choice model needs: the set of alternatives, a subset of alternatives the organism actually considers or perceives (so the considered set can be smaller than the objectively available one), possible outcomes, a pay-off function giving each outcome a value, and information linking alternatives to outcomes, possibly probabilistic. He restates the classical rules (max-min, probabilistic expected value, certainty) and then argues his first empirical proposition: there is a complete lack of evidence that, in actual human choice situations of any complexity, these computations can be or are in fact performed.

His simplifications are the heart of the paper. A "simple" pay-off function takes only two values (satisfactory or unsatisfactory) or three (win, draw, lose). His worked example is a person selling a house who regards $15,000 as an acceptable price: anything above is satisfactory and anything below is not. In psychological terms the boundary sits at the aspiration level; in economic terms it would sit at the price giving indifference between selling and not selling. He answers the objection that a rational seller would still prefer $25,000 to $16,000 by noting that offers arrive in sequence and must be accepted or rejected as they come, and that a simplified pay-off can approximate a smoother utility curve. The decision rule is then a search: find outcomes that are satisfactory, then find an action all of whose possible outcomes are in that set. Information gathering is costly, so the choice includes how far to refine knowledge, and he illustrates with chess, where the player looks for a good move, not the best one. A final section replaces a single scalar pay-off with a vector of values that have no common denominator (salary, climate, pleasantness of work, prestige), which means options may not be fully ordered.

A reprint introduction (Competition Policy International, 2010) notes that Simon does not use the phrase "bounded rationality" in this paper (it appears in 1957) but does define an aspiration level as the boundary between what is considered satisfactory and unsatisfactory.

### Replication and criticism
This is a model, not an experiment, so replication does not apply in the usual sense. The claim that people satisfice has been tested and extended by later work on search, aspiration adjustment and heuristics. Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier (2011) build on it with the "scissors" image from Simon's later writing: rational behavior is shaped by two blades, the structure of the task environment and the computational capabilities of the actor, so looking only at cognition cannot explain success or failure. The paper's weakness is that its psychology is introspective and illustrative; Simon says explicitly that the empirical task of finding which simplifications humans use, and when, was left for later. The heuristics-and-biases tradition (tversky1974judgment) kept Simon's rejection of unlimited rationality but read the departures as errors, while the ecological-rationality tradition read them as adaptive; the field still disputes which reading is better.

### Implications for LTF
Beginners are Simon's organism at its most limited: a small considered set, no way to compute the best option, and a high cost of gathering information. LTF should design for satisficing. Give a clear definition of "good enough" for each task (for example, a visible success band on a challenge) so learners can stop when it is reached, not chase perfection. Narrow the considered set: show one recommended next lesson with a short alternatives list, not a full catalog. Procedures and checklists in the app are legitimate simplifications of the kind he describes, since they replace open-ended optimization with a bounded search. When options do not share a common unit (time, difficulty, interest), do not collapse them into one score; show the dimensions. Aviation procedures themselves are outside this source.

## iyengar2000when
Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing? Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 79(6), 995-1006.

### Core claims
Prior research had suggested that more choice is better because it supports a sense of control and intrinsic motivation. The authors report three studies, in the field and the laboratory, that challenge the assumption that more options are always more motivating. People were more likely to buy gourmet jams or chocolates, or to take on an optional class essay, when offered a limited set of 6 options than an extensive set of 24 or 30. They also reported greater satisfaction with their selections, and wrote better essays, when the original option set had been limited. The authors describe extensive choice as initially attractive but ultimately demotivating. Evidence type: one field experiment, one classroom study, one laboratory experiment.

### Specifics
Study 1 (field). On two Saturdays, a tasting booth stood in an upscale grocery store in Menlo Park, California (the paper describes about 754 shoppers observed). The booth showed either 24 or 6 jams, rotated hourly. Of the 242 customers who encountered the extensive display, 60 percent (145) stopped; of the 260 who met the limited display, 40 percent (104) stopped. Customers who stopped did not sample significantly different numbers of flavors. Everyone who approached received a coupon for $1 off any jam of the brand, coded so purchases could be traced to the condition. Nearly 30 percent (31) of the limited-choice group later bought a jar, against 3 percent (4) of the extensive-choice group (chi-square with N = 249 reported as 32.34, p < .0001). So the large display attracted more attention and sold far less.

Study 2 (classroom). Students in a course were offered an optional two-page extra-credit essay and chose among either 6 or 30 topics. Of 70 students in the limited condition, 74 percent turned in the essay; of 123 in the extensive condition, 60 percent did (p < .05). Graders rated essays from the limited condition higher on content and form (overall mean 8.09 versus 7.69, p < .02).

Study 3 (laboratory). Participants chose from 6 or 30 Godiva chocolates, or were assigned one (no choice). The 30-chocolate group more often said they had been given too many options, took longer to decide (mean 24.36 seconds against 8.91), and rated the decision as more difficult and more frustrating. Afterward, participants could take a chocolate or a cash payment as compensation: 48 percent in the limited condition chose chocolate, against 12 percent in the extensive and 10 percent in the no-choice condition. Participants with 30 options reported feeling they had been given "too many", while those with 6 said the number was about right.

### Replication and criticism
This paper is the origin of the "jam study" that dominates popular accounts, and it has not replicated reliably. Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010) meta-analyzed 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036) and found a mean effect of virtually zero with large variance. A Psychology Today piece reports that the lead author's own attempt to replicate the jam finding for her dissertation in 2008 failed, and a Christian Science Monitor article describes another failed attempt (both secondary, via search). Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman (2015) argue the effect appears under specific conditions (see chernev2015choice). Notably, the original jam purchase figures come from a single store over two days and a coupon-linked purchase; the percentages are real for that study but do not generalize by themselves.

### Implications for LTF
Do not cite "24 jams" as proof that a menu must be short; it is a contested single field result. What the paper does support, carefully, is a testable design hypothesis: when beginners face many undifferentiated options, they may stop or defer. LTF can therefore keep lesson choices short by default (one recommended next step, a short "more" list) and measure whether the long list matters in LTF's own analytics, not assume it. Quiz answer sets should stay at the familiar 3 to 5 options. Fly mode should not present a menu of settings mid-flight. Challenge pickers can group options by purpose instead of listing 30 equal items.

Confirmed this session: Crossref works/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995 (title, authors, volume, issue, pages); full text read (PDF fetched from faculty.washington.edu, abstract, Study 1 counts 242, 260, 145, 104, 31, 4; Study 2 percentages 74 and 60; Study 3 chocolate choice 48, 12, 10 percent); replication context from web search (Psychology Today, Christian Science Monitor) and Scheibehenne et al. 2010 abstract.

## scheibehenne2010can
Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload. Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409-425.

### Core claims
The "choice overload" hypothesis holds that offering more options can reduce people's motivation to choose and their satisfaction with what they chose. Some laboratory and field studies found this, others found no effect, and some found that more options helped. The authors collected the studies and combined them. Their headline result is that the average effect of the number of options was close to zero, with considerable variance between studies. They found several possible preconditions for overload but no sufficient conditions, so no setting reliably produces it. This is a meta-analysis of experiments, so it is stronger evidence about the average than any single study, though it depends on which studies were found and how they were coded.

### Specifics
The data set was 63 conditions drawn from 50 published and unpublished experiments, with 5,036 participants in total (abstract at econpapers.repec.org and edoc.unibas.ch). Including unpublished work was a deliberate attempt to limit publication bias. The outcomes pooled across studies include reduced motivation to choose and reduced satisfaction with the chosen option. The abstract states that the mean effect was virtually zero and that effects varied widely: some studies show strong overload, others a benefit from more options. The authors reviewed the moderators proposed in individual studies and say these may still explain when overload happens, and they point to future research directions. A press summary of the work (phys.org) reports that more options helped especially when people had clear prior preferences; I treat that as a secondary reading of the paper's moderator discussion, not as a figure I verified in the paper.

An early version of the paper circulated as a PsycEXTRA conference dataset in 2008, "Can there ever be too many options? Re-assessing the effect of choice overload" (Crossref record).

### Replication and criticism
This is the paper that moved the field from "more choice is demotivating" to "when, if ever." It is also contested. In the same journal issue, Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman published a commentary titled "Choice Overload: Is There Anything to It?" (JCR 37(3), 426-428). They argue that choice overload has several antecedents, so searching for one main effect across all conditions is not very informative; the useful question is when overload occurs. They propose a theory-based meta-analysis testing a conceptual model of the psychological process instead. Their own 2015 meta-analysis (see chernev2015choice) of 99 observations then reported a significant overall effect once four moderators were included. The Scheibehenne group replied to the criticism (their reply is listed on the Indiana ABC West site, per search; I did not read it). Taken together: the best-supported statement is that there is no robust general effect, and that conditions matter. The jam study that started the literature (see iyengar2000when) did not hold up as a general rule.

### Implications for LTF
This source is a guard against a popular and lazy design rule. LTF's design record should not say "never show more than six options." It should say: options do not harm on average, but a beginner facing a difficult, unfamiliar choice with unclear preferences is the situation where problems have been reported. That describes LTF's onboarding (which aircraft system should I learn first, which lesson next) more than it describes a settings menu or a glossary list. So: test choices at the places where the learner knows least. When a long list is the right answer (a glossary, a list of airports, a full syllabus), show it, but give a way to filter. Use LTF's own completion and drop-off analytics before cutting content to fit a rule of thumb. Aviation facts are not drawn from this source.

## chernev2015choice
Chernev, A., Böckenholt, U., & Goodman, J. (2015). Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis. Journal of Consumer Psychology, 25(2), 333-358.

### Core claims
Earlier research had repeatedly shown that more options can hurt choice, but had not established when large assortments help and when they hurt. This paper proposes that the effect of assortment size on choice overload is moderated by four factors, and tests the proposal with a meta-analysis of 99 observations (N = 7,202) drawn from prior research. The authors also find that four common outcome measures work about equally well and can be used interchangeably, and that the overall effect of assortment size is significant once the moderators are included, which contradicts the near-zero average reported by the earlier meta-analysis (scheibehenne2010can). The paper is both a conceptual framework and a meta-analytic test of it.

### Specifics
The four moderators, with the direction the abstract reports (Kellogg research page): choice set complexity (greater complexity increases choice overload), decision task difficulty (higher difficulty increases overload), preference uncertainty (greater uncertainty about what you want increases overload), and decision goal (a more prominent effort-minimizing goal increases overload). A Kellogg Insight article on related work by Böckenholt and Chernev with a co-author illustrates the logic: a set of five retirement funds with 50 data points each is more likely to overwhelm than 50 funds described by one metric; decisions under time pressure are more likely to cause overload; people who already know what they value filter options more easily; and wanting a final choice that requires trade-offs is more likely to cause overload than browsing for later. That article is a trade summary of a related analysis, not of this paper, so I use it only to illustrate the factors.

The outcome measures that the paper treats as interchangeable are satisfaction or confidence, regret, choice deferral, and the likelihood of switching. The Kellogg Insight account of the related work adds that outright "paralysis" (not choosing) was erratic across studies while switching intentions and post-choice regret were steadier.

### Replication and criticism
The key dispute is between this paper and Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010). Chernev and colleagues argue that a null average hides real moderated effects, and say a theory-driven meta-analysis should replace a search for a single main effect (their 2010 commentary, abstract at econpapers.repec.org). Scheibehenne and colleagues replied (reply posted on the Indiana ABC West site; I did not read it). The disagreement is partly about method (what counts as a comparable study and how to code moderators) and partly about interpretation. Independent confirmation of the four-factor account in preregistered experiments would be the real test; I did not find a summary of such a study this session, so treat the four factors as a well-argued hypothesis with meta-analytic support, not as established law.

### Implications for LTF
This paper is the more usable of the two meta-analyses for design because it names conditions. Rate any LTF choice screen against the four factors. Complexity: are the options comparable on the same few attributes? Difficulty: is there time pressure (a timed quiz, a fly-mode moment)? Preference uncertainty: does the beginner know what they want (almost never at first)? Goal: is it a final decision or a browse? Where several factors stack up, such as a first-time learner choosing an initial training path, reduce the set, compare options on the same attributes, and offer a "not sure yet" route that does not force a final choice. Where a learner is browsing the syllabus, a full list is fine.

Confirmed this session: Crossref works/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002 (authors, J Consumer Psychology 25(2) 333-358; issued online 2014); abstract with 99 observations, N = 7,202, four moderators read on the Kellogg research page (kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual) and in web search; commentary abstract at econpapers.repec.org (JCR 37(3), 426-428).

## thaler2008nudge
Thaler, R. H., & Sunstein, C. R. (2008). Nudge: Improving decisions about health, wealth, and happiness. Yale University Press.

### Core claims
The book proposes libertarian paternalism: a middle path between command-and-control regulation and strict neutrality in which choice architects arrange the context of decisions so that people are steered toward better outcomes while keeping freedom to choose otherwise. The Open Library work description frames it as protecting people against damaging psychological traits (inertia, bounded rationality, undue influence) by exploiting those same habits to nudge them toward better choices. The authors define a nudge as any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing economic incentives (definition as quoted on Harvard's Nudge blog archive). The evidence base is a synthesis of behavioral economics and psychology, and the book is a policy argument, not a primary study.

### Specifics
The blog archive gives the authors' own illustrations: putting fruit at eye level counts as a nudge while banning junk food does not; home energy reports that compare your use with your neighbors'; asking citizens to plan when, where and how they will vote; and automatic enrollment in a retirement plan through payroll deduction. The common tools are defaults, social comparison, and making the preferred option salient. The Harvard page also states the condition that the intervention be easy and cheap to avoid. The book's intellectual debts are the heuristics-and-biases tradition (tversky1974judgment), prospect theory framing (tversky1981framing), and default and status quo findings (johnson2003defaults), each of which is in this lane. I could not confirm a page-by-page account of the book's chapters or its list of design principles this session, so none is reproduced here. A mnemonic sometimes attributed to the book was only found in a marketing blog and I did not verify it against the book, so I leave it out.

### Replication and criticism
The nudge idea has become one of the most criticized parts of applied behavioral science, on three fronts. First, effect sizes shrink. DellaVigna and Linos (Econometrica, 2022) analyzed all trials run by two large US nudge units (126 randomized trials, over 23 million people) and found an average take-up effect of 1.4 percentage points, against 8.7 points in a sample of academic papers; they attribute most of the gap, about 70 percent in the published version, to selective publication (abstract via search at econometricsociety.org and nber.org/papers/w27594). Second, Maier and colleagues (2022) argue that after adjusting for publication bias there is no remaining evidence for nudging overall, though they also note heterogeneity (see maier2022no). Third, some critics see choice architecture that works through biases as manipulation; Thaler himself later wrote about "sludge", the friction-adding counterpart (see thaler2018nudge). Defaults, the best-known nudge, have a large average effect in a 58-study meta-analysis (d = 0.68) but wide heterogeneity (see johnson2003defaults).

### Implications for LTF
The book gives LTF a vocabulary (choice architecture, default, nudge, and by extension sludge) and a test for acceptable influence: the nudge keeps every option open and is easy to avoid, and the learner would endorse it if they saw what it was doing. For LTF that means: a "next lesson" button as default is a nudge; a countdown or guilt message is not. Opt-outs must be as easy as opt-ins. Because the evidence for average nudge effects is weaker than the book suggests, LTF should not budget on a nudge-driven lift in engagement; run its own small tests and expect small effects. Where a nudge touches safety, such as pre-flight review prompts in fly mode, base it on the habit's value and not on a claimed percentage improvement. A nudge that cannot be explained to the learner in one sentence should not ship.

## kahneman2011thinking
Kahneman, D. (2011). Thinking, fast and slow. Farrar, Straus and Giroux.

### Core claims
The book organizes decades of judgment and decision research around two modes of thought. The publisher description (Open Library) puts it this way: System 1 is fast, intuitive and emotional; System 2 is slower, more deliberative and more logical. The book argues that many of our judgments, from overconfidence in corporate strategy to mistakes in predicting what will make us happy, can be understood by knowing how the two systems interact, and that people can learn where their intuitions can and cannot be trusted. It is a synthesis for general readers by one of the two founders of the heuristics-and-biases program, and a canonical statement of that program. It is not a primary study: it reports other people's experiments, along with the author's own.

### Specifics
The book is generally described as bringing together the topics in this lane (I did not read it): the representativeness, availability and anchoring heuristics from the 1974 paper (see tversky1974judgment), framing and loss aversion from the 1981 paper (see tversky1981framing), and the broader behavioral-economics ideas that Nudge popularized (see thaler2008nudge). The two-systems vocabulary is explicitly a metaphor in the author's own telling, which the Open Library blurb conveys by calling them "the two systems that drive the way we think." Kahneman and Frederick's earlier proposal, cited by Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier (2011), defines a heuristic as assessing a target attribute by another property that comes more readily to mind (attribute substitution) (I did not confirm how heavily the book uses it). Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier note that since the 1970s the opposition of logic and statistics (rational) against heuristics (error-prone intuition) was entrenched in psychology, from the heuristics-and-biases program to various two-system theories.

### Replication and criticism
This book is the best-known casualty of the replication crisis in this lane, which is why it belongs here. Its chapter on priming leaned on social priming studies. In 2012 Kahneman wrote an open letter to priming researchers saying he saw a "train-wreck looming" after an independent lab failed to replicate a well-known elderly-walking priming study (Chronicle of Higher Education account, via search). Schimmack, Heene and Kesavan (2017, a blog analysis and not a peer-reviewed paper) reported that 11 of the 12 priming articles cited in the chapter have a replicability index below 50 and concluded that readers should not treat the cited studies as solid evidence. A large multi-lab replication was reported in that coverage as finding no effect for one such priming claim. Kahneman acknowledged that behavioral priming effects cannot be as large and robust as the chapter suggested. A defense by Sherman and Rivers (2021) argues the critique is misdirected. Separately, other findings the book discusses have held up better: a Many Labs 2 summary reports that the framing effect was reproduced at about half its original strength (psychologicalscience.org; I did not confirm the anchoring result). The two-systems framework is also contested, with Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier arguing that heuristics are tools to be evaluated in context, not failures of a faster system.

### Implications for LTF
Use the book for its structure, and check each specific claim against the replication record before it informs a design rule. Do not build features that depend on social priming (backgrounds or words that subtly change behavior). The anchoring, framing and default findings are safer. Treat "System 1 versus System 2" as a communication aid for the team: a beginner at an unfamiliar task is slow and effortful, while an experienced pilot's recognition of a pattern is quick, so fly mode should avoid demanding slow reasoning from a learner who is already loaded. Quiz feedback should explain why an intuitive wrong answer is attractive, since the attraction is the instructional opportunity. Do not cite the book for numbers in lessons; cite the primary papers.

The book itself was not read.

## gigerenzer2011heuristic
Gigerenzer, G., & Gaissmaier, W. (2011). Heuristic decision making. Annual Review of Psychology, 62, 451-482.

### Core claims
The review argues that the science of heuristics has changed dramatically. A heuristic is defined, for purposes of the review, as a strategy that ignores part of the information with the goal of making decisions more quickly, frugally and/or accurately than more complex methods. The classical view, in the heuristics-and-biases program, was that saving effort costs accuracy. Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier argue that for many decisions the assumptions of rational models are not met, so how well a heuristic works is an empirical question, and that formal models of heuristics show that people and organizations often use simple rules adaptively and that ignoring information can sometimes give more accurate judgments than weighting and adding it all. Evidence type: a review of tests of formal models, with simulations and field comparisons.

### Specifics
The review contrasts Simon's question ("how do human beings reason when the conditions for rationality are not met?") with the question in the optimal-agent tradition, and borrows Savage's distinction between small worlds (all alternatives, consequences and probabilities known) and large worlds. Its key concepts: less-is-more effects, an inverse-U relation between accuracy and the amount of information, computation or time, so that past a point more is harmful; ecological rationality, which the review defines as the degree to which a heuristic is adapted to the structure of the environment; and Simon's scissors, in which rational behavior is shaped by two blades, the structure of task environments and the computational capabilities of the actor.

It gives formal definitions of named heuristics. Recognition heuristic: if one of two alternatives is recognized and the other is not, infer that the recognized alternative has the higher criterion value, which is ecologically rational when recognition correlates with the criterion (recognition validity α = C/(C+W), correct over correct plus wrong). Take-the-best has three building blocks: search cues in order of validity; stop at the first cue that discriminates; decide for the alternative with the positive value. Tallying counts equally weighted cues, and the review cites a study of 20 environments in which tallying had on average higher predictive accuracy than multiple regression, and reports that unit-weight models tended to succeed when the ratio of alternatives to cues was 10 or smaller, linear predictability was low and cues were redundant. The 1/N rule allocates resources equally across N options. It explains less-is-more partly through the bias-variance dilemma: a simple model can be better when samples are small and the world is noisy, because it trades a little bias for much less variance. It also discusses fast-and-frugal trees for classification, with medical examples.

### Replication and criticism
This is a position-taking review, so the evidence is strongest where cited formal comparisons exist and weaker where it generalizes. The authors note that studies show people do not automatically use the recognition heuristic when it applies, so the model of when people evaluate it is incomplete. The authors themselves stress that a heuristic is ecologically rational only when adapted to its environment, so the claim is conditional, not universal: the same rule can fail in another environment. The review opposes the heuristics-and-biases reading of Tversky and Kahneman (1974) and of two-system theories, which it says tied heuristics to error. Kahneman's own writing is cited in the review as the program's summary (see kahneman2011thinking), and the two camps have not converged.

### Implications for LTF
This source licenses simple rules for beginners and tells LTF how to choose them. Pilots and learners decide in a world where information is partial, so a short rule with one or two cues that has been tested may beat a long checklist of weights. For lessons: teach a few reliable cues in order of importance (take-the-best style), not a long list to be weighed, and give a clear stopping rule. For dashboards: a simple count ("lessons completed") can be a better indicator than a composite score. For quizzes: do not reward complex reasoning when a robust cue exists in the sim. For fly mode: design display and cue order so the learner can stop searching at the first discriminating cue, and limit the number of items competing for attention. Specific cue orders or procedures for the aircraft must come from the aircraft sources, not from this review.

## openscience2015estimating
Open Science Collaboration. (2015). Estimating the reproducibility of psychological science. Science, 349(6251), aac4716.

### Core claims
Reproducibility is a defining feature of science, but the extent to which it characterizes current research is unknown. The Open Science Collaboration conducted replications of 100 experimental and correlational studies published in three psychology journals, using high-powered designs and original materials where available. Nearly all of the original studies reported significant results; far fewer replications did, and replication effects were roughly half the size of the original effects. The paper is the central evidence base for caution about psychology findings in this lane: many of the effects used in design advice (defaults, priming, framing and choice overload) come from the same literature.

### Specifics
Headline numbers, from the abstract as reproduced on university repositories (discovery.dundee.ac.uk, research-portal.uu.nl): 97 percent of the original studies had statistically significant results; 36 percent of the replications did. Replication effect sizes were about half the magnitude of the originals (a substantial decline). 47 percent of original effect sizes fell within the 95 percent confidence interval of the replication effect size. 39 percent of effects were subjectively rated by the replication teams as having replicated the original result. Pooling the original and replication results, under the assumption of no bias in the originals, left 68 percent of effects significant. Replication success was better predicted by the strength of the original evidence than by characteristics of the original or replication teams. The project's OSF page says the work was coordinated by the Center for Open Science. The reply to critics (below) says the paper reports five measures of reproducibility, so no single number summarizes it.

### Replication and criticism
The project is itself a replication study, and it was debated. Gilbert, King, Pettigrew and Wilson published a technical comment in Science in 2016 arguing that the estimate of low reproducibility was overstated because of sampling error and differences in how replications were run; the Open Science Collaboration's reply (Science 351(6277):1037, 2016) says the commentators examined only one of five measures, that their reading rests on statistical misconceptions and selective interpretation, and that both optimistic and pessimistic conclusions are possible and neither is yet warranted (reply abstract via search; Data Colada also described both sides as having overlooked sampling error). Later large replication efforts, such as Many Labs 2, found a mixed picture, reporting that 15 of 28 findings (54 percent) were reproduced at the conventional threshold and that 75 percent of replications had smaller effects than the original (abstract via search). The robust lesson is not that psychology is false but that single-study, small-sample, surprising results (and the effect sizes they report) should be treated as provisional, and that published effect sizes are inflated.

### Implications for LTF
This paper sets the evidence standard for the Design Knowledge Base. Rules built on one famous experiment (the jam study, social priming, a single framing demo) should be marked as contested, and rules built on meta-analyses, field trials with large samples, or regulatory findings should be marked stronger. In practice: when a design rule cites a behavioral effect, record the effect type (single study, replicated, meta-analysis), expect the real effect to be smaller than the paper says, and prefer LTF's own A/B results with enough learners to detect small effects. Do not promise that a nudge or an incentive will raise completion by a fixed percentage. Design reviews should ask "what did the replication show?" before adopting a behavioral claim, and the same standard should apply to LTF's own claims to learners (for example, claims about learning gains). This is not a source for aviation facts.

## maier2022no
Maier, M., Bartoš, F., Stanley, T. D., Shanks, D. R., Harris, A. J. L., & Wagenmakers, E.-J. (2022). No evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 119(31), e2200300119.

### Core claims
This letter re-analyzes the large meta-analysis of nudging (choice architecture) interventions by Mertens and colleagues. Mertens et al. reported a moderate average effect but also moderate publication bias. Maier and colleagues argue that the bias is the real headline: once it is corrected, the evidence for an average nudge effect disappears. The authors' wording on the PMC page is that "no evidence for the effectiveness of nudges remains" after adjustment. They also report that most categories show heterogeneity, which they say means that some nudges might work even if the average effect is near zero. The evidence type is a statistical re-analysis of an existing meta-analysis, not new experiments.

### Specifics
Unadjusted, the Mertens et al. summary was a random-effects d = 0.43 (95 percent interval 0.38 to 0.48). The authors applied robust Bayesian meta-analysis combined with publication-bias adjustment (RoBMA-PSMA), which averages across selection models and other bias corrections so the severity of bias need not be assumed in advance. The adjusted overall estimate was d = 0.04 (interval 0.00 to 0.14, BF01 = 0.95) when ignoring clustering of estimates within studies, and d = 0.11 (0.00 to 0.24, BF01 = 0.31) using only the most precise estimate per study. The authors describe the Egger regression coefficient (b = 2.10) as indicating severe bias, and note that Mertens et al.'s own sensitivity analysis suggested the true effect could be as low as d = 0.08 if bias was severe.

By nudge category, the adjusted effects were: information 0.00 (unadjusted 0.25; BF01 about 34 and 11 across the two analyses), structure 0.12 (unadjusted 0.58; BF01 1.12 and 0.33, undecided), and assistance 0.01 (unadjusted 0.22). By domain: health 0.01, food 0.02, environment 0.01, finance 0.00, prosocial 0.00, other 0.08. The authors conclude that information and assistance interventions show evidence against an effect, structure interventions (the category that includes defaults and similar changes to the decision environment) are undecided, and that publication bias is strong across subdomains. All categories and domains except finance show heterogeneity.

### Replication and criticism
The authors state their own limitations. Fully three-level bias-corrected models were computationally intractable, so they used two workarounds, ignoring the three-level structure or using one estimate per study; per-subfield analyses ignore clustering and differ slightly from Mertens et al.; and the Bayes factor was described inconsistently in the paper. They also acknowledge that the conclusion depends on how bias is modeled. In a follow-up blog rejoinder, the authors say the overall result is undecided rather than evidence against nudging, and that the title was not ideal (Bayesian Spectacles, via search). I did not find or read any published reply by the original meta-analysis authors this session. A separate line of evidence supports the concern about inflation: DellaVigna and Linos (2022) found much smaller average effects in trials run by nudge units than in published academic papers, and attribute most of the gap to selective publication (see thaler2008nudge). The fair summary is that average nudge effects are probably much smaller than the published literature suggests, that defaults and other structure changes may still work, and that context determines the outcome.

### Implications for LTF
This is the main caution for any LTF plan that depends on nudging learners. Do not expect a pre-checked option, a streak message or a progress bar to deliver the lift quoted in blog posts. Budget design effort for what works through ability and clarity (simpler steps, better explanations) before motivation tricks, which matches the Fogg model's emphasis on making behavior easy (see fogg2009behavior). Where LTF uses a structural nudge, such as the default for a reminder, run a small test and be prepared for a null result. Information-only nudges (a message saying most learners finish) have the weakest evidence here, so avoid relying on them. LTF should also apply the same publication-bias suspicion to its own internal A/B tests: pre-register the metric and report null results.

## thaler2018nudge
Thaler, R. H. (2018). Nudge, not sludge. Science, 361(6401), 431.

### Core claims
This one-page essay in Science, by the co-author of Nudge, makes a simple argument: the same understanding of human behavior that supports helpful nudges also supports their opposite, which Thaler calls sludge. The abstract describes sludge as activity that just mucks things up and makes wise decision making and prosocial activity more difficult. He frames it as the question of what to do about activities that are essentially nudging for evil. The essay is an opinion piece by an established author, so it is craft and policy authority, not an experiment. I could only read the abstract and secondary accounts; the full text was not accessible, so everything below beyond the abstract is flagged as secondary.

### Specifics
Secondary accounts (a Leicester staff blog, behavioraleconomics.com, a Cardiff working paper on sludge and transaction costs, and Wikipedia's entry on the topic) agree on the shape of the concept: sludge is excessive or unjustified friction that makes it harder to do something that is in a person's interest, whether it is a design, administrative or policy obstacle. Examples cited in those accounts are product rebates that require difficult procedures, subscription cancellations that can only be done by phone, complicated or long government student aid application forms, and a company that promises refunds but makes claiming them procedurally complex enough that few follow through. The key design point is the symmetry: friction can be added or removed on purpose, and removing friction from good behavior or adding it to behavior that benefits the designer is a choice.

The FTC's 2022 staff report adopts the term and quotes a workshop panelist's definition of sludge as "a high friction experience that, by its nature, causes people to become fatigued and give up", applying it to the ABCMouse cancellation path, which required six to nine screens (see ftc2022bringing). That report ties the concept to legal tests of unfair practices and to the ROSCA requirement for a simple cancellation mechanism.

### Replication and criticism
There is nothing to replicate, since this is an essay, but the concept has been critiqued. Secondary sources report that early definitions treat sludge as "nudging for evil" or as excessive friction, and that whether sludge always reduces welfare is still debated; a commentator from healthcare argues that some friction is beneficial because it can slow people down and make them think carefully. So the line between harmful sludge and protective friction is a judgment about whose interest the friction serves, not a property of the friction itself. The concept is also vulnerable to the same evidence worry as nudging: the evidence for the size of sludge effects is mostly from field cases and policy examples, not from large trials in the sources I found. Dark patterns research (see luguri2021shining) provides experimental evidence that obstruction and hidden information change decisions, which supports the concern in practice.

### Implications for LTF
Sludge is the quality check for LTF's own flows. Audit every action that is in the learner's interest and every action that is not, and compare the effort required. Starting a lesson, resuming a lesson, exporting progress, deleting an account and cancelling any paid plan or email subscription should each take no more steps than signing up. Do not insert "are you sure?" interruptions or offers into cancellation and deletion. Remove friction that does not protect the learner: forms asking for information LTF does not need, repeated sign-in prompts, hard-to-find settings. Friction is allowed where it protects the learner, such as a confirmation before wiping progress, as long as the learner can proceed and the wording is neutral. In fly mode, any step that slows the learner while they are flying the sim is sludge, since attention is already scarce. Record each deliberate friction in the design log with its reason.

The essay's full text was not read.

## gray2018dark
Gray, C. M., Kou, Y., Battles, B., Hoggatt, J., & Toombs, A. L. (2018). The dark (patterns) side of UX design. In Proceedings of the 2018 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (pp. 1-14). ACM.

### Core claims
Harry Brignull coined "dark patterns" in 2010 for user interface designs that trick people into doing things they did not intend. Gray and colleagues argue that practitioner talk about dark patterns had not been connected to academic work on ethics and values in design. They build a corpus of examples that practitioners shared, analyze it, and propose five strategy categories (nagging, obstruction, sneaking, interface interference, forced action) organized by the designer's motivation, not by the page context. They also argue that the ethical issues go beyond individual tricks to the responsibility of practitioners. The evidence is a qualitative document analysis, so it supports a taxonomy and illustrative cases, not prevalence or effect size.

### Specifics
The corpus held 118 artifacts: 45 from social media, 40 from practitioner blogs, 19 from news outlets, 10 from the researchers' own interactions with products, and 4 from darkpatterns.org (Brignull's site). Two researchers collected over a two-month period. Searches began with "dark patterns" and the hashtag, then broadened to terms such as "unethical" and "manipulative" to find unlabeled cases. The researchers first coded against Brignull's twelve types (including bait and switch, disguised ads, forced continuity, friend spam, hidden costs, misdirection, price comparison prevention, privacy Zuckering, roach motel, sneak into basket, trick questions) and found the types mixed strategies and context-specific content, so they re-coded openly and obtained five strategies.

The definitions: nagging is a minor redirection of expected functionality that may persist across interactions, such as repeated interruptions (Instagram's notification prompt with only "Not Now" and "OK"). Obstruction impedes a task flow, making an interaction harder than it needs to be to dissuade an action; subtypes include roach motel (easy to enter, hard to leave), price comparison prevention, and intermediate currency, in which real money is exchanged for a virtual currency that disconnects people from the dollar value. Sneaking hides, disguises or delays information relevant to the user so that they take an action they might object to with full knowledge (hidden costs, forced continuity, sneak into basket, bait and switch). Interface interference manipulates the interface so that certain actions are privileged over others, with three subtypes: hidden information (fine print, discolored text), preselection (an option chosen by default before the user acts), and aesthetic manipulation (visual emphasis, false hierarchy, disguised ads). Forced action requires a specific action to continue, for example Windows 10's update forcing before shutdown, or Salesforce requiring consent to share data in order to unsubscribe.

In the discussion, the authors relate dark patterns to Norman's gulfs of execution and evaluation: interface interference and obstruction leave the user "flying blind" about what can be done, while nagging and forced action foreground only the shareholder's preferred actions.

### Replication and criticism
This is an exploratory taxonomy based on a convenience corpus of artifacts shared by practitioners, so it is biased toward cases salient enough to be posted and says nothing about how common each pattern is; the scale study by Mathur et al. addresses that (see mathur2019dark). Categories overlap by design (the authors say the roach motel can invoke hidden information). Mathur et al. adopt the term "sneaking" from this paper but add urgency, social proof and scarcity categories. The strategy-by-motivation framing has been adopted in regulatory language, as in the FTC report's categories (see ftc2022bringing), though that report organizes by consumer harm.

### Implications for LTF
Use these five strategies as a review checklist for every LTF flow. Nagging: does any prompt repeat after the learner declines (notification asks, upgrade banners)? Obstruction: is cancelling, deleting or exporting harder than signing up? Sneaking: are costs, data uses or consents revealed after the learner has committed? Interface interference: is the learner's preferred option visually weaker, hidden, or preselected? Forced action: does anything require a consent or an account to continue that is not needed for the lesson? Several of these are especially dangerous in a fly-mode context, where a nagging prompt or forced action interrupts a learner mid-flight. Add the five categories to the design review template, and require a stated reason for any design that falls under one.

Confirmed this session: Crossref query returned 10.1145/3173574.3174108 (CHI 2018, pages 1-14; five authors); full text read from par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10057203 (corpus of 118 artifacts and source counts, five strategy definitions and subtypes, Brignull types table, Norman discussion).

## mathur2019dark
Mathur, A., Acar, G., Friedman, M. J., Lucherini, E., Mayer, J., Chetty, M., & Narayanan, A. (2019). Dark patterns at scale: Findings from a crawl of 11K shopping websites. Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction, 3(CSCW), 1-32.

### Core claims
This is the first large-scale measurement of dark patterns on the web. The authors crawled shopping sites, found 1,818 dark pattern instances of 15 types in 7 broader categories, and show that these occur on about 11.1 percent of the roughly 11,000 sites studied. They develop a taxonomy of dark pattern characteristics (asymmetric, covert, deceptive, hides information, restrictive) and link each type to the cognitive biases it exploits. They also find 183 websites using deceptive practices and 22 third-party entities that sell dark patterns as a turnkey service. The evidence is automated crawling with manual classification, so it is strong on prevalence in the sample and silent on effect on consumers.

### Specifics
Method: the team built a crawler that collected 53,180 product pages from 11,286 shopping websites, drawn from a list of about 19,000 English-language sites, then used a "checkout crawler" built on OpenWPM to simulate adding a product to the cart, viewing the cart and checking out. They extracted text segments, grouped them with hierarchical clustering (HDBSCAN), and manually inspected the clusters for dark patterns. The authors state that their numbers are a lower bound because the automated approach only examines text-based interface elements on a sample of product pages. More popular sites, by Alexa rank, were more likely to feature dark patterns.

The taxonomy of characteristics: asymmetric (unequal weight or burden on the choices, such as a prominent accept button and a hidden decline), covert (the effect is hidden from the user, as with a decoy), deceptive (induces false beliefs), hides information (obscures or delays necessary information), and restrictive (restricts the set of choices, such as sign-up only through social media). The biases it links to are anchoring, bandwagon, default, framing, scarcity and sunk cost. The seven categories are sneaking, urgency, misdirection, social proof, scarcity, obstruction and forced action. Sneaking contains sneak into basket, hidden costs and hidden subscription (Brignull's forced continuity); urgency contains countdown timers and limited-time messages; misdirection contains confirmshaming, trick questions, visual interference and pressured selling; social proof contains activity notifications and testimonials; obstruction is represented by "hard to cancel" and forced action by "forced enrollment". Most patterns found were covert, deceptive and information hiding.

Deception: the authors examined countdown timers by recording repeated crawler visits and counting a timer as deceptive if it reset after timeout with the offer still valid, or if the offer remained valid after the timer expired. They found 157 deceptive countdown timers on 140 websites, one example offering a 60-minute timer on a deal that persisted afterward. In total they found 234 deceptive instances across 183 websites. They also identify 22 third-party entities, two of which openly advertised practices that enable deceptive messages.

### Replication and criticism
This paper is a measurement, not an experiment, and it is limited to English-language shopping sites, to text-based patterns and to the pages the crawler reached, so prevalence outside this sample is unknown. Classification depends on the researchers' manual labeling. The authors released their crawler and data set on GitHub, which allows others to repeat the crawl, though I did not see a full independent replication this session.  It does not measure how much each pattern changes behavior; experimental work such as Luguri and Strahilevitz (see luguri2021shining) does, and finds that some highly visible patterns (urgency messages) do not work as marketers expect, so prevalence is not evidence of effectiveness.

### Implications for LTF
The prevalence data show which patterns learners will have met on other sites and will expect or distrust: countdown timers, low-stock messages, confirmshaming ("No thanks, I don't want to learn"), pre-checked add-ons and drip pricing. LTF should avoid all of them. Specific rules: no timers on offers unless the deadline is real and stated, no manufactured scarcity, no confirmshaming copy on decline buttons, no social proof that is not a real count. Review third-party widgets for built-in dark patterns, since the study found vendors selling them. The five characteristics are a useful audit tool: for any new control, ask whether it is asymmetric, covert, deceptive, information-hiding or restrictive, and require a written reason for any yes.

## luguri2021shining
Luguri, J., & Strahilevitz, L. J. (2021). Shining a light on dark patterns. Journal of Legal Analysis, 13(1), 43-109.

### Core claims
Dark patterns are user interfaces whose designers knowingly confuse users, obstruct their true preferences or push them into actions. Legal scholars Luguri and Strahilevitz ran two large online experiments with census-weighted samples of American adults to measure how much dark patterns change consumer decisions, which patterns work best, and whether they cause a backlash. They find that mild dark patterns more than doubled the share who signed up for a dubious service, and that aggressive ones nearly quadrupled it, but aggressive patterns also provoked a strong consumer backlash. They argue that many dark patterns may violate laws against unfair and deceptive practices, and that dark patterns can make contracts voidable. The evidence is randomized experiments, which are stronger evidence for effect than the observational taxonomies (see gray2018dark and mathur2019dark).

### Specifics
Study 1 (as read on the OUP page): participants were offered a dubious paid identity theft protection service. A control condition got a neutral interface; a mild condition used a few dark patterns; an aggressive condition used many. Acceptance rates were 11.3 percent in the control, 25.8 percent in the mild group and 41.9 percent in the aggressive group. Stakes ($2.99 versus $8.99 a month) had no significant effect on acceptance. The aggressive condition produced a strong consumer backlash, while the mild condition did not. Less educated respondents were significantly more susceptible to mild dark patterns than the well educated.

Study 2 isolated individual patterns. The most effective were hidden information, obstruction, and trick questions. Hidden information raised acceptance from 14.8 percent in the control to 30.1 percent; a trick question raised it from 19.2 to 33.4 percent. Loaded language and bandwagon (social proof) messages were moderately effective. Scarcity and "act now" countdown messages had no significant effect on acceptance. Price had little effect, so raising the cost did not reduce uptake. The authors conclude that the structure of the decision, not the price, drove purchasing.

On law, the authors argue that many dark patterns may violate federal and state rules on unfair and deceptive trade practices, that contracts formed through dark patterns could be voidable, and propose dark pattern audits in FTC consent decrees and using firms' own A/B tests to identify which patterns are manipulative enough to be unlawful.

### Replication and criticism
The study is a pair of large online experiments with census-weighted samples, so it is among the strongest sources in this lane for effect sizes of manipulative interfaces. It has limits. The scenario is a simulated service offering within a survey platform, with an artificial sign-up decision, so real-world effect sizes might differ in either direction. Study 1 bundles several patterns per condition; Study 2 isolates single ones. I did not see an independent replication of this exact design this session. The FTC report cites a closely related finding, that dark patterns doubled the percentage who signed up for a dubious identity theft protection service and that the effect grew with multiple patterns (see ftc2022bringing). That is the same research as reported in the staff report, not independent confirmation.

### Implications for LTF
The study defines what manipulative design costs the people subjected to it: it works, it works better on less educated users (and LTF's beginners may include such users), and it is likely to be unlawful. Treat this as a reason to rule out hidden information and obstruction, and trick-question wording, in any consent, subscription or data screen, even though the patterns might lift a metric. The backlash result shows that if LTF ever uses "mild" manipulation, the cost can hide in lost trust and not appear in conversion numbers. Because scarcity and countdown messages did not work in this study, there is no business case for them either. When LTF A/B tests sign-up or payment screens, a large lift from a variant that hides terms is a warning, not a win.

## ftc2022bringing
Federal Trade Commission. (2022). Bringing dark patterns to light: Staff report. Bureau of Consumer Protection.

### Core claims
This September 2022 staff report summarizes an April 29, 2021 FTC workshop and the academic literature on digital dark patterns and organizes common patterns under four consumer-protection concerns, with enforcement examples and recommendations for firms. It credits Harry Brignull with coining the term in 2010 and describes dark patterns as design practices that trick or manipulate users into choices they would not otherwise have made and that may cause harm. It says dark patterns often take advantage of cognitive biases, are stronger in combination, and can be tested at scale online. The report is a regulator's staff view, not a rule or a court holding, but it shows how the main US consumer agency reads the law (FTC Act, ROSCA and others). It says the FTC will continue to take action where practices violate those laws.

### Specifics
Four categories: (I) design elements that induce false beliefs, such as disguised ads, fake countdown timers, claims that an item is nearly sold out when stock is ample, false claims that others are viewing or buying the item, and rankings that look neutral but are paid for (the LendEDU case); (II) design elements that hide or delay disclosure of material information, such as terms buried in long terms of service, fees behind tooltips or below the fold (in the LendingClub complaint, mobile users had to scroll about four times to see the fee and amount), and drip pricing, where mandatory fees appear late. A study quoted in the report found users not shown ticket fees upfront spent about 20 percent more and were 14 percent more likely to complete the purchase; (III) design elements that lead to unauthorized charges, including children's in-app purchases (Amazon made more than $70 million in refunds available; charges ranged from $0.99 to $99.99), free trials that roll into subscriptions, and cancellation paths such as ABCMouse's six to nine screens, which the report calls a prime example of what a panelist termed sludge; (IV) design elements that obscure or subvert privacy choices, listed as six tactics to reject collection, repeated prompts, confusing toggles, buried choices, a highlighted accept button against a greyed-out alternative, and defaults that maximize data collection.

Legal frame and advice: ROSCA (2010) prohibits charging for online negative option sales unless the seller clearly discloses material terms before taking billing information, gets express informed consent, and provides a simple way to stop recurring charges. The report says cancellation should be at least as easy as signing up, in the same medium, without delaying new offers. Consent needs an affirmative, unambiguous act and cannot be buried in general terms. Companies are responsible for the net impression of a design, not just individual words. It recommends looking beyond sales and click-through rates to whether consumers understand material terms, remedying designs that induce false beliefs, minimizing data collection, and not highlighting one option over another in privacy choices. It warns that poor color contrast (a white disclosure on yellow) and peripheral placement can be a violation, and that mobile scrolling makes hiding information easier. Appendix A lists dark pattern types with examples (false activity messages, false low stock, baseless countdown timers, price comparison prevention, roadblocks to cancellation and others).

### Replication and criticism
The report is a synthesis, not an experiment, and it describes allegations in complaints as allegations. The effect figures it cites come from other studies, including the Luguri and Strahilevitz work (see luguri2021shining), which are experimental but not independently replicated that I found. The report ties each pattern to existing statutes and does not create new rules. I did not check later FTC developments, so this note covers the report as published.

### Implications for LTF
Treat this report as LTF's compliance baseline. If LTF ever has a paid plan or free trial: disclose price, billing frequency and trial end before taking payment details; require an affirmative click for consent; let learners cancel from the same place and in the same number of steps as signing up; and include all mandatory fees in the displayed price. Do not use fake timers or stock messages, endorsements that are not genuine, or an "accept" button that visually dominates "decline". Privacy: choose private defaults, make consent a separate, specific choice, and collect only the data the lessons need. Apply the report's contrast advice to all disclosure text, in both light and dark themes. In fly mode, avoid any upsell. This is a design reading, not legal advice.

## fogg2009behavior
Fogg, B. J. (2009). A behavior model for persuasive design. In Proceedings of the 4th International Conference on Persuasive Technology (pp. 1-7). ACM.

### Core claims
Fogg presents a model for understanding human behavior in which a target behavior happens only when three factors converge at the same moment: motivation, ability and a trigger (renamed "prompt" by Fogg in late 2017). If a behavior does not occur, at least one of the three is missing. According to the ACM abstract and Fogg's current site, the model is intended both for analyzing why behavior change efforts fail and for designing persuasive technology, and gives teams a shared vocabulary. This is a conceptual model from the founder of Stanford's Behavior Design Lab, which is craft and theory authority, not an experimental result.

### Specifics
From the model's own site (behaviormodel.org, which post-dates the 2009 paper): motivation has three core motivators, each with two sides: sensation (pleasure or pain), anticipation (hope or fear) and belonging (acceptance or rejection). Fogg also describes a "Motivation Wave": when motivation is high, people will do hard things, and when it subsides they will do only easy things. Ability is described as simplicity, a function of the person's scarcest resource at that moment; time and money are given as examples, and "your weakest link determines what makes a behavior hard to do." Designers can raise ability by training people (called the hard path), by giving a tool or resource, or by scaling the behavior back so it is easier, which is the approach Fogg advocates in Tiny Habits. Motivation and ability are in a compensatory (trade-off) relationship, which is the curved line in the model's diagram: high motivation can make up for low ability, and vice versa. Prompts come in three types named facilitator, signal and spark; the site's Facebook re-engagement email is called mostly a facilitator because of its prominent green sign-in button. I did not confirm the definitions of signal and spark this session, so I do not give them. Fogg says designers should match the prompt type to the user's context, which combines motivation and ability.

The 2009 paper's keywords as listed: persuasion, behavior change, simplicity, motivation, persuasive technology, captology, triggers, persuasive design. The site says the model has been cited in more than 1,900 academic publications.

### Replication and criticism
The strongest honest statement is that this is a heuristic design model, widely used and cited but not a tested causal theory. I found no experiment this session that tests the full model's predictions, such as the compensatory curve or the three prompt types, and its constructs are broad enough that failure can usually be explained afterward by a missing element. Its ethical risks are real: the same model that helps a designer remove friction from a good habit can be used to prompt a person at the moment their motivation is high and ability is low, which is how many dark patterns operate (see mathur2019dark, luguri2021shining). The meta-analytic evidence on motivation-driven nudges (see maier2022no) suggests that effort spent on ability and simplicity is likely to pay better than effort on motivational tricks, and the model itself puts ability and prompts on equal footing with motivation.

### Implications for LTF
Use the model as a diagnostic checklist when a learner stalls. Is motivation low (the learner does not see why), is ability low (the step is too hard or too long), or is there no prompt (they do not know what to do next)? For beginners, ability is a likely first suspect: split a lesson into smaller steps, give a tool such as a checklist or a widget, and make the next action obvious (a single "continue" prompt). Prompts should be honest and respect the learner's attention: a reminder after a missed session is a signal; a guilt message is a dark pattern. Because a prompt arriving when ability is low does harm, never prompt in fly mode. Where LTF wants a habit (regular practice), start with a small, easy behavior tied to an existing routine, and measure whether it works instead of assuming the model predicts the lift.
