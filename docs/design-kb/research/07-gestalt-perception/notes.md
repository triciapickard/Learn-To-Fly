# Lane 07: Gestalt and perceptual organization (research notes)

## wagemans2012century
Wagemans, J., Elder, J. H., Kubovy, M., Palmer, S. E., Peterson, M. A., Singh, M., & von der Heydt, R. (2012). A century of Gestalt psychology in visual perception: I. Perceptual grouping and figure-ground organization. Psychological Bulletin, 138(6), 1172-1217.

### Core claims
This is the standard modern review of perceptual grouping and figure-ground organization, written for the centenary of Wertheimer's 1912 paper on apparent (phi) motion. The authors argue that the old Berlin-school claims were right about the phenomena but weak on method: the original demonstrations relied on subjective reports, simple or confounded stimuli, laws with little precision, and a growing list of laws with no mechanism behind them. A century of work has since put numbers on many of the effects, found new principles, and shown that organization happens at several levels of processing rather than in one early step. Their verdict is that reports of Gestalt psychology's death were greatly exaggerated, but that modern vision science is not a Gestalt school; it has adopted Gestalt problems and kept mainstream methods.

### Specifics
Classic grouping principles listed with figures in the review: proximity, similarity (colour, size, orientation), common fate, symmetry, parallelism, good continuation and closure. Newer ones: generalized common fate (grouping by simultaneous luminance change, Sekuler and Bennett 2001), synchrony, common region (Palmer 1992), element connectedness and uniform connectedness (Palmer and Rock 1994).

Quantitative proximity work: Kubovy and Wagemans (1995) and Kubovy, Holcombe and Wagemans (1998) used dot lattices and found that when the ratio of the two competing inter-dot distances exceeds about 1.5, grouping along the longer axis is almost never seen. Grouping log-odds fall on a single attraction function of relative distance, which the authors call the Pure Distance law. Oyama (1961) had earlier fitted a power law with an exponent of about 2.89. Kubovy and van den Berg (2008) showed with 300 ms presentations that proximity and similarity combine additively. The review also reports Rush (1937), whose attempt to express similarity in units of proximity the authors describe as methodologically flawed.

Contour integration: Field, Hayes and Hess (1993) embedded contour elements in random distractors and proposed the association field. Elder and Goldberg (2002) measured natural-image statistics and found an ecological proximity exponent of about 2.92, close to Oyama's perceptual value. 
Figure-ground: classic configural cues (convexity, symmetry, small area, surroundedness) are joined by newer image-based cues: lower region, top-bottom polarity (wider at the bottom), extremal edges (Palmer and Ghose 2008), edge-region grouping, articulating motion, advancing region and entropy contrast. Effect sizes are smaller than the textbook demonstrations suggest: Peterson and Salvagio (2008) found convex regions seen as figure on 57 percent of two-region trials at 100 ms, rising to 89 percent with eight regions; the review concludes the traditional demonstrations overstated convexity and symmetry. Past experience (familiar upright shapes are more often figure) and attention both bias assignment. 

### Replication and criticism
This is a review of experiments, psychophysics and neurophysiology, so its strength varies by claim. The dot-lattice work is parametric and replicated within the Kubovy group and by others. The synchrony literature is contested: Farid and Adelson (2001) argued some effects may be stimulus artifacts. The authors themselves flag the lack of computational process models and weak integration with single-cell and "what versus where" accounts. Uniform connectedness as the entry-level step was disputed by Peterson (1994) and Kimchi (2000). The review is therefore best treated as a map: well-measured for proximity, similarity and good continuation, thinner for the long tail of named principles.

### Implications for LTF
First, treat the classic laws as strong defaults, not guarantees. Grouping by proximity and common region will usually work in LTF layouts, but the effect sizes in controlled studies are modest and can be overridden, so any grouping that carries safety-relevant meaning (a checklist item and its action, a speed and its unit) should be backed by a second cue such as a shared card or label. Second, the 1.5 distance-ratio finding is a useful reminder that a gap only separates groups when it is clearly larger than the gap inside the group; spacing tokens for widgets and fly-mode panels should keep inner gaps and outer gaps at a visibly different scale rather than a near-equal one. Third, because organization happens at several levels, quick-glance fly mode should be tested with brief exposures (a few hundred milliseconds) on a second monitor, not only with calm inspection.

## wagemans2012conceptual
Wagemans, J., Feldman, J., Gepshtein, S., Kimchi, R., Pomerantz, J. R., van der Helm, P. A., & van Leeuwen, C. (2012). A century of Gestalt psychology in visual perception: II. Conceptual and theoretical foundations. Psychological Bulletin, 138(6), 1218-1252.

### Core claims
The companion to the grouping review asks what Gestalt theory actually claims, and whether any modern framework delivers it. The authors treat holism (the whole is something other than the sum of its parts) as the core idea and examine it through emergent features, configural superiority, global precedence, and the long argument between simplicity (Prägnanz) and likelihood. Their conclusion is that Gestalt phenomena are real and reliable, but the original theoretical efforts did not stand the test of time; each modern framework (Garner-style dimensional analysis, structural information theory, Bayesian models, dynamical systems) explains part of the problem, and none has solved it entirely. They argue the frameworks must be connected, framed around the question of why things look as they do.

### Specifics
Holism: Wertheimer called it the fundamental formula; Koffka (1935) argued the whole is something other than the sum of its parts because part-whole relations are meaningful even where summation is not. Von Ehrenfels (1890) had treated Gestalt qualities as an add-on to sensations, which the Berlin school rejected more radically.

Emergent features and configural superiority: Pomerantz, Sager and Stoever (1977) showed that adding a redundant context element can make an odd item easier to find if the addition creates salient emergent features; in their arrow and triangle displays search was more than twice as fast, and the effect vanished when parts were slightly shifted. Chen (2005) found the strongest configural superiority effects involve topological features: holes, connectedness, inside versus outside.

Global precedence: Navon (1977) used hierarchical letters (a large letter built from small letters) and proposed that global structure is processed first. Kimchi (1994) found the global advantage only when the local task relied on line orientation, not closure; Han, Humphreys and Chen (1999) found a far weaker global advantage for closure than orientation. Kimchi (1998) found with primed matching that global form was primed at brief exposures while local elements were primed only at longer ones. They conclude wholes dominate parts in attention and perception, but "does not imply a specific processing order" and cite Reverse Hierarchy Theory (Hochstein and Ahissar 2002).

Simplicity versus likelihood: Necker cube and Ames window demonstrations can be explained by either principle. Mach and Attneave suggested the two may be two sides of the same coin; Palmer (2003) proposed simplicity is an internally accessible surrogate for likelihood. Structural information theory (van der Helm) says percepts correspond to the simplest descriptive codes, and limits visual regularities to repetition, mirror or broken symmetry, and alternation. Bayesian models (Feldman) treat good continuation as a likelihood distribution over deviations from collinearity and argue Prägnanz corresponds to Bayes' rule; the open problem is choosing priors. 
### Replication and criticism
The empirical items here (emergent features, hierarchical letters) are laboratory experiments with many replications, but they are task-dependent: the same display can show global dominance or local dominance depending on the judgment. The theoretical items are contested by design: simplicity versus likelihood remains unresolved, Bayesian accounts face the prior-selection problem, and structural information theory must still map its codes onto the visual system. The authors are candid that the field lacks a unified theory; a reader should take the paper as an honest statement of what is established (the phenomena) and what is not (a single mechanism).

### Implications for LTF
Emergent features are the most practical idea here. A good instrument widget makes the thing the learner needs (for example whether two needles are aligned, or whether a bar is inside a band) an emergent feature of the display, such as a straight line, a closed shape or a symmetry, so it can be read at a glance without reading numbers. The configural-superiority finding that topological properties (inside versus outside, connected versus broken) are the strongest suggests that "in the green band or not" is a better visual question than "is the number near 70". For lesson explanations, the simplicity principle gives a respectable justification for removing decorative marks that create accidental alignments or competing shapes. Finally, the paper's honesty about unresolved theory is a model for the KB: rules drawn from it should be phrased as defaults with named exceptions, not laws.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record 10.1037/a0029334 (title, authors, Psychological Bulletin 138(6), 1218-1252); text of the author manuscript read at pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3728284 (sections on holism, Garner, emergent features, global precedence, simplicity and likelihood, SIT, Bayesian approaches, conclusion).

## wertheimer1923untersuchungen
Wertheimer, M. (1923). Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II. Psychologische Forschung, 4(1), 301-350. English translation: Laws of organization in perceptual forms, in W. Ellis (Ed.), A source book of Gestalt psychology (1938), pp. 71-88, London: Routledge & Kegan Paul.

### Core claims
This is the founding statement of the grouping "laws". Wertheimer argues that we do not first see a mosaic of separate sensations and then combine them; what we see is already organized into units, and that organization follows describable factors in the stimulus pattern. He rejects the idea that grouping is explained by habit or learned association, and he rejects the assumption that fewer or simpler stimulus points automatically give simpler percepts. Simplicity, in his account, is a property of wholes, not of parts. He tends to say "factors" rather than "laws", a point made in a recent article by Stemberger (reference-global.com, gth-2024-0018).

### Specifics
The paper works by demonstration. Rows of dots are drawn so that the reader can see how they group, and the argument is built from what changes when one aspect of the row is changed. The English translation read this session (Classics in the History of Psychology, York University) lists the factors as follows.

Proximity: in a row of dots with alternating small and large gaps (the translation gives 3 mm and 12 mm), observers see pairs separated by the large gaps, not single dots; the same holds for taps in time. Similarity: elements of one colour, or soft versus loud beats, band together, and the degree of dissimilarity matters. Direction (later called good continuation): in lines, the factor of direction decides which segments continue one another, so a line is seen to carry on through a crossing. Common fate: groups that shift together are seen as units; he distinguishes shifts that agree with the existing grouping (smooth) from shifts that cut across it (confusing, and prone to force a regrouping). Closure: self-enclosed shapes tend to be seen as wholes. Good curve: in some figures a smooth continuous curve outweighs closure. Objective set (Einstellung): an ambiguous row takes a definite grouping depending on the sequence of rows seen before it. 
His method relies on observers reporting what they see, with the reader invited to check the demonstrations; the translation page itself does not use the word phenomenological.

### Replication and criticism
The paper is demonstration and observation, not a controlled experiment with statistics, and for that reason the later literature has treated it as a source of hypotheses. The central modern critique, set out in Wagemans et al. (2012), is that the original laws were subjective, imprecise and open-ended: each new demonstration seemed to add a law. Several factors have since been measured (proximity through dot-lattice studies, good continuation through contour-integration experiments and natural image statistics) and several turned out weaker or more conditional than the demonstrations implied (closure, convexity). Wertheimer's anti-habit argument has also been qualified: later figure-ground work found that familiar upright shapes are more often seen as figure, so experience does influence organization. Still, the main empirical claim, that grouping occurs spontaneously and follows regularities that can be manipulated, has held up for a century.

### Implications for LTF
The original paper is more useful than its textbook summaries because it shows that each factor is a matter of degree and that factors compete. For design, that translates into a working rule: a grouping cue is not binary, and when two cues disagree (for example a card border that groups items and a spacing that separates them) the result is ambiguous. LTF layouts should align cues, so that proximity, shared background and shared type style all say the same thing. The common-fate section, with its contrast between shifts that respect the grouping and shifts that cut across it, is directly relevant to animation in widgets and fly mode: elements that belong together should move together, and an animation that moves part of a group but not the rest invites misreading. Finally, the paper's own method (show, then ask what you see) is a good model for a lesson page: let the learner see the grouping before the principle is named.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record 10.1007/bf00410640 (Psychologische Forschung 4(1), 301-350, 1923); the full English translation page at psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms.htm (source note, factor list, examples); web search confirming Stemberger's remark on "factors" (reference-global.com, gth-2024-0018).

## palmer1992common
Palmer, S. E. (1992). Common region: A new principle of perceptual grouping. Cognitive Psychology, 24(3), 436-447.

### Core claims
Palmer proposes that elements lying inside the same bounded region of space tend to be seen as a group, and that this factor is a genuinely new grouping principle that cannot be reduced to proximity, closure or any known factor. Using demonstrations built like Wertheimer's original dot rows, he shows that common region strongly influences perceived grouping and can overcome other powerful factors, namely proximity and similarity. He further argues that the principle depends on perceived depth relations, so it must operate after at least some depth perception has occurred, and that it is dominated by the smallest background area and can follow a hierarchical embedding scheme.

### Specifics
The abstract (confirmed on PubMed 1516361) gives four concrete findings. First, the demonstrations are Wertheimer-style: rows or arrays of elements are arranged so that proximity or similarity favors one grouping, and then closed outlines are drawn so that a different grouping falls inside common regions; the outlines win. Second, grouping by common region depends on perceived depth: if the enclosing outline is not seen as a surface or background behind the elements, the effect weakens. This is the evidence Palmer uses to place common region later in processing than classical, image-based factors. Third, when regions are nested, the smallest enclosing background area dominates, so an element inside a small box inside a large box groups with the small box's contents. Fourth, the principle can follow a hierarchical embedding scheme, so groups can sit inside larger groups.

The principle has become the standard name for what interface designers call a card, a panel or a boxed group. Palmer also sorted grouping cues into intrinsic ones (properties of the elements themselves, such as proximity and similarity) and extrinsic ones (properties of the context that contains them, such as common region); that classification is attributed to Palmer (1992, 1999) in a later paper found by web search this session, not in the abstract itself.

Beck and Palmer (2002) and Palmer and Beck (2007, Perception and Psychophysics 69, 68-78) introduced the repetition discrimination task, in which people report whether a row contains repeated shapes and the grouping effect is the response-time difference between repeats inside a group and repeats across a group boundary (confirmed through a university research record and Wagemans et al. 2012). Palmer and Beck's third experiment is reported by a later paper as indirect evidence for common region and for element connectedness; I did not read that experiment directly this session.

### Replication and criticism
The core claim has been widely replicated and extended. Wagemans et al. (2012) list common region among the established newer principles and report that response-time measures give objective support. A web-search summary of a later study (Luna and colleagues, 2003) reports that common region dominated shape similarity and was faster than connectedness; another line of work reports that cooperating grouping principles produce stronger grouping than either alone. These secondary reports are consistent with Palmer's claims but I did not read the originals. A caution: Palmer's own argument that common region operates after depth perception means it is not a purely image-level effect, so it can be weakened when an outline is not read as a separating surface (for example a hairline with low contrast, or a gradient that blends into the page). 
### Implications for LTF
Common region is the single most useful Gestalt principle for LTF layout because it is cheap and strong. A card, a tinted panel or a bordered box around a checklist, a widget or a challenge brief tells the learner what belongs together even when spacing is loose. Because it can beat proximity and similarity, it can also create mistakes: an element placed inside the wrong panel will be read as part of it. So panel borders in lessons, quizzes and fly mode should follow meaning; an instrument widget and its caption belong in one region, and unrelated explanatory text should be outside it. The dependence on depth means panels need enough contrast against the page in both light and dark themes to read as surfaces: a faint border that disappears in dark mode removes the cue. In fly mode, where the learner glances between sim and app, a few clearly bounded regions (one per task) will work better than one large field of text with only spacing to separate it.

## palmer1994rethinking
Palmer, S., & Rock, I. (1994). Rethinking perceptual organization: The role of uniform connectedness. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 1(1), 29-55.

### Core claims
Palmer and Rock describe a principle they call uniform connectedness (UC): closed regions of homogeneous properties, such as lightness, chromatic colour or texture, tend to be perceived initially as single units. They argue that UC is not one more grouping principle but the process that produces the first, entry-level organization of the visual field into primitive units. Classical grouping principles (proximity, similarity, common fate and so on) then operate after UC, building superordinate units out of two or more of these basic units. Parsing processes also operate after UC, cutting basic units into subordinate parts. The paper is therefore a proposal about the order of processing as well as a new factor.

### Specifics
The abstract (confirmed on PubMed 24203413) makes these points. UC effects appear even when pitted against powerful grouping principles such as proximity and similarity. UC cannot be reduced to those principles because, the authors say, it is not a form of grouping at all: grouping combines already-segregated elements, whereas UC defines what the elements are. In the proposed framework the order is UC first (entry-level units), then classical grouping above it and parsing below it. UC in the retinal image is a necessary but not a sufficient condition for unit formation: connected regions that are perceived to lie in separate depth planes fail to be perceived as one unit. Taken together with other evidence that grouping principles depend on perceived (rather than retinal) relations, this suggests organization into UC objects is achieved in a late, postconstancy representation of environmental surfaces, after lightness and depth have been resolved.

Wagemans et al. (2012) summarize the evidence that followed. Using the repetition discrimination method, Palmer and Beck (2007) found faster responses for repeated elements that were connected. Humphreys and Riddoch (1993) reported a patient with Balint's syndrome who could tell a one-colour from a two-colour array of circles only when pairs were joined by lines, a neuropsychological result that supports connectedness as a basic unit-forming cue. Han, Humphreys and Chen, as summarized in a web-search result this session, found that discrimination of stimuli grouped by similarity was slower than for proximity and was speeded up when uniform connectedness was added.

### Replication and criticism
The claim that UC comes first has been contested. Wagemans et al. (2012) note that Peterson (1994) disputed its entry-level status and that Kimchi (2000), studying the microgenesis of grouping, found collinearity and closure at least as important early on. A commentary titled "The proper placement of uniform connectedness" (1994) argued that Koffka (1935) had already recognized the need for a unit-formation principle (found by web search; I did not read the commentary). A Korean-language study from 2009 (Park and Kim, reported in a web-search summary) found a UC effect in only one of nine brief-exposure experiments, which would argue against UC being a fast, early process. So the evidence is mixed on ordering, but the phenomenon (that connected, uniform regions behave as units even against proximity and similarity) is consistently supported. Treat the claim that "UC is a prerequisite of grouping" as a theoretical position, and the practical claim about connectedness as well supported.

### Implications for LTF
Connectedness is the strongest cue available to a flat interface, so LTF should use it deliberately. A line joining two items (a leader line from a callout to a part of a diagram, a connector in a flow of steps, a bar joining a label to its value) makes them one unit more reliably than closeness. This suggests: draw explicit connectors between a cockpit diagram and its labels rather than relying on nearness; make a timeline or step sequence a continuous track with markers on it; and avoid accidental connections, such as an underline or border that touches two unrelated items, since connectedness can override proximity and similarity. The depth point matters for layered designs: elements that appear to sit on different planes (a floating card over a panel) are not grouped by touching. In widgets such as a speed tape or a bar gauge, a solid filled region reads as a single object, which is why a uniformly coloured bar is understood at a glance, whereas a segmented bar needs an outline to be read as one object. Both themes should keep uniform fills genuinely uniform: gradients and noise textures in a fill weaken the unit.

## treisman1980feature
Treisman, A. M., & Gelade, G. (1980). A feature-integration theory of attention. Cognitive Psychology, 12(1), 97-136.

### Core claims
Treisman and Gelade propose that the visual system registers simple features (colour, orientation, size, and similar dimensions) early, automatically and in parallel across the visual field, while objects, meaning the correct combination of features at a location, are identified in a separate stage that needs focused attention. Attention acts as the glue that binds features at one location into a single object. When attention is overloaded or directed elsewhere, features from different objects can be wrongly combined, producing illusory conjunctions. The paper reports nine experiments and uses them to argue for a distinction between feature search, which is parallel and does not depend on how many other items are on display, and conjunction search, which is slower and grows with the number of distractors.

### Specifics
The paper's main paradigm is visual search: a participant reports as quickly as possible whether a target is present among a variable number of distractors, and the experimenter plots response time against display size. The slope of that line, in milliseconds per item, is the measure of search efficiency. In a feature search the target differs from all distractors in one simple property (for example a single coloured item among items of another colour), and response time is essentially flat as items are added. In a conjunction search the target is defined by a combination of two properties that each appear in the distractors (a red vertical bar among red horizontal and green vertical bars), and response time rises with the number of items. The usual interpretation in the paper is that features are detected in separate feature maps, which can be checked in parallel, whereas checking a conjunction requires attention to move to each location. 
Illusory conjunctions come from related work in the same programme. A summary of Treisman and Schmidt (1982) reports that observers shown four briefly flashed objects reported illusory conjunctions on 18 percent of trials; and a Bálint's syndrome patient with parietal damage reported illusory conjunctions on 23 percent of trials with simple stimuli (Wikipedia article on feature integration theory, which cites Friedman-Hill et al. 1995 and Robertson et al. 1997; these figures are secondary and I did not read the originals). 

### Replication and criticism
The feature versus conjunction distinction has been replicated many times, but the strict parallel versus serial reading has not survived. Wolfe (2021) states that conjunction searches were often more efficient than the theory predicted, so FIT was revised; Guided Search (from 1989) replaced the dichotomy with a continuum of guidance, with pop-out at one end and unguided search at the other, and Wolfe describes Guided Search 6.0 as an evolution, not a repudiation, of FIT (PMC8965574). Duncan and Humphreys (1989) argued from target-distractor similarity that search efficiency varies continuously, not categorically. Textbooks often still present the dichotomy, so widely repeated rules such as "conjunction search is always serial" are best treated as simplified. The illusory-conjunction results are a separate line of evidence and have held up as a demonstration that binding is not automatic. The evidence is experimental, with many replications, and is among the best-established in attention research.

### Implications for LTF
The practical lesson is that a single distinct feature can be found at a glance, but a target defined by a combination of features cannot be found efficiently. For fly mode this suggests that an alert or the next action should differ from its surroundings by one strong feature (for example colour alone, or position alone), not by a combination such as "the amber, italic item". In a checklist or dashboard, a status that needs spotting (a failed challenge criterion, an unmet checklist step) should pop out through one channel and should not share that channel with the many items that are fine: if five of ten rows are coloured, colour no longer identifies the one that matters. The illusory-conjunction work cautions against dense, glanceable displays that mix colours and shapes, since features of neighboring items can be mis-bound when attention is brief; the widgets that display several values together should separate them clearly and give each a stable location. For quizzes, the paradigm gives a useful way to test whether a design works: if a learner needs to scan item by item to find something that should pop, the design fails.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record 10.1016/0010-0285(80)90005-5 and PubMed 7351125 (title, authors, journal, pages; PubMed carries no abstract); web-search summaries of the experiments; Wikipedia article on feature integration theory (stages and illusory-conjunction figures); Wolfe (2021) PMC8965574 for the history and critique.

## duncan1989visual
Duncan, J., & Humphreys, G. W. (1989). Visual search and stimulus similarity. Psychological Review, 96(3), 433-458.

### Core claims
Duncan and Humphreys argue against the clean split between serial and parallel search and between feature and conjunction search. Their results show that search gets harder as the target becomes more similar to the non-targets, and also as the non-targets become less similar to one another (more varied non-targets make search harder). Put the other way, search is easiest when the target is very different from the distractors and the distractors are all alike. These two similarity relations produce a continuum of search efficiency, with easy pop-out at one end and slow item-by-item search at the other. The authors propose a model in which a parallel stage first groups and describes the visual input; the inputs then compete for access to awareness and action, and an input gains weight when it matches an internal description (a template) of what the observer currently needs.

### Specifics
The abstract on the Cambridge MRC Cognition and Brain Sciences Unit bibliography page (confirmed this session; the paper itself I did not read) lists the model's pieces. First, a parallel stage performs perceptual grouping and description, so organization of the display is a part of the search model, not a nuisance. Second, selection is a competition among inputs; the weight of each depends on how well it matches the description of the sought information. Third, grouping makes the weights of grouped inputs change together, so suppressing one of several similar non-targets suppresses its neighbours too, which the authors call spreading suppression. Fourth, the model explains why non-targets that resemble the target hurt search, and why it matters that non-targets can be grouped locally.

This is why conjunction searches tend to be hard, without needing a different mechanism from feature search.

The 1992 reply (Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 18, 578-588, "Beyond the search surface", abstract read on the CBU bibliography) answered Treisman's 1991 tests. Treisman found conjunction search harder than feature search even when similarity was controlled. Duncan and Humphreys separated their account into a summary of findings in terms of similarity and a theory of attention (input-template matching plus spreading suppression), and reported new data in which, with both factors controlled, feature and conjunction search were equally difficult.

### Replication and criticism
The similarity principle itself is robust and is built into later models. Wolfe's Guided Search (2021 version, PMC8965574) treats efficiency as a continuum determined by how well features guide attention, which agrees with the continuum idea though not with its mechanism. Search summaries in the literature say the two models, Guided Search and similarity theory, can each account for a wide range of data but differ in assumptions: similarity theory holds that segmentation and description occur in parallel and are not slowed by limited resources, and a later author (Proulx, via a web-search summary) argued both understate bottom-up saliency in conjunction search. Treisman's critique and the reply show the disagreement is about mechanism and about whether feature and conjunction search are different kinds of search; the practical regularities are shared. The evidence is from laboratory experiments with simple stimuli; its transfer to complex interfaces is plausible but untested here.

### Implications for LTF
This paper gives the most practical rule for any glanceable display: make the thing to be found unlike the others, and make the others like one another. In a checklist, a dashboard or an instrument cluster, a highlighted item stands out only if the rest are uniform. If each row has its own colour, icon and weight, a highlighted row has no similarity advantage, which is why heavy visual variety (many colours for categories, many icon styles) makes status colours harder to find. This suggests LTF dashboards and progress views should use a restrained, uniform base style for ordinary items and spend one visual channel (for example colour or a bold edge) on the exceptions. The target-non-target similarity rule also warns against near-duplicate states: two statuses that differ only slightly in hue are found slowly and confused. Because grouping is part of the model, putting non-targets into locally coherent groups (a tidy list, a regular grid) helps search; a ragged layout hurts. For fly mode, where a learner glances back and forth, keep the surrounding furniture quiet and uniform so the few dynamic items stand out.

## wolfe2021guided
Wolfe, J. M. (2021). Guided Search 6.0: An updated model of visual search. Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 28(4), 1060-1092.

### Core claims
Guided Search is the dominant theory of how attention is steered during visual search, and this is its sixth major version, about thirty years after the first. The core idea is that attention is not directed blindly: a spatial priority map combines several sources of guidance, and selective attention goes to the most active location about 20 times per second. In version 6.0 the sources are top-down and bottom-up feature guidance, history, reward, and scene syntax and semantics. Selection feeds a slower recognition process that takes more than 150 ms per item, and because selection is faster than recognition several items are recognized at once, so the model is a hybrid of serial and parallel processing. Wolfe presents it as an evolution, not a repudiation, of Feature Integration Theory.

### Specifics
Search slopes (response time per extra item) form a continuum. Pop-out is near zero ms per item. An unguided search for a T among L-shaped distractors is about 250 to 350 ms per item on target-absent trials, and about half that when the target is present. A search for a 2 among 5s is about 90 ms per item on target-absent trials, again about half when present. Wolfe notes that the classical serial-versus-parallel dichotomy persists in textbooks.

Guiding attributes: colour, motion and orientation guide search; intersection type and surface material do not. Guidance by colour and orientation uses coarse, categorical representations, and a difference of roughly 10 to 15 degrees is typically needed for orientation to guide. Shape is harder to classify (Huang 2020 proposed three shape dimensions: segmentability, compactness and spikiness). Scene guidance is the main addition: a rapid gist of layout and semantics available after a very brief exposure, and later anchor-object guidance such as a sink guiding the search for a toothbrush.

In a T versus L search, the attentional field had a radius of about 5 to 8 degrees, and target Ts within 2 degrees of fixation were fixated within the next three fixations about 70 percent of the time.

Quitting rule: search ends when an accumulating signal reaches an adaptive threshold that falls after a correct "absent" response and rises after a miss. Target prevalence matters: at low prevalence there are more misses and faster "absent" responses; at high prevalence, more false positives and longer "absent" responses; the criterion shifts, while sensitivity (d prime) changes little.

### Replication and criticism
The preattentive features and the prevalence effect are among the most replicated results in the field. Wolfe is candid about open problems: the number of items selected at once, how guidance changes with eccentricity and crowding (called vastly understudied), whether distractor inhibition contributes, and how quitting thresholds are set in real-world search with no trial feedback. His conclusion states that it is unclear whether laboratory search findings apply to real-world search. Competing accounts (Duncan and Humphreys' similarity theory, other saliency models) disagree on mechanism, and the 2021 simulation's zROC slope of 0.77 sits above the roughly 0.6 found in the literature, a mismatch the author reports. For designers, the guiding-feature list is the most reliable part; the scene-guidance claims are newer and less tested.

### Implications for LTF
First, use the continuum, not the myth, in design reviews: ask how efficiently an important item can be found, with pop-out as the goal for critical states. Second, the 10 to 15 degree orientation figure and the categorical nature of colour and orientation guidance mean subtle differences do not guide; two shades of green or two slightly tilted needles will not pop, so status colours should differ by category (for example distinct hues plus a second cue) and widget needles should differ visibly. Third, the functional visual field is relevant to fly mode: a learner looking at the sim monitor, then glancing at the LTF window, will find items near the fixation point much faster, so the key item in a fly-mode view should sit where the eye lands, usually near the top and centre of the glance, with minimal peripheral critical information. Fourth, the prevalence effect is a warning for challenge and checklist design: rare problems are missed more, so a rare error state (a missing step) needs a loud, redundant signal, and a lesson that asks learners to spot an error should vary how often errors appear. Wolfe himself cautions that real-world transfer is unproven, so these are design defaults to test.

## wolfe2004what
Wolfe, J. M., & Horowitz, T. S. (2004). What attributes guide the deployment of visual attention and how do they do it? Nature Reviews Neuroscience, 5(6), 495-501.

### Core claims
This opinion and review article asks which visual properties can steer attention during search and which cannot, and offers a ranked list. The authors argue that the old two-stage picture (a parallel "preattentive" stage, then attention) should be replaced by a guiding representation: an abstraction taken from early vision that controls where attention goes, sits beside the main route to object recognition, and is not itself that route. They therefore avoid the word preattentive. They conclude that colour, motion, orientation and size are assured guiding attributes, others (such as line termination) are probable, and others (such as threat, faces and a person's own name) are probably not guiding.

### Specifics
Measure and its limits: search efficiency is the slope of response time against set size. A slope near zero is efficient. The authors give about 20 to 40 ms per item for target-present trials in a search for a 2 among 5s, with absent slopes a bit more than twice as steep; and 150 to 300 ms per item if search is limited by the rate of eye movements. Efficient search is necessary but not sufficient: a black X can be found easily although it is a conjunction of luminance polarity and shape. Four other tests give converging evidence: effortless texture segmentation, search asymmetries (a moving item among stationary ones is easier than the reverse), illusory conjunctions, and tolerance of distractor heterogeneity.

Signal and noise: using Duncan and Humphreys' framing, efficiency rises with the target-distractor difference and falls with the distractor-distractor difference. The authors state that a search for a unique basic feature can be made arbitrarily hard by shrinking the difference, but that searches without a unique feature cannot be made arbitrarily easy by enlarging it. The bend in the function is far from the resolution limit: efficient colour search needs differences much larger than a just noticeable difference (a MacAdam ellipse), and the efficient region does not look like a scaled ellipse. For orientation, the critical target-distractor difference for efficient search is about 15 degrees, against roughly 1 to 2 degrees needed to discriminate lines. Distractor heterogeneity hurts most when distractors flank the target in feature space: a vertical target is easy among distractors all tilted 20 degrees left or all 20 degrees right, but hard among a mix of both. 
The ranked list (Table 1): undoubted are colour, motion, orientation and size (including length and spatial frequency). Possible include lighting direction, glossiness, expansion, number and aspect ratio. Doubtful include novelty, letter identity and alphanumeric category. Probable non-attributes include intersection, optic flow, colour change, three-dimensional volumes, faces, the observer's own name and semantic category such as "animal" or "scary". 
### Replication and criticism
This is an expert synthesis, not a single experiment, and Wolfe and Horowitz label their own categories as degrees of confidence. They say the face placement is debatable and that others would put faces higher. A 2017 follow-up by the same authors was listed in web search but I did not read it, so I cannot say whether the classification changed. The later Guided Search 6.0 paper (Wolfe 2021) keeps the core claims, confirms colour and motion, and says intersection type and surface material do not guide, which fits this list. 
### Implications for LTF
This is the best single reference for deciding which visual channel to spend on an alert. Colour, motion, orientation and size are the reliable channels; shape cues are less certain, and "this icon means danger" semantics will not make something pop on its own. A warning meant to be spotted in a glance should therefore differ in colour, size or orientation from its neighbours, with the difference well above the just noticeable level: roughly 15 degrees of tilt in an orientation cue suggests two needle states must differ visibly to pop, which constrains instrument widgets that show a normal band versus an alert. The heterogeneity result matters for status colours: if a dashboard shows many different hues, a target hue flanked by others in colour space is hard to find, whereas a target that can be separated by a single line in colour space (for example the only warm colour among cool ones) pops. So an LTF palette for state should keep ordinary content in a narrow range and reserve a separable accent for exceptions, in both themes.

## julesz1981textons
Julesz, B. (1981). Textons, the elements of texture perception, and their interactions. Nature, 290(5802), 91-97.

### Core claims
Julesz reports research with pairs of textures that have identical second-order statistics, and concludes that the preattentive texture discrimination system cannot globally process third- and higher-order statistics. Discrimination, he argues, is the result of a few local conspicuous features, which he calls textons. Only the first-order statistics of these textons (how many of each there are) have perceptual significance, and the relative phase or arrangement between textons cannot be perceived without detailed scrutiny by focal attention. This is the abstract of the paper, confirmed on PubMed (7207603). The paper therefore replaced an earlier statistical conjecture, that preattentive vision is sensitive only to first- and second-order statistics of the image, with a feature-based account.

### Specifics
The research method was to build texture pairs of randomly placed small elements, and to see whether a region of one element type segregates from a background of another without scrutiny. Landy's review chapter (read this session as a PDF from the NYU site) gives the standard illustration: a region of randomly placed Xs is easily distinguished from a background of Ls, but a region of Ts can only be distinguished with scrutiny, even though the individual elements are easy to tell apart one by one. There is no visible texture edge, and no perceived textured object, between the Ts and the Ls.

The same review states how the theory developed. The early conjecture was that observers were sensitive only to differences in the first- and second-order statistics of constituent textures. Counterexamples were found, and Julesz moved to the feature-based approach: the basic features, or textons, are defined as required by the empirical results and include elongated blobs and line endings or terminators; segregation results from differences in the blobs' characteristics (number, length, orientation and so on) and in the number of terminators. A web-search summary adds size and line crossings among early candidates.

The review also describes the later standard model of texture segregation, in which linear spatial filters like cortical simple cells are followed by a point-wise nonlinearity and further filtering, converting a texture difference into a response difference that can be detected as an edge (Bergen and Adelson 1988, Landy and Bergen 1991, Malik and Perona 1990, and others).

### Replication and criticism
The conjecture in its original statistical form was refuted by counterexamples, and the 1981 paper is the response. The texton idea is still cited, but modern accounts mostly prefer filter-based models over a list of textons. Landy notes that texture segregation and search are similar but not interchangeable: it is not always true that element pairs that search easily also segregate well, citing Wolfe (1992, titled "Effortless texture segmentation and parallel visual search are not the same thing"), and performance is often asymmetric, depending on which element is the target. Wolfe and Horowitz (2004) call effortless texture segmentation a good but imperfect diagnostic for a guiding feature. The evidence base is laboratory psychophysics with simple stimuli and the central demonstration (X versus L easy, T versus L hard) is reported as a standard result in the review.

### Implications for LTF
Texture segregation is the perceptual mechanism behind many design choices that learners never notice: striped table rows, hatched regions on a chart, a patterned fill on a gauge, a pitch-attitude indicator's sky and ground, the appearance of a runway or terrain in an explanation diagram. The rule from Julesz that matters is that a region will stand out from its surroundings preattentively when its local elements differ in simple features (orientation, size, line ends), and that a region differing only in how the same elements are arranged will not. So a hatch pattern for a "caution" band will segregate cleanly when its line orientation differs from the surrounding pattern, but a different arrangement of identical marks will require a deliberate look. Because fly mode relies on quick glances, texture should not carry critical differences unless the elements differ in a simple feature. Use flat fills for panels and reserve texture for a small number of purposeful encodings, with a second cue (label or colour) alongside, since a fine texture is also easily lost at small sizes or on low-quality displays.

I did not read the 1981 paper body.

## whitney2018ensemble
Whitney, D., & Yamanashi Leib, A. (2018). Ensemble perception. Annual Review of Psychology, 69, 105-129.

### Core claims
Ensemble perception is the visual system's ability to extract summary statistical information, such as the average size, orientation, motion direction, colour or facial expression, from a group of similar objects, often in a single brief glance. The review argues that this ability is found at low, middle and high levels of visual analysis, that it is efficient and in some conditions more precise than the perception of single items, and that it does not depend on being able to recognize individual items. It frames ensemble perception as a foundational limit on cognition, memory and behaviour: when we cannot see everything at once, what we get is a statistical gist.

### Specifics
Operational definition (section 3): ensemble perception is the ability to discriminate or reproduce a statistical moment (a mean or variance), and it requires integrating two or more items. Gestalt grouping cues need not involve a statistical moment, so grouping alone is not evidence of ensemble processing, though grouping may constrain or be produced by ensembles. Sensitivity falls as the variance of the underlying distribution rises, but the shape of the distribution (normal, rectangular, bimodal) matters less when variance is held constant.

Low-level ensembles: observers report the average motion direction of random dots (Watamaniuk and colleagues), the average speed, orientation, brightness, hue and spatial position. These may underlie texture recognition and scene gist. Mid-level: observers perceive the average size of an array of circles even in displays of less than one-tenth of a second (Ariely 2001; Chong and Treisman 2003), though the review calls ensemble size perception somewhat controversial (Myczek and Simons 2008), partly because low-level size detectors are less clear than for orientation or motion. High-level: observers judge average emotional expression and gender in a crowd of faces (Haberman and Whitney 2007, 2009), perhaps from a glance as short as 100 ms or less, and are less sensitive to crowds of inverted or scrambled faces, suggesting holistic face representations. 
How many items are integrated? A plot across many studies (their Figure 4) is fitted by a power function with exponent 0.58, suggesting observers integrate roughly the square root of the number of items. Individual estimates range from about 3 to 5 items per display in some basic-feature studies, to 60 to 75 percent efficiency for average size or variance of circles (integrating at least three circles), to more than 4 to 8 objects in face and biological-motion studies; the review notes these estimates vary and are debated. 

### Replication and criticism
Many low-level results (average motion, orientation) are well replicated. The mid-level size claim is the most disputed, mainly through the subsampling argument of Myczek and Simons (2008) that observers may use only a few items; the review itself reports that estimates of efficiency depend on methods and assumptions and are debated (it cites several papers on both sides). The "square root" rule is a fitted summary across heterogeneous studies, not a law, and the plotted values are conservative lower bounds that use the lower end of reported ranges. Whether ensemble coding is automatic, obligatory, unconscious or outside attention is described as an ongoing debate. The review is an author-written synthesis from a lab that has contributed heavily to the area, so claims about the breadth of ensemble perception deserve independent confirmation, particularly for social judgments.

### Implications for LTF
Ensembles explain why a well-built dashboard can be understood in a glance: the learner takes in the average level, the spread and the outliers of a set of similar marks without reading each one. LTF progress displays, checklists and a row of gauges should be designed so the ensemble answers the real question. A bar chart of lesson completion shows overall progress at once, whereas a table of numbers needs reading. In an instrument cluster widget, if the learner should notice that "one reading differs from the others", similar marks arranged in a regular row will make the odd one an outlier, but the outlier may be down-weighted in the average, so critical outliers should be additionally marked, not left to ensemble perception. The finding that variance reduces sensitivity says a noisy or jittery display makes the mean harder to read, so streaming widgets should be smoothed or otherwise stabilized when the learner is meant to read a trend.

## elder2002ecological
Elder, J. H., & Goldberg, R. M. (2002). Ecological statistics of Gestalt laws for the perceptual organization of contours. Journal of Vision, 2(4), 324-353.

### Core claims
Many experiments had measured how strong grouping cues are for controlled laboratory stimuli, but little was known about how useful each cue is in natural images. Elder and Goldberg measure this directly. People traced the contours they perceived in natural photographs; the traced curves were mapped to sequences of discrete tangent elements found in the image; and the relations between successive tangents on traced curves were compared with relations between randomly chosen tangent pairs. The difference between the two sets of statistics gives the likelihood distributions that an optimal Bayesian model of contour grouping would need. Their conclusion is that the classical cues of proximity, good continuation and luminance similarity can be put on an ecological footing, that proximity is by far the most powerful of the three, and that the proximity cue follows a scale-invariant power law in close agreement with earlier psychophysics.

### Specifics
The abstract (confirmed on PubMed 12678582) lists four results. First, when appropriately defined the cues are approximately uncorrelated, which supports a simple factorial model: the evidence from each cue can be multiplied together to form a combined estimate. Second, there is moderate image-to-image variation in the statistics, which argues for general probabilistic models rather than image-specific ones. Third, the cues differ greatly in inferential power, with proximity far the strongest. Fourth, statistical modeling of the proximity cue indicates a scale-invariant power law that agrees closely with prior psychophysics.

The review by Wagemans et al. (2012, PMC3482144) adds details. Elder and Goldberg's ecological power-law exponent for proximity was about 2.92, close to Oyama's (1961) perceptual estimate of about 2.89, whereas Sigman and colleagues (2001) had estimated a much weaker ecological exponent of 0.6, but with measurements not restricted to the same contour. For good continuation, Elder and Goldberg's sequential statistics gave much larger likelihood ratios than Geisler and colleagues' (2001) statistics for unordered sets, supporting good continuation as an ecologically useful cue. For similarity, brightness similarity was informative for grouping while contrast similarity was relatively weak; Geisler and Perry (2009) later showed contrast polarity is also informative.

Rather than assuming that a Gestalt law is good, it measures how much knowing the cue reduces uncertainty about whether two image elements belong to the same contour. It treats the old "laws" as evidence in a probabilistic decision.

### Replication and criticism
The finding that proximity dominates and good continuation also carries real information has been supported by later ecological work, as the review notes. The difference between Elder and Goldberg's exponent and Sigman et al.'s illustrates that results depend on how contours are sampled, and the Wagemans review attributes the discrepancy to measurement restricted or not to one contour. The study's stimuli are edges and contours in photographs, so it says nothing directly about grouping of text, icons or interface elements, and the three cues examined are only part of the classical set; common region, closure and symmetry were not measured here. It relies on human tracing as ground truth, which may reflect task bias. Overall, the evidence is a carefully controlled measurement study that has been widely cited, and it gives the strongest quantitative reason to treat proximity as the first-order grouping cue.

### Implications for LTF
For a designer the paper is a quantitative justification for a ranking: when elements in a layout are in competition, spacing carries most of the grouping information, and the closest neighbour will usually be taken as the partner. LTF layouts should therefore be spaced as if proximity were the primary grouping cue, with other cues (borders, colour, alignment) reinforcing it, not contradicting it. The power-law and scale-invariance result implies that the relation that matters is the ratio of distances, not absolute pixels, which supports a spacing scale built on ratios (for example a label that is much closer to its own value than to the next row) and implies that the same rule should hold when the interface is zoomed or resized. The independence of cues suggests a useful checklist: if proximity, continuation (alignment) and similarity all agree, grouping is very strong; if one disagrees, grouping weakens in proportion to that cue's power. Because proximity is strongest, a label placed ambiguously between two values will be read as belonging to the nearer one, even when styled like the other.

## healey2012attention
Healey, C. G., & Enns, J. T. (2012). Attention and visual memory in visualization and computer graphics. IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, 18(7), 1170-1188.

### Core claims
This survey is written for visualization designers. It reviews research on low-level (preattentive) vision, attention and visual memory, and asks how the findings should shape the display of data. The central message is that what viewers see depends on where attention is focused and on what is in their minds before they look; designers should assign visual features to data attributes in ways that suit the strengths of the visual system and the viewer's task, and avoid visual interference between features. The paper treats the term preattentive as a convenient label for how fast and easy some properties are to detect, noting that attention plays a role even in the first fixation.

### Specifics
Definition and tasks: tasks that can be done on a large multi-element display in less than 200 to 250 ms are conventionally called preattentive, because a saccade takes at least 200 ms to initiate, so the task is done in a single glance. Four task types are listed: target detection, boundary detection (a texture edge between two groups with a common property), region tracking, and counting and estimation. Examples: a red circle among blue circles, or a curved form among straight ones, pops out regardless of the number of distractors.

Theories covered, in order: feature integration (Treisman), texton theory (Julesz), similarity theory (Duncan and Humphreys, with target-distractor and distractor-distractor similarity), guided search (Wolfe), Boolean map theory (Huang and colleagues), then ensemble coding (summaries of the distribution of features that are available even when individual elements cannot be located), and feature hierarchy.

Feature hierarchy is the most design-relevant section. Callaghan showed that in boundary detection the visual system favours colour over shape: random variation in colour in the background slowed, but did not completely block, detection of a boundary formed by shape, while random variation in shape had no effect on seeing colour patterns. Luminance-on-hue and hue-on-texture preferences were also found. In Healey and Enns' own experiment, a 20 by 15 array of paper-strip glyphs varied hue, size, density and regularity: differences in hue, size and density were easy to see in isolation but differences in regularity were not; random texture variation did not affect detection of hue targets, but random hue variation degraded detection of size and density targets. A visual search experiment cited in the paper showed viewers can rapidly choose among up to seven isoluminant colours (Healey). On memory, the paper discusses change blindness: changes that do not alter the gist of a scene are the ones most likely to be missed, and large multi-monitor displays make this worse than small screens.

### Replication and criticism
This is a survey, so it inherits the status of the original studies: the preattentive features and change blindness are heavily replicated, while individual visualization guidelines come from a smaller set of studies, often with simple stimuli and short trials. The 200 to 250 ms rule of thumb is a convention, not a boundary, and Wolfe and Horowitz (2004) explicitly avoid the term preattentive and show search slopes are not bimodal, so the survey's presentation of preattentive features as a clean category should be read with that in mind. The interference results come largely from the authors' own lab and from Callaghan's earlier work, so they should be checked in complex, realistic displays. The paper itself lists challenges (visual acuity, aesthetics, engagement) as open.

### Implications for LTF
This paper is the best bridge from vision science to the design of dashboards and instrument widgets. Three rules follow. First, spend the strongest visual feature on the most important data attribute, so that decorative or secondary variation (texture, ornament) cannot hide it; in a status panel colour is stronger than shape, so if shape and colour both vary, colour will dominate. Second, avoid combining features on one mark when one will interfere with another (hue varying randomly makes size and density harder to read), which argues for restrained, consistent colour in widgets that also encode magnitude by size. Third, change blindness matters for fly mode and live widgets: a value that changes during a glance away to the sim will be missed unless the change draws attention or the new state persists visibly, so state changes (a challenge criterion met, a checklist step completed) should use a clear transient plus a lasting marker. Preattentive claims should be verified with the actual interface rather than assumed.

## rosenholtz2007measuring
Rosenholtz, R., Li, Y., & Nakano, L. (2007). Measuring visual clutter. Journal of Vision, 7(2), 17, 1-22.

### Core claims
Clutter is a recurring complaint in interface and visualization design, but designers lacked a reliable, computable measure. Rosenholtz, Li and Nakano argue that excess or disorganized display items cause crowding, masking, reduced recognition through occlusion, and greater difficulty segmenting a scene and finding things in it. They propose and test image-based measures of clutter that work on arbitrary images, including real screenshots, and show that these correlate well with search performance. They also suggest the measure can stand in for set size, which Guided Search and similar models need but which is hard to define in complex imagery.

### Specifics
Three measures are tested (abstract confirmed on PubMed 18217832). Feature Congestion is a new version of the measure from Rosenholtz, Li, Mansfield and Jin (2005, a CHI paper). Its rationale is an analogy: the more cluttered a display is, the harder it would be to add a new item that would reliably draw attention. A later study described in a web search summary says the measure is based on local variability in colour, orientation and luminance. Subband Entropy is based on the idea that clutter relates to the visual information in the display; it decomposes an image into wavelet subbands and computes the entropy in each. Edge Density was used by Mack and Oliva (2004) as a measure of subjective visual complexity.

The authors tested the measures as stand-ins for set size in search models and on search-in-clutter displays from Wolfe, Oliva, Horowitz, Butcher and Bompas (2002) and Bravo and Farid (2004), as well as in new experiments. They report that the measures correlate well with search performance. An additional experiment indicated that colour variability, which Feature Congestion accounts for but Edge Density and Subband Entropy do not, matters for visual clutter. MIT hosts MATLAB code for Feature Congestion and Subband Entropy (dspace.mit.edu handle 1721.1/37593).

### Replication and criticism
The measures have been used and tested by others. A 2009 Journal of Vision study by Henderson, Chanceaux and Smith, reported in a web search summary, tested whether feature congestion, subband entropy and edge density correlate with search performance and found statistically significant but numerically modest correlations. I did not locate the exact coefficients of the original paper, and I did not read the full text, so any claim here about effect size is limited to the abstract. A known limitation is that these measures describe low-level image statistics: they do not capture organization (a neatly grouped display with many items can be less confusing than a sparse but chaotic one), meaning, or the observer's task. The paper's own definition of clutter is tied to search difficulty, so it is a performance-based concept, not a general measure of beauty or ease of use.

### Implications for LTF
Clutter has a rigorous definition here: the more difficult it would be to add a new item that draws attention, the more cluttered the display. That is a useful test for fly mode and dashboards: if adding a warning would not stand out, the screen is too busy. Because colour variability contributes to clutter, a multi-colour interface, where many categories each have their own hue, is more cluttered than one with a restrained base palette, which supports the Duncan and Humphreys and Wolfe and Horowitz recommendations to keep the background uniform. The approach also gives LTF a possible automated check: Feature Congestion or a similar computed clutter score could be tracked across screenshots of lesson, challenge and fly-mode pages as an informal regression metric alongside contrast checks, with a caveat that the paper supports it as a predictor of search difficulty, not as a pass or fail threshold. Organization matters beyond count: because crowding and masking worsen with disorganized items, grouping related items into clear regions (common region, aligned columns) should reduce the effective clutter of a dense panel even when the item count is unchanged. Treat any numeric threshold as something to calibrate with LTF's own pages and learners.

## gibson2015ecological
Gibson, J. J. (2015; originally 1979). The ecological approach to visual perception. New York: Psychology Press (Houghton Mifflin, 1979, original publisher).

### Core claims
Gibson argues that vision should be studied as an animal moving through a structured environment, not as a camera-like retinal image that the brain must interpret. Light that has been reflected by surfaces forms an ambient optic array, and the array contains information specifying the layout and the events of the world, so perception can be direct, the pickup of information, with no need for inner representations built up from sensations. The most influential idea is the theory of affordances: what an environment offers the animal. The chapter on affordances (chapter 8, read this session from a course copy) states the radical hypothesis that the composition and layout of surfaces constitute what they afford, and so to perceive them is to perceive what they afford, which implies that the values and meanings of things in the environment can be perceived directly.

### Specifics
The book (Classic Edition, 346 pages, per the publisher page) has four parts: the environment to be perceived; the information for visual perception (including the ambient optic array and the theory of affordances); visual perception; and depiction. Appendices define the principal terms of ecological optics and the concept of invariants.

Affordance as defined in chapter 8: the affordances of the environment are what it offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, either for good or ill. The noun is Gibson's own coinage, and it refers to both environment and animal, implying their complementarity. His worked example is a terrestrial surface that is nearly horizontal, nearly flat, sufficiently extended and rigid relative to the animal: it affords support. A surface of those properties that is also knee-high relative to the perceiver affords sitting. Affordances are measured relative to the animal, not in physical units, and "knee-high for a child is not the same as knee-high for an adult". An affordance is neither an objective nor a subjective property, or is both: it points both ways, to environment and observer. He also discusses positive and negative affordances: a cliff edge affords walking along on one side and falling on the other.

Gibson traces the idea to the Gestalt notion of demand character: he quotes Koffka's Principles of Gestalt Psychology to the effect that a postbox "invites" the mailing of a letter and the handle "wants to be grasped" (as quoted in the chapter, citing Koffka 1935 p. 353), and Lewin's valence. Where Gestalt psychology held that these qualities are phenomenal, Gibson held that they are properties of the environment taken with reference to an observer.

### Replication and criticism
The book's claim that perception is direct is contested by representational and computational accounts, including Marr's (1982), and a review on the Western Norway University site (Karlsen) notes that the claim that meaning and value are directly perceived is radical and controversial and that Gibson's descriptions are human-centred. The affordance concept has been widely adopted and widely misused in design. Norman (1999, as quoted in a web search summary of his essay) conceded he should have used the term "perceived affordance" because in design what matters is what the user perceives, and McGrenere and Ho (2000) analyze the ambiguity of Norman's usage. Treat "affordance" in LTF documents as either Gibson's relation between animal and environment or Norman's perceived affordance or signifier, and say which.

### Implications for LTF
The book matters to LTF in three ways. First, flight is a task of optic flow, horizon and ground texture: much of what a pilot reads outside a cockpit window (the angle of the horizon, texture gradients on the ground, the flow pattern on approach) is ecological information of the kind Gibson described. Second, the affordance idea is a standard for controls: a widget should look like what it does, so that its possible actions are perceivable (a draggable thing looks draggable, a read-only gauge does not look like a slider). Where the web medium lacks real affordances, use the Norman distinction and make signifiers explicit. Third, affordances are relative to the perceiver, so LTF should not assume a beginner already sees what makes a cockpit readable; lessons should teach what to look at before asking what to do.

## marr2010vision
Marr, D. (2010). Vision: A computational investigation into the human representation and processing of visual information. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press (originally W. H. Freeman, 1982; foreword by Shimon Ullman, afterword by Tomaso Poggio).

### Core claims
Marr argues that to understand vision, or any information-processing system, one must analyze it at several distinct levels, and that the most neglected and most important is the computational level: what problem the system solves and why the solution it uses is appropriate to the world. He then proposes a specific sequence of representations by which the visual system could turn a two-dimensional retinal array into a description of the three-dimensional world: a primal sketch of edges and regions, a viewer-centred 2.5D sketch of surface layout and depth, and a 3D model representation. The book was written in the late 1970s and published after his death; the 2010 MIT Press edition reissues it.

### Specifics
Three levels of analysis (Marr's tri-level hypothesis). The computational level specifies what the device does and why; the representational and algorithmic level specifies how, meaning which representations and which processes; the implementational level specifies how the system is physically realized, for example in which neural structures. A philosophical analysis read this session (Bechtel and Shagrir 2015, Topics in Cognitive Science 7, 312-322) quotes Marr (1982, p. 22) as characterizing the computational level as specifying what the device does and why, and says that the "why" part is often overlooked: Marr grounds the computed function in physical constraints of the environment. Their example is edge detection: early vision computes zero-crossings of a second derivative of the image because sharp changes in reflected light often occur along physical edges such as object boundaries. Marr illustrated the levels with a cash register, according to a web account, and argued that the computational level matters most for designing effective systems. 
Representations. The primal sketch extracts basic components such as edges and regions, comparable to a quick pencil sketch. The 2.5D sketch adds depth cues (stereopsis, optic flow, motion parallax) and represents a viewer-centred three-dimensional view of the environment but not the full surroundings. The 3D model is the final stage, representing the scene as a continuous three-dimensional map (Wikipedia account of Marr's framework, with the caveat that it is a secondary source).

Wagemans et al. (2012) note that mid-level vision, where grouping and figure-ground belong, still lacks the integrated models found at low levels.

### Replication and criticism
Marr's levels have remained a standard vocabulary in cognitive science, but they are criticized. Pillow (2024, found by web search and not read in full) argues the framework shows undue favouritism for the computational level at the expense of the other two and that the levels are not independent, and McClamrock (1991) argues the notion of level runs together two kinds of explanatory shift (grain and contextual function), as summarized in the same results. Bechtel and Shagrir (2015) defend the three levels as making non-redundant contributions. The three-stage representational scheme (primal sketch, 2.5D sketch, 3D model) is less widely accepted today: modern vision research stresses feedback, attention, task and scene context, and a web account notes that Marr's framework does not capture visual attention well. Gibson's school rejected the premise that perception requires internal representations of this kind. The book is theory plus computational models, not a set of experiments; its value is as a framework, not as a source of effect sizes.

### Implications for LTF
Marr's main usefulness for LTF is methodological. The three-level distinction gives a way to organize design decisions: at the computational level, what does the learner need to know about the aircraft state and why (for example, whether the aircraft is in a safe configuration); at the representational level, what display form makes that information easy to extract (a gauge, a number, an attitude picture); and at the implementation level, the actual widget code and pixels. LTF design reviews should resist jumping straight to pixels, and should ask first which task a display exists to support. The what-and-why discipline is also a useful test for widget text: a diagram that cannot state what the learner should conclude from it and why has no computational-level definition yet. The staged picture of vision supports the practice of keeping early visual structure (edges, regions, texture, depth cues) clean before adding meaning: if the sketch is cluttered, later recognition suffers.

## ware2008visual
Ware, C. (2008). Visual thinking for design. Burlington, MA: Morgan Kaufmann (Elsevier). ISBN 9780123708960.

### Core claims
Ware writes for working designers and argues that a display is a cognitive tool, an extension of the viewer's brain in the way a hammer extends the hand (publisher's jacket text, via the Open Library record). The book converts what is known about perception, attention and memory into practical design advice. Its central ideas, as described in a detailed review, are active vision and visual queries: we do not build a full inner model of the scene; the world serves as its own memory and we query it with eye movements as needed. Design therefore means making the information needed by the next query easy to find and easy to read. The book is a short, readable bridge between vision science and design, and it is deliberately lighter than Ware's technical text Information Visualization: Perception for Design.

### Specifics
I did not have the book itself; what follows comes from the publisher's description (Open Library edition OL25538971M, 197 pages, ISBN 9780123708960) and Robert Kosara's chapter-by-chapter review on eagereyes.org (June 2008). The review lists nine chapters. (1) Visual memory and change blindness, introducing active vision and a first model combining bottom-up and top-down processing, and a "brain pixel" device for the eye-as-camera comparison. (2) Parallel processing and pop-out: visual pathways, eye-movement planning, pre-attentive features and a visual search model built on them; Ware notes that "pre-attentive" is a misnomer. (3) Spatial organization and pattern: from edge detection to texture, pattern learning and visual metaphor. The review calls this chapter superficial and notes missing pointers to further reading. (4) Colour, including why greyscale shows more detail than colour, and a link between the number of colour names across languages and the number of colours people easily tell apart. (5) Depth perception: depth cues, motion in depth, and design for perception that is roughly 2.5D (Ware suggests closer to 2.05D). (6) Three-dimensional objects and memory (geons, working and long-term visual memory). (7) Visual and verbal narrative. (8) Meta-seeing: creative visual thinking from the designer's side, including sketching and critique. (9) A concluding recap, "The Dance of Meaning".

It reports the book has marginal notes and no back-matter references and that the reviewer found nearly two dozen errata in a first reading, including a misspelling of Anne Treisman's name.

Do not attribute a specific list of laws to Ware without checking the book.

### Replication and criticism
This is an expert synthesis for designers, not a primary study. The reviewer's criticisms are of coverage and documentation: superficial treatment of some topics, thin referencing, errors, and an apparent tension between the "world as memory" idea in chapter 1 and the claim that most of what we see is already in our heads in chapter 6. A summary site notes that some argue Ware's camera-eye analogy oversimplifies vision biology (a weak secondary source; I did not verify the claim). Ware's description of pop-out as a misnomer matches Wolfe and Horowitz (2004), who avoid the term preattentive. The book was published before Wolfe's 2021 revision and should be read with that update in mind. Because there are few references in the book, its specific claims must be traced to the primary papers before being used as evidence in LTF guidance; for LTF it is best treated as an accessible secondary source that points to Gibson, Treisman and Wolfe.

### Implications for LTF
The book's most useful idea for LTF is the visual query. A lesson page or widget should be designed around the question the learner is asking at that moment (what is my airspeed, am I within the target band), so that the answer is available by looking at one place. Fly mode in particular is a sequence of rapid queries made between glances at the sim; it should present a stable layout in which each query has a fixed location, so eye movements become routine. Active vision also suggests that the learner does not need to memorize what is on screen: keep the information visible and let them look it up, rather than hiding it behind taps or expecting recall. Pop-out guidance applies to the next action or an alert (see Wolfe and Horowitz 2004). The 2.5D point supports flat, layered interface design and cautions against 3D effects, tilt or perspective in widgets unless the depth is actually needed, since depth mainly helps with grasping tasks, in the author's view.

## arnheim1974art
Arnheim, R. (1974). Art and visual perception: A psychology of the creative eye (The new version). Berkeley: University of California Press. (First edition 1954.)

### Core claims
Arnheim, an art theorist and Gestalt psychologist, applies Gestalt principles to the visual arts. Vision, he argues, is not a mechanical recording of elements but the apprehension of significant structural patterns, and the patterns we see are dynamic: they carry perceptual forces such as tension, balance, direction and weight. The preface to the new version says the book was entirely rewritten to show that two principles, the tendency toward the simplest structure and the dynamic character of percepts, apply to every visual phenomenon. This is a craft and theory treatise, not an experimental report, and it has long been used as a bridge from Gestalt psychology to design education.

### Specifics

Balance. A dark disc placed off-centre on a white square is felt to strain toward or away from certain points; these pulls come from the structural skeleton of the square (its centre, central vertical and horizontal axes and diagonals), even though none of it is drawn. Arnheim calls these psychological forces real in experience, not physical forces in the ink. Equilibrium is reached when forces compensate one another, and "dead center is not dead". Visual weight increases with position on the skeleton, with distance that opens into space, with size (black must be larger than white to balance it), with colour (red heavier than blue, bright heavier than dark), with isolation, with regular compact shape, with verticality compared to obliqueness and with intrinsic interest. 
Shape. The basic law is that any stimulus pattern tends to be seen so that the resulting structure is as simple as the given conditions permit; simplicity is judged by structural features such as distance and angle, not the number of elements. Arnheim distinguishes levelling (symmetry, repetition, reduced tension) from sharpening (enhanced difference, increased tension). In the section on similarity and difference he reduces Wertheimer's grouping rules to one rule of similarity, which acts only in conjunction with separation, as a force of attraction among segregated things. Similarity can apply to shape, brightness, colour, location, movement and speed, and proximity is recast as similarity of spatial location. A visual object is more unitary the more strictly similar its elements are. A discrepancy between form and meaning interferes with simplicity.

Figure and ground (Space). Following Rubin, the surrounded surface tends to be seen as figure and the surrounding, unbounded one as ground. Further factors he lists: smaller area, enclosure and texture, simplicity and symmetry, convexity, alignment with the frame's vertical and horizontal axes, lower position, and relative motion. Negative spaces must be given sufficient figure quality to be perceivable in their own right, and the spacing between figures must be as carefully controlled as the figures themselves.

### Replication and criticism
Arnheim's claims are craft and phenomenological observations, not controlled experiments, and they predate the quantitative work reviewed by Wagemans et al. (2012). Some of his figure-ground factors have been tested later with mixed outcomes: that review reports lower-region effects (Vecera et al. 2002) as an empirical finding, but concludes that the strength of convexity and symmetry in traditional demonstrations was overestimated (Peterson and Salvagio 2008). The book's value is its descriptive richness, which makes the forces of a composition nameable, and it has influenced designers for decades.

### Implications for LTF
Arnheim gives LTF a vocabulary for layout critique beyond grouping: balance, weight and tension. A lesson page with an off-centre hero image, a heavy block at the top and thin text below has a visible weight distribution, and reviewers can discuss it concretely using his factors (size, colour, isolation, location). Because bright and saturated elements look heavier, dark theme and light theme will distribute weight differently: a bright accent on a dark ground carries more weight than the same accent on white, so accent usage should be re-balanced when the theme changes. His statement that negative space needs figure quality and that spacing between figures must be controlled as carefully as the figures supports treating margins and gutters as designed elements in the grid, not leftovers. The warning that a discrepancy between form and meaning interferes with simplicity is a practical rule for icons and diagrams: do not give a decorative shape the structural simplicity of a meaningful one.
