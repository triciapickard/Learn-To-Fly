# Lane 03: Type on screens, research notes

## dyson2004physical

Dyson, M. C. (2004). How physical text layout affects reading from screen. Behaviour & Information Technology, 23(6), 377-393.

### Core claims

Dyson reviews the screen-reading experiments on text layout: line length, columns, window height and interlinear spacing. She writes from the Department of Typography and Graphic Communication at the University of Reading, and her central argument is that the literature is small, confounded and immature, so that confident rules copied from print typography are not supported for screens. Her synthesis is that most line-length studies on screen found faster reading with longer lines, and that the number of characters per line, not the physical width of the line or the visual angle, is the variable that best organizes the results. Readers' preferences often diverged from their performance.

### Specifics

Dyson separates three quantities that are easily confused: physical line length (or visual angle), characters per line, and type size. Any pairwise comparison holds one constant and varies the other two, so a study can almost never isolate one of them.

The studies she describes, all confirmed from her review (stu.westga.edu copy of the paper):
- Kolers and colleagues (1981) compared 35 and 70 characters per line, achieved by halving character width on a low-resolution display. Twenty participants read about 300-word passages. The narrower characters with more per line were read faster, with fewer total fixations. Duchnicky and Kolers (1983) confirmed this and found a positive correlation between characters per line and reading rate.
- Dyson and Kipping (1998) held type at 10 point Arial with 12 point spacing and varied line length from 25 to 100 characters per line, with 48 participants and passages of about 800 words. The longest line was read faster than the shortest, with no other significant differences.
- Bernard and colleagues (2002) compared 45, 76 and 132 characters per line in 12 point Arial with 20 adults and found no difference in reading time or efficiency. Dyson notes that this failed to replicate the earlier result and suggests the 132-character line was extreme.
- Dyson and Haselgrove (2001) found that 55 characters per line produced better comprehension than 100, using a comprehension test with several question types, and that the 25-character line fell in between.
- Dyson and Kipping (1997) compared one column of about 80 characters per line with a three-column paged layout. The single paged column was read fastest, but only for 18 to 24 year olds, and readers preferred three columns.
- Window-height studies (for example 20 versus 60 lines, and 15, 25 and 35 lines) mostly found no difference in speed or comprehension, though readers preferred larger windows. On leading, early screens favored double over single spacing, but Dyson found no recent empirical studies of fine spacing.

She records the print benchmarks: Tinker's work interpreted as an optimum of 52 characters per line (by Rayner and Pollatsek), and a guideline of not more than about 70 (Spencer). Her proposed mechanism is that saccades scale in characters, not degrees, and that greater viewing distance on screens shrinks the return-sweep angle.

### Replication and criticism

This is expert synthesis of experiments, not a meta-analysis, and she is candid about the problems: small samples (some studies had 10 to 20 participants), low-resolution 1980s displays, and tasks ranging from proofreading to searching for hidden shapes. She criticizes studies that pooled many "recommended" settings (the "kitchen sink" approach) because they cannot say which setting mattered. The finding that longer lines were read faster did not replicate in the Bernard study with 132 characters per line. The studies predate responsive layouts, touch screens and high-density displays.

### Implications for LTF

Treat line length as a range, not a number. The evidence supports neither very short lines nor very long ones as clearly better for speed, but comprehension and stated preference tilt toward moderate lines, so lesson prose should sit in a moderate band (a measure in the region of the mid-range lines in these studies) rather than stretching to fill a wide desktop window. Keep lesson text in a single column, and use a narrower column inside the full-screen "fly mode" where a learner glances away from a simulator. Because performance and preference diverge, do not rely on user comments about "feels easy"; test completion time and quiz results. Because the evidence is old and confounded, treat the line-length cap as a default to validate in LTF's own tests, and avoid multi-column lesson layouts, since in the one comparison reported the single paged column was read fastest, though only by younger readers.

## legge2011does

Legge, G. E., & Bigelow, C. A. (2011). Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography. Journal of Vision, 11(5), 8.

### Core claims

Legge, a vision scientist, and Bigelow, a type designer, argue that print size is constrained by the visual system. Text can be read at maximum speed across a "fluent range" of print sizes spanning about a factor of ten in angular x-height, from roughly 0.2 degrees to 2 degrees, and the print sizes used in books, newspapers and historical type specimens fall largely inside that range. Their conclusion is that properties of human visual processing play a dominant role in constraining the print sizes in common use, while economic, social, technological and artistic factors also matter.

### Specifics

Definitions and conversions confirmed from the paper (PMC3428264):
- Angular size is 57.3 times physical size divided by viewing distance. At a standard reading distance of 40 cm (16 inches), an x-height of 0.2 degrees corresponds to 1.4 mm (4 points) and 2 degrees to 14 mm (40 points).
- Critical print size (CPS) is the size below which reading speed drops sharply. The consensus value is about 0.2 degrees x-height, with individual estimates from about 0.15 to 0.3 degrees. Above about 1 to 3 degrees reading slows again for large print.
- The authors choose x-height as the main size metric rather than point size because point size is a global body measure that most letters do not fill. About 94.5% of running text is lowercase and about 87% of lowercase black area lies in the x-height zone. The x-height fraction of body size varies by typeface: Karow's measurement of 1,049 roman faces ranged from 0.28 to 0.58 with a mean of 0.46. 
- Contemporary print survey: newspaper running text has a mean of 0.23 degrees (1.64 mm), paperback fiction 0.24 degrees, hardcover fiction 0.24 degrees, with ranges from 0.20 to 0.28 degrees. 
- Historical type: 15th-century Italian roman averaged 0.30 degrees and 18th-century foundry types 0.22 degrees, with a minority (37% of the latter) below the fluent range.
- Screens: mean x-height fractions were 0.42 for hardcovers, 0.48 for newspapers and 0.52 for screen-designed faces. In the authors' online-news sample (132 ppi, 40 cm), home page body text averaged 0.19 degrees (below CPS) and article text 0.21 degrees (just above).
- Explanations: oculomotor limits are not the main cause, because RSVP (one word at a time) shows the same curve. The better account is the visual span, limited by crowding (Pelli and colleagues; Bouma's law). Below CPS, letter spacing falls within the crowding zone and the span shrinks. Widening spacing did not enlarge the span (Yu and colleagues 2007).

### Replication and criticism

The CPS and fluent-range findings rest on a long line of psychophysics from the Legge laboratory and others, with consistent values across different methods, including RSVP and page reading. This is therefore among the strongest bodies of evidence in the lane. The survey component is descriptive, and the argument that print sizes cluster in the fluent range is correlational: tradition may also explain the convergence. The authors acknowledge economic and social factors, noting that small subfluent sizes appeared when paper was expensive. The paper's "fluent range" is also defined for readers with normal vision, and the authors point out that low-vision readers have a CPS that is a multiple of their acuity limit. The 40 cm viewing distance is a convention that real devices violate.

### Implications for LTF

LTF should specify type sizes by what the eye receives, not by CSS pixels alone. Because phones are held near and desktop monitors far away, the same CSS size subtends different angles; the safe policy is to keep body text well above CPS, so that the x-height lands comfortably within the fluent range on a 24-inch monitor beside a sim as well as on a phone. Choose a body face with a generous x-height fraction (the screen-designed faces in the survey ran higher), and compare faces by x-height fraction, not by nominal size. In fly mode, where the learner glances from a sim monitor at arm's length or more, the viewing distance grows, and text that is fluent on a phone drops toward CPS, so size must scale up with expected distance and with the large-print end of the range when a tablet is propped beside a yoke. Aviation-instrument widgets that show numbers at small sizes should be tested against these thresholds.

## gugerty2004effects

Gugerty, L., Tyrrell, R. A., Aten, T. R., & Edmonds, K. A. (2004). The effects of subpixel addressing on users' performance and preferences during reading-related tasks. ACM Transactions on Applied Perception, 1(2), 81-101.

### Core claims

This is the main academic test of the claim that subpixel text rendering, which Microsoft markets as ClearType, helps people read. The team at Clemson University ran four experiments comparing subpixel-rendered text with aliased (black and white, unsmoothed) text on LCD screens. According to the abstract as relayed by search results, participants preferred the subpixel text and rated it more readable. It also improved the accuracy of lexical decision (deciding whether a briefly flashed string is a word) and the accuracy and speed of sentence comprehension. It did not change word-naming performance or the speed of reading for pleasure. The paper therefore supports a modest but real benefit for the lowest-level processes of word recognition, and no benefit for relaxed extended reading.

### Specifics

Subpixel addressing treats the red, green and blue stripes of an LCD pixel as separately addressable, which roughly triples the horizontal resolution available for drawing letter edges, at the cost of colored fringes.

Two of the experiments are described in detail in posts written by Kevin Larson, a Microsoft reading psychologist, on the Microsoft font blog (learn.microsoft.com archive of "fontblog"). These are a secondhand account by an interested party, so treat the numbers as reported, not as independently confirmed:
- Tachistoscopic lexical decision: 24 readers saw 280 words and 280 pseudowords (for example a real word versus a string such as "tadints"), half rendered with ClearType and half with black and white. Readers were more accurate with ClearType, which Larson reports as a 17% accuracy improvement after correcting for guessing. The task was chosen because ordinary reading of a word takes about a quarter of a second, too short to time directly.
- Sentence comprehension: 46 Clemson students each judged 144 pairs of sentences as belonging together or not. Larson reports that ClearType sentences were read faster by about 5% and answered correctly slightly more often, with accuracy of 91.5% for ClearType trials against 89.8% for black and white (F(1,45) = 6.06, p = .02). The benefit was larger for italic text. The stimuli used Verdana and Verdana Italic, and the screen was 115 dpi with the operating system at its default 96 dpi.

### Replication and criticism

The paper sits between two other lines of work. Later studies found benefits in some tasks (Dillon and colleagues in 2006 found faster extended reading and spreadsheet scanning with ClearType, but with large individual differences). Sheedy and colleagues found, in carefully controlled threshold and reading tests, that ClearType did not beat perceptually tuned grayscale rendering, although observers preferred a moderate setting. A crucial point raised even in comments on Larson's blog is that the comparison group in Gugerty et al. was aliased black and white, not grayscale anti-aliasing, so the gain is relative to the worst baseline and says little about whether subpixel rendering beats ordinary smoothing. The effects are small (a few percent in sentence reading), sample sizes are modest, and the stimuli came from a single typeface. The sponsor relationship (Microsoft interest in the technology) is a reason to read the vendor-blog numbers cautiously. The peer-reviewed abstract is more conservative than the blog posts.

### Implications for LTF

LTF cannot control subpixel rendering, which is decided by the operating system, browser and display. The practical conclusions are about robustness. First, do not design text that only looks acceptable under one rendering mode: use sizes and weights that survive aliased, grayscale and subpixel rendering, since learners will use laptops, tablets and phones with different pipelines. Second, because benefits were largest for the briefest word recognition (glances), type used in fly mode, where a learner takes in a checklist item in a fraction of a second, should favor the clearest, most generous shapes: not thin weights, not tiny sizes. Third, avoid lightweight italic for critical information, since italic was the case in which rendering mattered most. Fourth, do not assume that "smooth" rendering equals legibility: test on the real range of devices, including a low-density desktop monitor placed beside a simulator display.

## sheedy2005text

Sheedy, J. E., Subbaram, M. V., Zimmerman, A. B., & Hayes, J. R. (2005). Text legibility and the letter superiority effect. Human Factors, 47(4), 797-815.

### Core claims

Sheedy and colleagues measured how font design and display parameters change the smallest size at which text can be identified, a threshold legibility approach. Their headline finding is a letter superiority effect: individual lowercase letters were 10% to 20% more legible than lowercase words, meaning words had to be 10% to 20% larger to reach the same threshold. They conclude that individual letters play a large role and word shape a smaller role, if any, in word identification at threshold. This challenges the popular claim that we recognize words by their overall outline. The paper also ranks fonts and rendering modes, and reports an optimal legibility at about 9 pixels (10 points).

### Specifics

All of the following is from the abstract (Europe PMC record of the paper, confirmed this session), unless stated otherwise.
- Method: threshold size, where participants' visual acuity (the inverse of the minimum detection size) was measured for each condition. Stimuli were upper- and lowercase letters and lowercase words, across combinations of 6 fonts, 3 font-smoothing modes, 4 font sizes, 10 pixel heights and 4 stroke widths.
- Pixel height, font, stroke width and font smoothing each had significant main effects on threshold legibility.
- Optimal legibility was attained at 9 pixels (10 points).
- Verdana and Arial were the most legible fonts. Times New Roman and Franklin were the least legible.
- Subpixel rendering (ClearType) improved threshold legibility for some fonts, and in combination with Verdana it was the most legible condition.
- Increased stroke width (bold) improved threshold legibility, but only at the thinnest width tested.

Legge and Bigelow (2011) cite this study when discussing x-height: they report that Verdana, with an x-height fraction of about 0.55, was most legible in Sheedy and colleagues' set and Times New Roman, at about 0.45, least, and suggest that a larger x-height fraction gives more pixels to the lowercase body, a plausible mechanism.

### Replication and criticism

Threshold legibility is not reading. The study measures the smallest size at which text can be identified, so it speaks to small-text conditions, such as labels and tick marks, and not to paragraph reading. The letter-superiority conclusion is in line with the broader reading-psychology literature that rejects the idea of word-shape reading (see the word recognition review by Larson at Microsoft for the history, and Rayner's work in lane 02). A caution: the tested fonts were limited to six, the displays belonged to the early 2000s, and "optimal at 9 pixels" is a property of those displays and tasks. On modern high-density screens, pixel heights are not what limits legibility. It would be a misuse to cite "9 pixels is optimal" as a body-size rule for today's web.

### Implications for LTF

Use this paper chiefly to settle arguments. When someone says words are recognized by their shape and so, for example, all-lowercase labels or distinctive silhouettes matter more than letter forms, the evidence points the other way: letter forms, spacing and clarity matter. That supports choosing a UI typeface for open, distinct letters (clear differences between I, l and 1, and between O and 0, matter for aviation callouts such as altitudes and frequencies). It also supports sans-serif faces with large x-heights for small UI labels, while keeping in mind that this finding is about threshold size. For instrument-style widgets with small captions, test legibility at the actual rendered size on the lowest-resolution screen LTF supports. Do not read this paper as proof that sans-serif is better for long reading: it measured threshold detection, not sustained comprehension.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1518/001872005775570998 (Human Factors 47(4), 797-815, December 2005, four authors); the abstract as returned by the Europe PMC search record for this title; the Legge and Bigelow (2011) full text at PMC3428264 for the x-height-fraction comparison.

## dillon2006visual

Dillon, A., Kleinman, L., Choi, G. O., & Bias, R. (2006). Visual search and reading tasks using ClearType and regular displays: two experiments. In Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '06), 503-511. ACM.

### Core claims

Dillon and colleagues at the University of Texas at Austin School of Information asked whether the speed advantage claimed for ClearType-style subpixel rendering carries over to ordinary office work, not only to the brief word recognition tasks of earlier studies. They ran two experiments, one on extended reading of text and one on scanning spreadsheets for target information. Search-result abstracts and Kevin Larson's summary of the paper agree on the main pattern: performance was faster with ClearType, with no difference in accuracy or in self-reported visual fatigue. The abstract also reports substantial individual differences, from which the authors suggest that ClearType may not be universally beneficial to information workers.

### Specifics

The figures below come from Larson's Microsoft font blog post "ClearType improves the efficiency of typical office tasks" (July 2008), which is a secondhand account written by a member of the team that promoted ClearType, so they are reported as such and not independently confirmed from the paper.
- Extended reading: participants read five screens of 12 point Arial text rendered with ClearType and five screens rendered in black and white. Articles were counterbalanced so that half the participants saw a given article in each rendering. Mean reading time was 9 minutes 9 seconds with ClearType and 9 minutes 39 seconds without, a speed advantage of about 5%, and the advantage held for each of the five screens.
- Spreadsheet scanning: participants answered questions that required them to look through each row of a table (for example, counting books suitable for an age band at a given price). Mean completion time was 4 minutes 40 seconds with ClearType and 5 minutes 4 seconds without, a difference of about 8%.
- Search results summarizing the paper report that the scanning experiment had 26 participants, and that no accuracy or visual-fatigue differences were found in either experiment. A secondary source claims that more than a third of participants were disadvantaged by ClearType, but this figure was not confirmed from the paper and is not used here.

The paper compares ClearType with "regular" rendering, which in this period means aliased black-and-white text, not modern grayscale anti-aliasing.

### Replication and criticism

This study partly agrees with Gugerty and colleagues (faster sentence processing with subpixel rendering) and partly with Sheedy and colleagues, whose later controlled experiments found that preference favored moderate ClearType but that legibility, reading speed and comfort were not improved over grayscale rendering. The disagreement is mostly about the baseline: if the alternative is aliased text, subpixel rendering helps modestly, and if the alternative is good grayscale smoothing, the advantage is hard to find. The two experiments used one typeface and size (12 point Arial for reading), so generalization to other faces, sizes and densities is uncertain. Effects of 5% to 8% are small relative to the individual differences the authors report. Bias, Larson and colleagues later studied why people prefer subpixel text, in a 2010 paper in the Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology, and found that visual acuity, hue sensitivity and personality traits were linked to judgments (summary from the Microsoft Research page). This supports the notion that preference is partly personal and physiological. The vendor connection of some of those authors is another reason to treat the effect sizes with caution.

### Implications for LTF

Two lessons transfer. First, benefits of rendering technology appear in tasks that require scanning many words and numbers, such as tables and checklists, more than in leisurely prose. LTF's checklist, performance-data and quiz-answer lists belong to that class, so their typography should assume rapid scanning: clear row separation, aligned numerals, adequate size. Second, individual differences are large, so there is no single rendering or style that suits everyone. LTF should therefore not fix its text appearance with fragile tricks such as ultra-thin weights that only look right on a particular display, and should offer user control where cheap: text size scaling, a high-contrast mode, and respect for the operating system's font settings. Because a learner may flip between a phone, a laptop and a large monitor, test body and table text across rendering pipelines instead of tuning to a single one.

## delgado2018dont

Delgado, P., Vargas, C., Ackerman, R., & Salmeron, L. (2018). Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension. Educational Research Review, 25, 23-38.

### Core claims

Across 54 studies published or presented between 2000 and 2017, reading comparable text on paper produced slightly better comprehension than reading it on a digital screen. The authors call this the "screen inferiority effect" and describe it as robust but modest and conditional. Three moderators were significant: the paper advantage was larger when reading time was constrained than when it was self-paced, it appeared for informational texts (and mixtures) but not for narrative-only texts, and it grew with publication year, the opposite of what one would expect if people were simply getting used to screens. The authors conclude that nobody should recommend avoiding digital devices, but that the evidence should not be ignored, and that there are predictable conditions in which the two media are equivalent.

### Specifics

All of the following is from the accepted manuscript on the University of Valencia site (uv.es/lasalgon/papers).
- Inclusion rules: readers read individually and silently; the texts were comparable across media in content, structure and images (so hyperlinks and web navigation were excluded); participants read in their daily language and were from a normative population; studies were from 2000 to 2017 and in English. The search found 1,840 records, which yielded 54 studies and 76 media comparisons.
- Design and sample: 38 between-participant studies (56 effect sizes after removing two outliers) and 16 within-participant studies (18 effect sizes), with 171,055 participants in total, of whom the large majority (165,778) came from four large-scale studies. Most comparisons were with undergraduates and used computers.
- Main effect: Hedges' g = -.21 for between-participant designs (95% CI -.28 to -.14), and -.21 for within-participant designs (95% CI -.37 to -.06). Negative values mean paper was better. Heterogeneity was medium-high (I-squared = 72.24 between participants), with a prediction interval of -.56 to .14, so in some populations the effect is trivial or reversed.
- Time frame moderator: g = -.26 with time constraints versus -.09 for self-paced reading.
- Genre moderator: g = -.27 for informational texts, -.30 for mixed texts, and .01 for narrative-only texts.
- Year moderator: the paper advantage grew by about .01 per year since 2000.
- Not significant moderators: educational level, text length, type of device, need for scrolling, and type of comprehension question. Numerically, the effect was larger for computers (g = -.23) than for hand-held devices (-.12, not significant), and larger when scrolling was needed (-.25) than when it was not (-.13).
- Bias checks: no evidence of publication bias by Egger's test or other checks.

### Replication and criticism

A meta-analysis of experiments is among the strongest forms of evidence, and the authors tested sensitivity (removing outliers, large studies and unpublished work did not change the result). Limitations they state: high unexplained heterogeneity, simplistic use of publication year as a proxy for generation, ten otherwise-eligible studies lost to missing statistics, and the deliberate exclusion of digital affordances such as hypertext and navigation. The comparison is with paper, not with other screen designs, so the paper does not tell us how to set type on screens, and the comprehension tests mostly measured text recall and inference after reading, usually with university students. The narrative-only null is based on few comparisons. The effect is small by usual standards, and an interpretation in terms of a "shallowing" hypothesis (that screens invite shallower processing) remains theoretical, not directly tested here. The authors cite Mangen and colleagues (2013) as one of the individual studies pointing the same way.

### Implications for LTF

The result matters most for the lesson page. LTF teaches informational content (procedures, numbers, relationships) under conditions that fall in the zone where the screen penalty is largest: dense, expository text, often read quickly. Three design consequences follow, none of which require pretending that learners can use paper. First, do not rely on reading alone for the facts that must stick: pair text with active tasks (predict-then-reveal, quiz questions, interactive widgets), which also address the overconfidence the authors discuss. Second, keep time pressure out of reading: in-sim challenges that ask learners to read instructions while flying are a time-constrained condition, so put critical text in short, glanceable units. Third, since scrolling showed a numerically larger penalty (not significant), keep long lessons chunked into short sections with clear progress markers instead of one long scroll. Offer a printable lesson summary or checklist for learners who study better on paper.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.003 (Educational Research Review 25, 23-38, November 2018); read the full text of the author manuscript at uv.es/lasalgon/papers/Delgado 2018 dont throw away your printed books.pdf (abstract, methods, results, limitations, conclusions).

## wallace2022towards

Wallace, S., Bylinskii, Z., Dobres, J., Kerr, B., Berlow, S., Treitman, R., Kumawat, N., Arpin, K., Miller, D. B., Huang, J., & Sawyer, B. D. (2022). Towards individuated reading experiences: Different fonts increase reading speed for different individuals. ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction, 29(4), Article 38.

### Core claims

The authors ask how much reading speed can change if only the font changes, after font size is normalized by human perception. Their answer is that no single font is reliably fastest for everyone, but that individuals differ a great deal: participants read 35% faster in their personally fastest font than in their slowest of the five fonts they were tested on, with comprehension unchanged. Stated preference did not predict speed, even after size was controlled. The authors therefore argue for individuation: reading systems should let each reader find a font that suits them. They coin "Interlude Reading" for reading a few paragraphs between other tasks.

### Specifics

Everything below was read in the full text of the article (PDF from jeffhuang.com).
- Fonts and size: 16 common fonts. Because the same nominal size gives different apparent sizes across fonts, the team used Times at 16 px as a reference, computed sizes matching x-height, character height and character width, and ran a crowd-sourced perceptual task to choose a normalized size for each font.
- Participants: 352 crowd workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk (aged 18 to 71; 118 aged 35 or older), reading on their own devices (mostly laptops and desktops).
- Procedure: a preference test run as a double-elimination tournament over the 16 fonts (30 comparisons plus 6 repeated comparisons for consistency, averaging 72% self-consistency), then 10 rounds of an effectiveness test. Each round showed a passage split over two screens (69 to 93 words per screen), followed by two multiple-choice comprehension questions. Each participant read two passages in each of five fonts: Noto Sans, Times, their own preferred font, and two random others.
- Population-level results: font was not a significant factor for reading speed across the whole sample, while passage, topic familiarity, order, screen order (second screens read 39 WPM faster) and age (about 1.5 WPM slower per year) were. For participants aged 35 and over, font did matter (EB Garamond and Montserrat were faster, Avant Garde and Open Sans slower), and the age slope steepened to about 2.6 WPM per year.
- Individual-level results: participants read 14% faster in their fastest font (314 WPM on average) than in their most preferred font (275 WPM), and 35% faster than in their slowest (232 WPM). The authors report a large fastest-versus-slowest effect size (Cohen's d above 0.8) for 76% of participants.

### Replication and criticism

The authors are open about weaknesses: remote crowdsourced testing sacrifices internal validity (uncontrolled viewing distance, lighting and distraction), they call their Cohen's d analysis a rough sanity check given non-independent data, and they say future work should test the question in a controlled environment. The following criticism is the present writer's reading and not the authors' own claim. Comparing each person's fastest and slowest of five fonts, each measured with only four noisy speed readings, will always show a gap even if fonts have no real effect, because the maximum and minimum of noisy numbers differ by chance. The 35% figure is therefore an upper bound on what personalization can deliver, not a forecast, and I saw no retest in the sections read that would show the same person is fastest in the same font again. The population-level null (font does not matter on average) is the more conservative reading, and it agrees with Dyson (2004), who found no clear evidence of typeface differences in legibility. Several authors work at Adobe, a type vendor, and I found no independent replication.

### Implications for LTF

Do not build LTF's typography around a claim that one typeface is fastest. The evidence supports a more modest policy: choose a clear, well-normalized default and let learners change it. Concretely, offer a reading-settings control (font family among a small set, text size, line spacing) that persists per learner, and apply it to lesson text but not to safety-critical instrument widgets whose appearance should stay consistent. Normalize size by x-height, not nominal size, when comparing faces, since the authors show size drives preference. Because preference did not predict speed, pick defaults by piloting task time and quiz scores, not votes. Because readers aged 35 and above read more slowly and showed some font sensitivity, favor generous default sizes for adult beginners, and treat the 35% as an upper bound when specifying requirements.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1145/3502222 (ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction 29(4), published 31 March 2022); read the full article text (jeffhuang.com/papers/Readability_TOCHI22.pdf, abstract, related work, methods, results); web search confirming the author list.

## rello2016make

Rello, L., Pielot, M., & Marcos, M.-C. (2016). Make it big! The effect of font size and line spacing on online readability. In Proceedings of the 2016 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '16), 3637-3648. ACM.

### Core claims

Typical web font sizes of the era (10, 12 and 14 points) were chosen from convention, and prior HCI studies had never tested beyond 14 points, always finding that the biggest tested size won. Rello, Pielot and Marcos extended the range to 26 points. They report that readability, measured by mean fixation duration, improved significantly as font size rose, with comprehension also better at larger sizes, and that the benefit flattened out around 18 to 22 points. Line spacing mattered much less: only the extreme settings tested impaired comprehension. Their recommendation is that text-heavy web pages should use fonts of 18 points or larger with default line spacing.

### Specifics

All details below were read in the full text of the CHI paper (copy at changedyslexia.org).
- Participants: 104 volunteers (61 female), aged 14 to 54 (mean 30.2), mostly university students, recruited through a city's schools and universities. Because of eye-tracker failures, only 93 were analyzed for fixation data.
- Materials: six Wikipedia entries about animals, chosen from 24 candidates for matching length (40 to 60 words in each of the first two paragraphs), structure and layout, set in Arial (the comprehension item shown in the paper is in Spanish, so the texts were probably Spanish). The CSS was altered with a browser plug-in.
- Equipment: a Tobii 1750 eye tracker (17-inch monitor, 1024 by 768) with the participant about 60 cm away.
- Design: six font sizes (10, 12, 14, 18, 22, 26 points) as a repeated measure, and four line spacings (0.8, 1.0, 1.4, 1.8, where 1.0 is the browser default, equal to 120% of the font size) between groups. Participants read at least three paragraphs per article, answered six multiple-choice questions and rated readability and comprehension.
- Measure: mean fixation duration as an objective proxy for processing difficulty. Reading time was deliberately not measured, because speed was not the goal.
- Results: mean fixation duration fell from .255 s at 10 points to .241 (12), .224 (14), .208 (18), .199 (22) and .204 (26), a significant main effect (F(5, 445) = 66.825, p < .001, eta-squared .159). Fixations at 10, 12 and 14 points were significantly longer than at every larger size. Comprehension was significantly worse at 10 and 12 points than at 18 points. Subjective readability was highest at 18 points. The number of fixations did not explain the effect.
- Line spacing: no significant effect on fixation duration (F(3, 89) = 0.064, p = .978), but the 0.8 spacing gave significantly lower comprehension than 1.0, 1.4 and 1.8, and subjective comprehension was lower at 1.8 than at 1.0.

### Replication and criticism

This is a single experiment, in one language and with one typeface (Arial), but with a relatively large sample for eye-tracking. The authors report replicating the design with 28 people with dyslexia, with similar font-size effects. Limits they state: only the first three paragraphs were read, line length was fixed, and one font was tested. Further caveats are the present writer's observations. The "points" in the paper are relative to a 17-inch, 1024 by 768 monitor at 60 cm, where an 18-point setting is not the same visual size as 18 points today on a high-density laptop or a phone, so the numeric recommendation does not transfer unchanged; Legge and Bigelow's angular framing is the safer lens. Fixation duration is an indirect proxy for readability. The tested ranges also fix the character-per-line count: larger type on a fixed column gives fewer characters per line, so size and line length are confounded, which Dyson (2004) cautions about.

### Implications for LTF

This is the main empirical support for setting body text larger than the long-standing web defaults. Do not shrink lesson text to fit more on a screen: the evidence says small text costs comprehension. Express size in rem and test the x-height angle at a realistic distance, because a learner reading a second screen beside a simulator sits farther away than the study's 60 cm. For fly mode, size up further. Keep line spacing near the browser default and avoid very tight leading (0.8 hurt comprehension) or very loose leading. Because a larger size shortens the line in characters, let the lesson column grow with the type.

## w3c2026cssfonts

World Wide Web Consortium (2026). CSS Fonts Module Level 4. W3C Working Draft, 12 November 2026. Editor: Chris Lilley. https://www.w3.org/TR/css-fonts-4/

### Core claims

This is the technical standard that defines how browsers choose, load and render fonts. It is a standards document, not research evidence: it states what implementations are required or recommended to do, and its value for LTF is as the vocabulary and the reliable feature set for typographic decisions in CSS. It covers font matching, @font-face downloads, font-display loading policies, variable-font axes, optical sizing, font-size-adjust and font-variant properties, including numerals. The page fetched is a Working Draft, not endorsed by W3C, so details may still change, although most of the features discussed are widely implemented.

### Specifics

All of the following was read on the W3C page (fetched in segments, because the full text is very long) and paraphrased.
- Status: W3C Working Draft of 12 November 2026, editor Chris Lilley.
- Generic families: serif, sans-serif, monospace, cursive, fantasy, math and system-ui are universal, and serif, sans-serif and monospace must always map to some font. ui-serif, ui-sans-serif, ui-monospace and ui-rounded are UI-related and may not exist on every platform.
- Weights run from 1 to 1000 with 400 as normal and 700 as bold, and fractional weights are valid for variable fonts. User agents must not synthesize condensed or expanded faces.
- font-size-adjust (section 2.6): takes none or a number called the aspect value. The used size is scaled by the ratio of the specified aspect value to the actual font's, so that a fallback font with a different x-height ratio keeps the same apparent size. It affects used size and ex and ch units, not computed size or em units.
- font-display (section 4.9): auto lets the browser decide. block has a short block period (about 3 seconds recommended) then an indefinite swap period. swap has a very short block (100 ms or less recommended) and an indefinite swap. fallback has a very short block and a short swap period (about 3 seconds), after which the fallback stays for the page's life, and is described as suited to body text. optional uses the web font only if it is available in time for first paint, and the page must not shift when it arrives.
- font-variant-numeric (section 6.7): lining-nums (lnum), oldstyle-nums (onum), proportional-nums (pnum), tabular-nums (tnum), diagonal-fractions (frac), stacked-fractions (afrc), ordinal (ordn) and slashed-zero (zero). The text states that tabular numerals allow columns of numbers to line up.
- font-optical-sizing (section 8.1): auto (default) or none. Under auto the browser may set the opsz axis to a value close to the used font size, and synthesized optical sizing is not allowed. font-variation-settings (section 8.2) gives low-level access to axes by four-letter tags, and the spec recommends the higher-level properties where they exist. Registered axes map to properties: wght to font-weight, wdth to font-width, slnt or ital to font-style, opsz to font-optical-sizing.

### Replication and criticism

A specification cannot be replicated, but two cautions apply. A Working Draft is not final and browser support differs, so every feature should be checked against current support data. And the spec says what a font can do, not what is good for readers: it contains no legibility evidence, and the "about 3 seconds" and "100 ms" figures are recommendations to browsers and authors, not measured human thresholds.

### Implications for LTF

Several decisions follow directly.
- Numerals: any value that changes or aligns (altitude, airspeed, headings, countdowns, scores) should use tabular-nums so digits do not jitter, and consider slashed-zero or a face with a clearly distinct zero for readouts where O and 0 could be confused. Old-style numerals suit running prose and do not belong in instrument widgets.
- Loading: for body text, font-display fallback matches the spec's own guidance, so a learner sees readable text at once. Because fallback fonts have different x-heights, pair this with font-size-adjust or size-adjust metrics so the swap does not reflow the lesson. For a full-screen "fly mode" opened mid-flight, consider optional so nothing shifts.
- Variable fonts and optical sizing: if a variable face with an opsz axis is chosen, leave font-optical-sizing at auto so small labels get sturdier letterforms. A continuous wght axis also makes it possible to tune weight per theme, but that is a design hypothesis to test, not something this spec or this lane's evidence establishes.
- Use a defined fallback stack, and subset with unicode-range (section 4.5, which limits a face to the characters in its range) to stay inside the bundle-size budget.

## mangen2013reading

Mangen, A., Walgermo, B. R., & Bronnick, K. (2013). Reading linear texts on paper versus computer screen: Effects on reading comprehension. International Journal of Educational Research, 58, 61-68.

### Core claims

Mangen and colleagues at the National Centre for Reading Education and Research (University of Stavanger) compared comprehension of two long linear texts, one narrative and one expository, read either on paper or as PDFs on a computer screen. Norwegian tenth graders who read on paper scored significantly higher on the reading comprehension test than those who read the same texts on screen, even after controlling for their reading ability, vocabulary and sex. The authors explain this with navigation and spatial memory: paper offers fixed, tangible spatial cues to where information sits in the text, whereas scrolling on a screen gives an unstable, less tangible sense of the text as a whole. 

### Specifics

All details were read in the full text of the paper (PDF at educacion.udd.cl).
- Participants: 72 tenth graders (43% female), aged 15 or 16, from two urban schools in Norway, a homogeneous middle-class sample.
- Pretests: reading comprehension (one narrative and one expository text, 1,400 to 1,600 words each), a word-chain test of word reading and a 24-item synonym vocabulary test.
- Main survey, four weeks later: a new comprehension test with one narrative and one expository text (1,400 to 2,000 words across the two texts). Students were randomized to read on screen as PDFs, or on A4 paper. Both groups answered the questions on screen, within a one-hour limit. The students could look back at the text while answering, and those on screen could scroll and switch between the PDF and the questions.
- Display and print: 15-inch LCD monitors at 1280 by 1024 pixels and 60 Hz, with the PDF in Adobe Reader; the paper version used black 14 point Times New Roman at 100% scale.
- Analysis: a sequential multiple regression, entering vocabulary, word reading and the pretest first, then sex, then a dummy variable for reading modality. Adding modality increased explained variance by 4% (p = .025), with total explained variance 42% and a standardized coefficient for modality of .216 (p = .025). 
- Genre: contrary to the authors' prediction, there was no significant difference in the effect of medium between the narrative and expository texts (F(1,70) = .142, p = .707).
- Table 1 of the paper shows 25 students in the paper group and 47 in the computer group, with pretest scores that did not differ.

### Replication and criticism

This is a single randomized study with a fairly small sample, which gave a modest effect (a 4% increment in explained variance). The group sizes of 25 and 47 are unequal despite randomization. The authors acknowledge that they have no data on whether students on screen actually scrolled, so the scrolling explanation is a hypothesis. The finding that the effect did not differ by genre contrasts with the later meta-analysis by Delgado and colleagues (2018), which found the paper advantage for informational texts and mixed texts but not for narrative-only texts, though the meta-analysis authors urged caution because few narrative-only comparisons exist. The meta-analysis, which cites this paper, found a small pooled advantage for paper, so this study is consistent with the overall direction, but the individual study alone cannot establish a general law.

### Implications for LTF

The paper's most useful idea for LTF is not the paper-versus-screen contrast itself but the proposed mechanism: readers build a spatial map of a text, and scrolling weakens it. Several practical consequences follow for lesson pages. Give long lessons a visible structure so that the reader can keep an overview: a persistent section outline, stable headings, and a progress marker that shows where the current section sits in the whole lesson. Keep related content close together so that the learner does not have to remember where a number appeared, for example by repeating the key speeds or a checklist in the place where they are used rather than only in a table earlier on the page. Avoid infinite scroll for lesson content, and prefer chunked sections with consistent positions for recurring elements (objective, steps, check-your-understanding). Make look-back easy, since students in this study could look back at the text while answering: quiz questions on a lesson should let a learner see the relevant passage, for instance with a link that scrolls to and highlights it. Consider a print or PDF summary of each lesson for learners who prefer paper. The study used continuous prose, so it says little about interactive widgets.

## gould1987reading

Gould, J. D., Alfaro, L., Finn, R., Haupt, B., & Minuto, A. (1987). Reading from CRT displays can be as fast as reading from paper. Human Factors, 29(5), 497-517.

### Core claims

By the mid-1980s it was an established finding that people read more slowly from screens than from paper, and many designers took that to be a property of the technology. A team at IBM Research reports six experiments showing that the explanation lies in the image quality of the characters. When a display had paper-like character fonts, dark characters on a light background, anti-aliased (grey-level) characters and relatively high resolution, reading speed equaled that on paper. They say each of these variables probably contributes something, but they could not say how much each contributes. The experiments, together with earlier studies, rule out other general explanations: inherent defects of CRT technology, and personal variables such as age, experience or familiarity with CRTs.

### Specifics

The abstract was confirmed from the Crossref record for the paper (the abstract field returned by api.crossref.org for DOI 10.1177/001872088702900501):
- Six experiments.
- The four variables named: character fonts that resemble print rather than dot matrix, polarity of dark characters on a light background, anti-aliasing (the use of grey levels), and relatively high resolution, with 1000 by 800 given as an example of high resolution.
- Reading rate was the main dependent measure in the earlier work by the same group; the companion conference paper (Why reading was slower from CRT displays than from paper, SIGCHI and Graphics Interface, 1987) summarizes fifteen experiments (from its abstract on graphicsinterface.org).

Other details reach us only through Dyson's 2004 review, which was read in full. She states that Gould and colleagues (1987) found no evidence that eye movements on screen differ from those in print, and that readers tend to sit further from a screen than from a printed page, so that a longer physical line on screen subtends a smaller visual angle. She also describes the earlier Gould and Grischkowsky (1986) experiment, in which 18 experienced computer users proofread magazine excerpts of about 700 words from photographs of screens and print, and found no effect of visual angle over a middle range, with slower reading only at very small and very wide angles.

### Replication and criticism

The paper is a series of laboratory experiments from one group using proofreading tasks and the displays of its day (CRTs with low resolution and dot-matrix style fonts). Its conclusion is that the speed deficit was caused by poor image quality, and later improvements in resolution and rendering are consistent with that account, although direct replication on modern devices is not part of the evidence I read. The limit of the study is that it cannot separate the four variables, which the authors acknowledge. A second limit is the task: proofreading for errors is not comprehension, and the later literature, including the meta-analysis by Delgado and colleagues (2018), found residual differences in comprehension between paper and screens. Dillon's critical review (Ergonomics, 1992), whose abstract I confirmed only in part, concluded that single-variable explanations were not enough, which is a sign that image quality, while important, was not the whole story. Dyson (2004) also cautions that results from 1980s displays have questionable validity for later displays.

### Implications for LTF

Rendering quality was the original cause of the "screens are slower" result, and it is the thing a designer can still affect today in two ways. First, ensure polarity, contrast and sharpness are never the weak link: dark-on-light and light-on-dark themes both need enough contrast that no lesson text relies on thin, faint strokes. Gould's finding (on 1980s CRTs, with dark-on-light as the benchmark polarity, so it does not rank light and dark themes today) that paper-like fonts with anti-aliasing and high resolution reached paper speed suggests that on modern screens the remaining gaps are likelier to come from layout, size and navigation than from the glyphs, which is where LTF should look. Second, the viewing-distance observation matters for fly mode: a screen read from further away needs a larger size to deliver the same visual angle, so design fly-mode text from angular size, as Legge and Bigelow suggest, not from the sizes that worked on a phone. Treat 1980s-era speed numbers as history, not as targets.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1177/001872088702900501 (Human Factors 29(5), 497-517, October 1987, five authors) including its abstract text; graphicsinterface.org page for the 1987 conference paper; Dyson (2004) full text for the secondhand details.

## boyarski1998study

Boyarski, D., Neuwirth, C., Forlizzi, J., & Regli, S. H. (1998). A study of fonts designed for screen display. In Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '98), 87-94. ACM Press.

### Core claims

By 1998 two typefaces, Georgia (a serif) and Verdana (a sans serif), had been designed specifically for reading on screens, in contrast with print faces such as Times New Roman. A Carnegie Mellon team asked whether such fonts actually make people read faster than the older fonts. The accounts of the paper agree that the main answer was no: no reliable differences in reading speed appeared between the new screen fonts and the older ones, between serif and sans serif, or between aliased and anti-aliased rendering. The authors drew the cautious conclusion that fonts in use for the past few years perform about as well as the new screen fonts, and that improvements in reading speed would need more than a change of font.

### Specifics

The paper itself could not be fetched, so the details come from two secondary accounts that I fetched and read, and they do not agree on everything.
- Human Factors International's summary (humanfactors.com/newsletters/screen_fonts) says the participants were faculty, staff and graduate students at Carnegie Mellon University aged roughly 20 to 53, that text was set at 10 points with 13-point leading and an average line length of about 10 words, on a 17-inch screen at 640 by 480 pixels, and that the anti-aliasing comparisons used 16-point text. It says the comparisons were serif versus serif (Georgia and Times New Roman), serif versus sans serif, Verdana versus Verdana Italic, and aliased versus anti-aliased renderings, and that no reliable speed differences appeared in any of them.
- Wallace and colleagues (2022), whose full text I read, describe the study as having 48 university participants reading four fonts (Georgia, Verdana, Verdana Italic and Times) in a lab, measuring reading speed, comprehension and preference, and report that anti-aliasing did not decrease preference for a font at 10 points. They note that 10 point is rarely studied now because monitors have changed.
- A search summary reported that readers preferred Georgia and Verdana to Times, while the HFI page reports no preference data. I could not resolve this without the original paper, so no preference claim is made here.

### Replication and criticism

Later work extended rather than overturned the result. Bernard and colleagues ran several studies on font type and size (in one, eight typefaces at 10, 12 and 14 points, per Dyson 2004), and Wallace and colleagues (2022), with 16 fonts and several hundred crowd-sourced participants, found no single font reliably faster across the population. Dyson (2004) summarizes the typeface literature as having no clear evidence of legibility differences between faces. Sheedy and colleagues (2005), using threshold legibility and not reading speed, did find that Verdana and Arial were the most legible at small sizes. These are different tasks: a null on reading speed for passages at 10 points does not contradict a difference at the threshold of legibility. The study's limits are its single viewing condition (a low-resolution 1998 display), a small participant pool of university staff and students.

### Implications for LTF

Do not spend design effort arguing serif versus sans for body text: the best controlled comparisons find no reliable speed difference, so choose for other reasons (distinct letterforms, a good numeral set, availability in the weights and features LTF needs, and licensing). The lasting lesson of this paper is that screen-optimized proportions (a large x-height, open apertures, generous spacing) are about legibility at small sizes and low resolution, and that is where they matter for LTF: small labels in instrument widgets, table text and captions. For lesson body text at comfortable sizes, the choice of face is a second-order decision compared with size, line length, spacing and contrast.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1145/274644.274658 (CHI '98, pages 87-94, four authors); HFI summary page at humanfactors.com/newsletters/screen_fonts.html; Wallace and colleagues (2022) full text for the related-work description. The original paper's text was not accessible.

## bernard2003effects

Bernard, M., Fernandez, M., Hull, S., & Chaparro, B. (2003). The effects of line length on children and adults' perceived and actual online reading performance. Proceedings of the Human Factors and Ergonomics Society Annual Meeting, 47(11), 1375-1379.

### Core claims

A research team including Bernard (the same group as Shaikh and Chaparro, at Wichita State University) tested whether very long, medium and short lines change how fast and how efficiently children and adults read online, and which they prefer. The result, as reported in an earlier Usability News article (volume 4, issue 2, 2002) and in secondary summaries, is that actual reading performance did not differ across line lengths for either group, but perceptions did. Adults liked the medium line best and found the short line easiest to concentrate on, while rating the longest line as the best amount of scrolling. Children disliked the longest line. Preference and performance come apart, and the null on speed stands against Dyson and Kipping's finding that longer lines can be read faster.

### Specifics

I could not read the 2003 proceedings paper (the publisher page returned a block). The details below come from the Human Factors International write-up of the 2002 Usability News article (humanfactors.com/newsletters/reading_text_online.asp), from a search summary of the paper's abstract, and from Dyson's 2004 review, which describes a related 2002 study.
- Participants: adults aged 18 to 61 (median 29) and children aged 9 to 12. The HFI page does not give sample sizes.
- Line lengths: full, about 132 characters per line, medium about 76 and short about 45. Dyson describes a related adult study (20 participants, passages of about 1,000 words in 12 point Arial, margins varied, efficiency measured with a substitution-word task).
- Performance: reading time and reading efficiency did not differ significantly by line length for adults or for children.
- Adults' perceptions: they found the short line significantly easier to concentrate on than the medium and full lengths (p < .01), none chose the full width as their favorite, most chose medium with short close behind, and they rated the full width as the most optimal for the amount of scrolling.
- Children's perceptions: HFI reports that children least preferred the full width and strongly preferred the short line, with no difference in perceived concentration. A search summary of the abstract describes children as showing no significant difference in perceived efficiency or preference, which conflicts with the HFI account, so the children's preference result is treated as uncertain.
- Related: Shaikh and Chaparro (2005, Wichita State) compared 35, 55, 75 and 95 characters per line with 20 college-age readers and found faster reading at 95, no change in overall satisfaction, and a strong preference for the most extreme line lengths.

### Replication and criticism

This study failed to reproduce the advantage of long lines seen in Dyson and Kipping (1998), and Dyson (2004) argues that the difference may come from the extreme length of the 132-character line and from the larger type (so the line was also physically longer). Both lines of evidence suggest that within a broad middle range, line length has small effects on actual reading speed. Shaikh and Chaparro's follow-up shows faster reading at the longest line, so the null in the 2002 and 2003 papers is not a settled result. The related adult study used a substitution-word task as its efficiency measure and a small sample. The secondary accounts I could read disagree on the children's results, so the details are provisional, and the proceedings paper is only five pages long.

### Implications for LTF

Because preference and performance diverge here, do not decide the lesson text width by asking learners what feels comfortable and do not treat a wide line as an error that costs speed: the data do not show a speed penalty in the medium to long range. The sensible policy is a moderate measure (a number of characters per line that sits between the 45 and 76 of this study and the 55 of Dyson and Haselgrove) for continuous lesson text, chosen because readers said they liked it and it supported better comprehension in one study. Adults found a narrow line easier to concentrate on, so a narrower column in fly mode is a low-risk choice. Do not stretch prose to the full width of a wide desktop display, and do not shrink it so far that learners scroll through a very long page of short lines. Test any width decision on LTF's own lessons instead of copying a number.

## rutter2017web

Rutter, R. (2017). Web Typography: A handbook for designing beautiful and effective responsive typography. Brighton: Ampersand Type.

### Core claims

Rutter, a designer who started the site webtypography.net in 2005 to apply Robert Bringhurst's rules to the web, argues in this book that web typography should be practised by everyone who builds pages, and that good typography is about reading, not decoration. The book is a craft handbook: it combines typographic theory with CSS and OpenType implementation detail. The publisher's blurb opens with the line "Typography is what comes between the author and the reader". Its table of contents (confirmed on book.webtypography.net) moves from setting type to be read (how we read, the em, line length, text size, line spacing, alignment and hyphenation, responsive paragraphs) through typographic detail (symbols, ligatures, hierarchy and scale, numerals and tables, tracking and kerning, vertical rhythm) to choosing and using fonts (how fonts render on screens, web fonts). For this lane the most useful parts are the two excerpts published on A List Apart, on numerals and on tables.

### Specifics

This evidence is craft authority, not experiment. I read the two A List Apart excerpts (October 2017) and the book's contents page, not the full book.
- Numerals: lining numerals sit on the baseline at one height, like capitals. Old-style numerals have a varying rhythm like lowercase. Lining figures draw the eye, so in running text they can feel like shouting, whereas old-style figures sit comfortably in prose. Lining numerals suit all-caps headings and places where readers scan, compare or search for data, such as lists and tables. Georgia ships only with old-style figures, Times New Roman only with lining. The CSS is font-variant-numeric: oldstyle-nums or lining-nums, with the older font-feature-settings tags onum and lnum as a fallback behind @supports.
- Sub and superscripts: use sub and sup elements, and for fonts with purpose-built glyphs the font-variant-position property.
- Tables ("designed to be read, not looked at"): size columns to their content and do not stretch tables full width; remove borders, fills and zebra striping and use alignment and white space instead, citing Tschichold on tables not looking like nets; left-align text columns and right-align numbers, with headings matching their data; when decimal precision varies, align on the decimal point (the CSS Text Level 4 text-align with a string value, with patchy support); use tabular lining numerals for numeric tables with font-variant-numeric: lining-nums tabular-nums, or a monospaced font; pad cells with less space on top than below and reduce line height in cells; on small screens, either use a condensed or a slightly smaller font (not both) with horizontal scrolling, or linearize into stacked blocks.

### Replication and criticism

These are expert recommendations. The tables chapter echoes Tufte and Tschichold and the numerals advice reflects craft convention, not controlled studies. One of its more distinctive claims, that lining figures help scanning and tables, is plausible but the excerpts I read cite no experimental evidence for it. His rule against zebra striping runs against a common practice, and he gives a reasoned argument (banding emphasizes alternate rows) rather than a measurement. Browser support for features such as decimal alignment was patchy when he wrote, and some details of the CSS will have changed since 2017. Treat the work as a source of well-reasoned defaults and vocabulary that LTF should check against its own readers.

### Implications for LTF

The numeral rules are directly usable. Use tabular lining numerals in every table, checklist value, performance figure and live instrument readout, so columns line up and changing values do not jitter; use old-style figures, if at all, only in running lesson prose, and only in a typeface that has them. Right-align numeric columns and align on the decimal point when precision varies, so that reading across a quiz or checklist table is quick. Avoid zebra striping and heavy gridlines in reference tables; separate rows with spacing and, where needed, hairline rules. On a phone, offer horizontal scrolling or a stacked layout for wide data tables. Because LTF's content mixes prose with numbers (speeds, altitudes, frequencies), set the numeral style at the component level, not globally, using font-variant-numeric on the relevant classes.

## santamaria2014web

Santa Maria, J. (2014). On Web Typography. New York: A Book Apart.

### Core claims

Santa Maria, a designer and co-founder of the A Book Apart imprint, wrote a short book on adapting the craft of typography to screens. The chapter excerpt published on A List Apart, "How We Read", argues that reading is a personal and context-dependent experience, so typography must work across the reader's circumstances, which designers cannot control. It separates legibility (text can be decoded) from readability (the reader wants to keep reading), and argues that poor typography can make readers abandon text that is technically legible. The book's publisher description says it covers how typography shapes the way we read and how to adapt the craft's practice for screens, and the contents begin with How We Read and How Type Works.

### Specifics

This is a craft book, and I read the publisher descriptions and the single published excerpt, not the full text.
- Claims about reading in the excerpt (as summarized from the A List Apart page): eyes move in saccades along a line with brief fixations between them, and only a few characters are seen clearly in each fixation. The excerpt says letters carry more identifying features in their upper halves. It says readers recognize words partly by their overall outline, which is the word-shape account. It cites Zuzana Licko for the idea that readers read best what they read most, so unfamiliar typefaces slow reading. It says uppercase words form blocky similar rectangles and are harder to read quickly. It admits that research on reading is only partly settled.
- Advice: design for readers, not for your own habits; type size and spacing choices add up; good typography is noticed least. The excerpt names no studies, speaking only of "many studies".
- Publication facts confirmed through Open Library: first published 2014, A Book Apart, New York, 142 pages in the listed edition.

### Replication and criticism

The word-shape claim is the contested part. Experimental psychologists reviewed the evidence from the old word-shape model and concluded that letters, not outlines, are the unit: Larson's review for Microsoft's typography group (read this session) argues that the supposed evidence for word shape, such as lowercase speed and the word superiority effect, has better explanations in letter recognition and practice, and that the work of Bouma that is often cited does not support the outline account. Sheedy and colleagues (2005) found that individual letters were more legible than words at threshold, which they read as favoring letter-based identification. A reader of the excerpt's comment thread, Kevin Larson, raised the same point and recommended Beier's book on letters (per the fetched summary). The top-half claim is also incomplete, as the same commenter noted. The author's reply was that findings vary and that typography has exceptions. The all-caps claim is better supported by practice effects than by outlines. These are reasons to read the excerpt as craft opinion, partly out of date on the science.

### Implications for LTF

Use the book for its framing, not its science. The distinction between legibility and readability is a useful vocabulary for LTF reviews: a checklist can be legible and still tiring to read. Avoid justifying decisions with the word-shape idea; if an LTF writer proposes all-lowercase labels or distinctive silhouettes because "readers see word shapes", point to the evidence for letter-based recognition. The reading-familiarity argument has some support in the craft tradition and in the practice-effect explanation of why lowercase is read faster, and it favors using a familiar, conventional text face for lessons and saving distinctive faces for headings or branding. Avoid long runs of all caps; use them only for short labels such as annunciator-style warnings, where speed over a few characters matters more than continuous reading.

## brown2011more

Brown, T. (2011). More meaningful typography. A List Apart, issue 327, 3 May 2011. https://alistapart.com/article/more-meaningful-typography/

### Core claims

Brown argues that web layouts should be built from the type outward instead of from boxes inward. Instead of picking arbitrary or easily divisible pixel numbers, a designer should derive measurements from a harmonious system tied to meaningful ratios and to the typeface actually used, and the tool he proposes is the modular scale. He presents this as compatible with grids and responsive design, treats the scale as a source of educated suggestions and not strict rules, and warns that the whole system depends on the chosen typeface being available. It is an essay by a practitioner, a piece of craft authority with no experimental evidence behind it.

### Specifics

All details come from the article page (fetched this session).
- Modular scale: a sequence made by multiplying and dividing a starting number by a ratio. Brown uses the golden mean (1:1.618). From a start of 10, multiplying gives 16.180, 26.179 and 42.358, and dividing gives 6.180, 3.819 and 2.360. A double-stranded scale combines two starting values or two ratios to give more usable numbers. He points to a calculator at modularscale.com.
- Worked example (a page set in Minion Pro with Myriad Pro Condensed): body text 18 px, a main column (measure) of 497.406 px with a line-height of 1.54, a 29.124 px h1, a 190 px h2 at line-height 1, and a 21.178 px intro derived by subtracting scale values ("comment math"). A 15 px sidebar text in Minion Pro Caption is an improvised value outside the scale.
- Advice: begin with the body text size and the typeface and derive the rest; avoid very tight or loose line spacing and tune line-height to text density.
- Responsive design: scales do not preclude grid-based or responsive design. Use smaller scale numbers as grid column widths, build a carefully measured reference layout and convert values to percentages, and avoid widths tied to common viewport sizes.
- Caveats: the measurements depend on the typeface; if the web font fails to load, fallback fonts take over, so he recommends baseline fonts first and then enhancement. He says some values were improvised and writes that modular scales are a tool and not magic.

### Replication and criticism

There is no empirical test of the claim that modular-scale type is more readable or more pleasing. Its justification is the long craft tradition of proportional systems in book design and the practical argument that a limited set of related sizes yields consistency. A reasonable criticism, offered here by the present writer and not drawn from a cited source, is that ratios such as the golden mean produce steep jumps at the extremes (the article's own 190 px headline sits far from the 18 px body) and that real interfaces need sizes that the scale does not generate, which is why Brown himself improvises. The piece predates fluid type techniques such as viewport-relative sizing, which solve a problem the article handles with fixed pixel values converted to percentages. Its warning about web-font failure remains relevant, and it anticipates what the CSS Fonts specification now supports through font-display and size adjustment. The physical-size evidence (Legge and Bigelow; Rello and colleagues) shows that the lower end of any scale must stay above a legibility floor, regardless of how elegant the ratios are.

### Implications for LTF

A modular scale is a good way to define LTF's type tokens: a small number of sizes (for example caption, body, lesson subhead, title) generated from a single ratio, so that every component draws from the same list, which supports the project rule that colors and spacing come from tokens and never raw literals. Choose a gentler ratio than the golden mean for an interface with dense widgets, because steep ratios make the small end too small or the large end too large. Set the base size from the evidence on legibility (the body size must clear the floor established by the reading studies in this lane), then derive the rest. Keep the scale in relative units so that user text-size settings scale everything together. Remember that the scale is a means of consistency, not a proof of legibility, and review each derived size at its smallest rendered use, for example in instrument-widget labels. Define the fallback font stack and metrics first, as Brown advises, so that a failed font load does not break layouts.

## marcotte2009fluid

Marcotte, E. (2009). Fluid grids. A List Apart, issue 279, 3 March 2009. https://alistapart.com/article/fluidgrids/

### Core claims

Marcotte, who later wrote the book Responsive Web Design (Open Library record), described how he built a fluid layout for a client from a fixed visual grid, and argued that designers often rely on a "minimum screen resolution" assumption to justify fixed widths. His method is a single formula, target divided by context equals result, which converts pixel-based comp values into relative ones: ems for type and percentages for widths and margins. The article is a short craft essay from a practitioner, and it is the origin of an approach that lets text sizes and line lengths scale with the reader's settings and the window instead of being locked in pixels.

### Specifics

All details are from the article page (fetched this session).
- Type: divide the target pixel size by the parent's font size to get an em value. For example, 24 px divided by 16 px is 1.5em for the h1, and 14 px divided by 16 px is 0.875em for a list.
- Layout: divide a target width or margin by the containing width to get a percentage. In his example the page is 988 px wide, so a 700 px title becomes 70.85% (700 divided by 988), and a 144 px margin becomes 14.575%. When an element sits inside a new container, that container becomes the new context, so 700 px inside an 844 px entry becomes 82.938%.
- Base: set the body to font normal at 100% so the browser's default size (usually 16 px) is the base, and size children in ems so they scale with user text-size settings.
- Measure: he sets a maximum width of 61.75em (988 divided by 16), which keeps line lengths in check while scaling with text size. This is an early example of expressing the measure in the type's own unit.
- Context for the choice of relative units: pixel-sized fonts were a problem because Internet Explorer largely refused to resize them.
- Example grid: seven columns of 124 px with 20 px gutters on 988 px.
- Stated limits: fixed-size content such as images and Flash is the main unresolved challenge; browsers round sub-pixel percentages differently (Internet Explorer rounds up, so he subtracts a pixel for that browser); each nested container needs its context recalculated; the formulas assume a 16 px default; and vertical rhythm and fluid line-height are not addressed.

### Replication and criticism

No experiment was conducted, but the design was adopted widely, which is a kind of practical validation, and The same author later wrote a book on the subject. The technique's weak point, which commenters raised, is cascading context: when ancestors change the font size, the divisor changes. Modern CSS has since added units and functions (rem, ch, clamp, viewport units) that remove most of the arithmetic, so the method is now of historical and conceptual interest more than of practical use. The central idea, that sizes should be proportional to something the reader controls, has been supported by the reading research in this lane: the Rello and colleagues' results on size and Legge and Bigelow's angular-size account both imply that a fixed pixel size cannot be right for all viewing distances. The article does not claim readability benefits for fluid type, and none are established here.

### Implications for LTF

Adopt the principle, with modern tools. Set body text and spacing in relative units (rem and em) so that a learner's browser or operating-system text setting enlarges everything coherently, and express the lesson measure in characters (the ch unit) or em, so that the column tracks the type size instead of the window. Do not fix text sizes in pixels, because the right physical size depends on viewing distance, which differs between a phone, a laptop and a monitor beside a simulator. Use a clamp-style fluid range for display headings, with a hard floor for body text so that fluidity never pushes it below the legibility threshold. Test the layout at 200% text size and at narrow widths. Keep a single base font size token from which everything derives.
