# Lane 03: Type on screens, research notes

## dyson2004physical

Dyson, M. C. (2004). How physical text layout affects reading from screen. Behaviour & Information Technology, 23(6), 377-393.

### Core claims

Dyson reviews the screen-reading experiments that varied the physical layout of text: line length, columns, window height (lines per screen) and interlinear spacing. She is a typographer and psychologist at the University of Reading, and her central argument is that the literature is small, confounded and immature, so that confident rules copied from print typography are not supported for screens. Her synthesis is that most line-length studies on screen found faster reading with longer lines, and that the number of characters per line, not the physical width of the line or the visual angle, is the variable that best organizes the results. She also stresses that readers' preferences often diverge from their performance, so that the line length people say they like is not necessarily the one they read fastest. The paper ends by calling for studies that examine how characters per line interact with eye movements, scrolling and familiarity with a format.

### Specifics

Dyson separates three quantities that are easily confused: physical line length (or visual angle), characters per line, and type size. Any pairwise comparison holds one constant and varies the other two, so a study can almost never isolate one of them. This is her main methodological tool for reading the literature.

The studies she describes, all confirmed from her review (stu.westga.edu copy of the paper):
- Kolers, Duchnicky and Ferguson (1981) compared 35 and 70 characters per line, achieved by halving character width on a low-resolution display. Twenty participants read about 300-word passages. The narrower characters with more per line were read faster, with fewer total fixations. Duchnicky and Kolers (1983) confirmed this and found a positive correlation between characters per line and reading rate.
- Dyson and Kipping (1998) held type at 10 point Arial with 12 point spacing and varied line length from 25 to 100 characters per line, with 48 participants and passages of about 800 words. The longest line was read faster than the shortest, with no other significant differences. Scrolling time explained part but not all of the gap. A follow-up with 25, 55 and 100 characters per line, with a grey area to the right to control glare, again found 100 faster than 25.
- Bernard and colleagues (2002) compared 45, 76 and 132 characters per line in 12 point Arial with 20 adults and found no difference in reading time or efficiency. Dyson notes that this failed to replicate the earlier result and suggests the 132-character line was extreme and physically long, and that text was centered rather than flush left.
- Dyson and Haselgrove (2001) found that 55 characters per line produced better comprehension than 100, using a comprehension test with several question types, and that the 25-character line fell in between. Participants were trained to read fast as well as normally, and there was no interaction between speed and line length.
- Dyson and Kipping (1997) compared one column of about 80 characters per line with a three-column paged layout of about 25 characters. The single paged column was read fastest, but only for 18 to 24 year olds. Readers preferred three columns.
- Window height studies (Duchnicky and Kolers; Dillon and colleagues 1990, 20 versus 60 lines; Dyson and Kipping 1998 with 15, 25 and 35 lines) mostly found no difference in speed or comprehension, though readers preferred larger windows.
- On leading, Kolers and colleagues and Kruk and Muter (1984) found that double spacing helped reading compared with single spacing on early screens, but Dyson says recent studies of fine spacing do not exist.

She also records the print benchmarks: Tinker's work interpreted as an optimum of 52 characters per line (by Rayner and Pollatsek), and a print guideline of not more than about 70 (Spencer). She offers a mechanism: eye saccades scale in characters, not degrees, so characters per line is the natural metric, and on screens the greater viewing distance shrinks the return-sweep angle.

### Replication and criticism

This is expert synthesis of experiments, not a meta-analysis, and she is candid about the problems: small samples (some studies had 10 to 20 participants), 1980s displays with low resolution, text that was scrolled rather than read in a page, and tasks ranging from proofreading to searching for hidden shapes. She criticizes studies that pooled many "recommended" settings (the "kitchen sink" approach) because they cannot say which setting mattered. She also criticizes the use of paper mock-ups to answer questions about screens. The finding that longer lines were not penalized did not replicate in the Bernard study with 132 characters per line, which Dyson attributes to differences in how the longest line was set. The study set predates responsive layouts, touch screens and high-density displays, so it constrains rather than settles current practice. Dyson also notes that the speed advantage of long lines coexisted with better comprehension for a mid-length line (55 characters) in the 2001 study.

### Implications for LTF

Treat line length as a range, not a number. The evidence supports neither very short lines nor very long ones as clearly better for speed, but comprehension and stated preference tilt toward moderate lines, so lesson prose should sit in a moderate band (a measure in the region of the mid-range lines in these studies) rather than stretching to fill a wide desktop window. Keep lesson text in a single column, and use a narrower column inside the full-screen "fly mode" where a learner glances away from a simulator. Because performance and preference diverge, do not rely on user comments about "feels easy" when evaluating layouts; test completion time and quiz results. Do not copy print rules (such as a hard 66-character line) without checking them against the display context. Because the evidence is old and confounded, treat the line-length cap as a design default to be validated in LTF's own usability tests, and avoid a multi-column lesson layout, since the best-controlled comparison favored a single column for younger readers.

## legge2011does

Legge, G. E., & Bigelow, C. A. (2011). Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography. Journal of Vision, 11(5), 8.

### Core claims

Legge, a vision scientist, and Bigelow, a type designer, argue that print size is constrained by the visual system. Text can be read at maximum speed across a "fluent range" of print sizes spanning about a factor of ten in angular x-height, from roughly 0.2 degrees to 2 degrees, and the print sizes used in books, newspapers and historical type specimens fall largely inside that range. Their conclusion is that properties of human visual processing play a dominant role in constraining the print sizes in common use, while economic, social, technological and artistic factors also matter. This is a rare paper that joins psychophysics with typographic history, so it works as a bridge between lab results and craft practice.

### Specifics

Definitions and conversions confirmed from the paper (PMC3428264):
- Angular size is 57.3 times physical size divided by viewing distance. At a standard reading distance of 40 cm (16 inches), an x-height of 0.2 degrees corresponds to 1.4 mm (4 points) and 2 degrees to 14 mm (40 points).
- Critical print size (CPS) is the size below which reading speed drops sharply. The consensus value is about 0.2 degrees x-height, with individual estimates from about 0.15 to 0.3 degrees. Above about 1 to 3 degrees reading slows again for large print.
- The authors choose x-height as the main size metric rather than point size because point size is a global body measure that most letters do not fill. About 94.5% of running text is lowercase and about 87% of lowercase black area lies in the x-height zone. The x-height fraction of body size varies by typeface: Karow's measurement of 1,049 roman faces ranged from 0.28 to 0.58 with a mean of 0.46. A 12 point Times New Roman has an x-height of about 5.4 points.
- Contemporary print survey: newspaper running text has a mean of 0.23 degrees (1.64 mm), paperback fiction 0.24 degrees, hardcover fiction 0.24 degrees, with ranges from 0.20 to 0.28 degrees. Newspaper x-heights never fell below 1.4 mm or 0.2 degrees.
- Historical type: 15th-century Italian roman averaged 0.30 degrees, 16th-century French text types 0.23 degrees, 18th-century foundry types 0.22 degrees, with a minority (27% and 37% respectively) below the fluent range.
- Screens: mean x-height fractions were 0.42 for hardcovers, 0.43 for paperbacks, 0.48 for newspapers, 0.49 for print faces adapted to screens and 0.52 for screen-designed faces. In the authors' online-news sample (132 ppi, 40 cm), home page body text averaged 0.19 degrees (below CPS) and article text 0.21 degrees (just above).
- Explanations: oculomotor limits are not the main cause, because RSVP (one word at a time) shows the same curve. The better account is the visual span, limited by crowding (Pelli and colleagues; Bouma's law). Below CPS, letter spacing falls within the crowding zone and the span shrinks. Widening spacing did not enlarge the span (Yu and colleagues 2007).

### Replication and criticism

The CPS and fluent-range findings rest on a long line of psychophysics from the Legge laboratory and others, with consistent values across different methods, including RSVP and page reading. This is therefore among the strongest bodies of evidence in the lane. The survey component is descriptive, not experimental, and the "ecological" argument that print sizes cluster in the fluent range is correlational: designers might also be converging on size for reasons of tradition. The authors acknowledge economic and social factors, noting that small subfluent sizes appeared when paper was expensive. The paper's "fluent range" is also defined for readers with normal vision, and the authors point out that low-vision readers have a CPS that is a multiple of their acuity limit. The viewing-distance assumption (40 cm) is a standard that real devices violate.

### Implications for LTF

LTF should specify type sizes by what the eye receives, not by CSS pixels alone. Because phones are held near and desktop monitors far away, the same CSS size subtends different angles; the safe policy is to keep body text well above CPS, so that the x-height lands comfortably within the fluent range on a 24-inch monitor beside a sim as well as on a phone. Choose a body face with a generous x-height fraction (the screen-designed faces in the survey ran higher), and compare faces by x-height fraction, not by nominal size. In fly mode, where the learner glances from a sim monitor at arm's length or more, the viewing distance grows, and text that is fluent on a phone drops toward CPS, so size must scale up with expected distance and with the large-print end of the range when a tablet is propped beside a yoke. Aviation-instrument widgets that show numbers at small sizes should be tested against these thresholds.

## gugerty2004effects

Gugerty, L., Tyrrell, R. A., Aten, T. R., & Edmonds, K. A. (2004). The effects of subpixel addressing on users' performance and preferences during reading-related tasks. ACM Transactions on Applied Perception, 1(2), 81-101.

### Core claims

This is the main academic test of the claim that subpixel text rendering, which Microsoft markets as ClearType, helps people read. The team at Clemson University ran four experiments comparing subpixel-rendered text with aliased (black and white, unsmoothed) text on LCD screens. According to the abstract as relayed by search results, participants preferred the subpixel text and rated it more readable. It also improved the accuracy of lexical decision (deciding whether a briefly flashed string is a word) and the accuracy and speed of sentence comprehension. It did not change word-naming performance or the speed of reading for pleasure. The paper therefore supports a modest but real benefit for the lowest-level processes of word recognition, and no benefit for relaxed extended reading.

### Specifics

Subpixel addressing treats the red, green and blue stripes of an LCD pixel as separately addressable, which roughly triples the horizontal resolution available for drawing letter edges, at the cost of colored fringes.

Two of the experiments are described in detail in posts written by Kevin Larson, a Microsoft reading psychologist, on the Microsoft font blog (learn.microsoft.com archive of "fontblog"). These are a secondhand account by an interested party, so treat the numbers as reported, not as independently confirmed:
- Tachistoscopic lexical decision: 24 readers saw 280 words and 280 pseudowords (for example a real word versus a string such as "tadints"), half rendered with ClearType and half with black and white. Readers were more accurate with ClearType, which Larson reports as a 17% accuracy improvement after correcting for guessing. The task was chosen because ordinary reading of a word takes about a quarter of a second, too short to time directly.
- Sentence comprehension: 46 Clemson students each judged 144 pairs of sentences as belonging together or not. Larson reports that ClearType sentences were read faster by about 5% and answered correctly slightly more often, with accuracy of 91.5% for ClearType trials against 89.8% for black and white (F(1,45) = 6.06, p = .02). The benefit was larger for italic text. The stimuli used Verdana and Verdana Italic, and the screen was 115 dpi with the operating system at its default 96 dpi.

### Replication and criticism

The paper sits between two other lines of work. Later studies found benefits in some tasks (Dillon and colleagues in 2006 found faster extended reading and spreadsheet scanning with ClearType, but with large individual differences). Sheedy and colleagues found, in carefully controlled threshold and reading tests, that ClearType did not beat perceptually tuned grayscale rendering, although observers preferred a moderate setting. A crucial point raised even in comments on Larson's blog is that the comparison group in Gugerty et al. was aliased black and white, not grayscale anti-aliasing, so the gain is relative to the worst baseline and says little about whether subpixel rendering beats ordinary smoothing. The effects are small (a few percent in sentence reading), sample sizes are modest, and the stimuli came from a single typeface. The sponsor relationship (Microsoft interest in the technology) is a reason to read the vendor-blog numbers cautiously. The peer-reviewed abstract is more conservative than the blog posts.

### Implications for LTF

LTF cannot control subpixel rendering, which is decided by the operating system, browser and display. The practical conclusions are about robustness. First, do not design text that only looks acceptable under one rendering mode: use sizes and weights that survive aliased, grayscale and subpixel rendering, since learners will use laptops, tablets and phones with different pipelines. Second, because benefits were largest for the briefest word recognition (glances), type used in fly mode, where a learner takes in a checklist item in a fraction of a second, should favor the clearest, most generous shapes: not thin weights, not tiny sizes. Third, avoid lightweight italic for critical information, since italic was the case in which rendering mattered most. Fourth, do not assume that "smooth" rendering equals legibility: test on the real range of devices, including a low-density desktop monitor placed beside a simulator display.

## sheedy2005text

Sheedy, J. E., Subbaram, M. V., Zimmerman, A. B., & Hayes, J. R. (2005). Text legibility and the letter superiority effect. Human Factors, 47(4), 797-815.

### Core claims

Sheedy and colleagues measured how font design and display parameters change the smallest size at which text can be identified, a threshold legibility approach. Their headline finding is a letter superiority effect: individual lowercase letters were 10% to 20% more legible than lowercase words, meaning words had to be 10% to 20% larger to reach the same threshold. They conclude that individual letters play a large role and word shape a smaller role, if any, in word identification at threshold. This challenges the popular claim that we recognize words by their overall outline. The paper also ranks fonts and rendering modes, and reports an optimal legibility at about 9 pixels (10 points).

### Specifics

All of the following is from the abstract (Europe PMC record of the paper, confirmed this session), unless stated otherwise.
- Method: threshold size, where participants' visual acuity (the inverse of the minimum detection size) was measured for each condition. Stimuli were upper- and lowercase letters and lowercase words, across combinations of 6 fonts, 3 font-smoothing modes, 4 font sizes, 10 pixel heights and 4 stroke widths.
- Pixel height, font, stroke width and font smoothing each had significant main effects on threshold legibility.
- Optimal legibility was attained at 9 pixels (10 points).
- Verdana and Arial were the most legible fonts. Times New Roman and Franklin were the least legible.
- Subpixel rendering (ClearType) improved threshold legibility for some fonts, and in combination with Verdana it was the most legible condition.
- Increased stroke width (bold) improved threshold legibility, but only at the thinnest width tested.

Legge and Bigelow (2011) cite this study when discussing x-height: they report that Verdana, with an x-height fraction of about 0.55, was most legible in Sheedy and colleagues' set and Times New Roman, at about 0.45, least, and suggest that a larger x-height fraction gives more pixels to the lowercase body, a plausible mechanism.

### Replication and criticism

Threshold legibility is not reading. The study measures the smallest size at which text can be identified, so it speaks to small-text conditions, such as labels and tick marks, and not to paragraph reading. The letter-superiority conclusion is in line with the broader reading-psychology literature that rejects the idea of word-shape reading (see the word recognition review by Larson at Microsoft for the history, and Rayner's work in lane 02). A caution: the tested fonts were limited to six, the displays belonged to the early 2000s, and "optimal at 9 pixels" is a property of those displays and tasks. On modern high-density screens, pixel heights are not what limits legibility. It would be a misuse to cite "9 pixels is optimal" as a body-size rule for today's web.

### Implications for LTF

Use this paper chiefly to settle arguments. When someone says words are recognized by their shape and so, for example, all-lowercase labels or distinctive silhouettes matter more than letter forms, the evidence points the other way: letter forms, spacing and clarity matter. That supports choosing a UI typeface for open, distinct letters (clear differences between I, l and 1, and between O and 0, matter for aviation callouts such as altitudes and frequencies). It also supports sans-serif faces with large x-heights for small UI labels, while keeping in mind that this finding is about threshold size. For instrument-style widgets with small captions, test legibility at the actual rendered size on the lowest-resolution screen LTF supports. Do not read this paper as proof that sans-serif is better for long reading: it measured threshold detection, not sustained comprehension.

## dillon2006visual

Dillon, A., Kleinman, L., Choi, G. O., & Bias, R. (2006). Visual search and reading tasks using ClearType and regular displays: two experiments. In Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (CHI '06), 503-511. ACM.

### Core claims

Dillon and colleagues at the University of Texas at Austin School of Information asked whether the speed advantage claimed for ClearType-style subpixel rendering carries over to ordinary office work, not only to the brief word recognition tasks of earlier studies. They ran two experiments, one on extended reading of text and one on scanning spreadsheets for target information. Search-result abstracts and Kevin Larson's summary of the paper agree on the main pattern: performance was faster with ClearType, with no difference in accuracy or in self-reported visual fatigue. The abstract also reports substantial individual differences, from which the authors suggest that ClearType may not be universally beneficial to information workers.

### Specifics

The figures below come from Larson's Microsoft font blog post "ClearType improves the efficiency of typical office tasks" (July 2008), which is a secondhand account written by a member of the team that promoted ClearType, so they are reported as such and not independently confirmed from the paper.
- Extended reading: participants read five screens of 12 point Arial text rendered with ClearType and five screens rendered in black and white. Articles were counterbalanced so that half the participants saw a given article in each rendering. Mean reading time was 9 minutes 9 seconds with ClearType and 9 minutes 39 seconds without, a speed advantage of about 5%, and the advantage held for each of the five screens.
- Spreadsheet scanning: participants answered questions that required them to look through each row of a table (for example, counting books suitable for an age band at a given price). Mean completion time was 4 minutes 40 seconds with ClearType and 5 minutes 4 seconds without, a difference of about 8%.
- Search results summarizing the paper report that the scanning experiment had 26 participants, and that no accuracy or visual-fatigue differences were found in either experiment. A secondary source claims that more than a third of participants were disadvantaged by ClearType, but this figure was not confirmed from the paper and is not used here.

The paper compares ClearType with "regular" rendering, which in this period means aliased black-and-white text, not modern grayscale anti-aliasing.

### Replication and criticism

This study partly agrees with Gugerty and colleagues (faster sentence processing with subpixel rendering) and partly with Sheedy and colleagues, whose later controlled experiments found that preference favored moderate ClearType but that legibility, reading speed and comfort were not improved over grayscale rendering. The disagreement is mostly about the baseline: if the alternative is aliased text, subpixel rendering helps modestly, and if the alternative is good grayscale smoothing, the advantage is hard to find. The two experiments used one typeface and size (12 point Arial for reading), so generalization to other faces, sizes and densities is uncertain. Effects of 5% to 8% are small relative to the individual differences the authors report. Bias, Larson and colleagues later studied why people prefer subpixel text, in a 2010 paper in the Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology, and found that visual acuity, hue sensitivity and personality traits were linked to judgments (summary from the Microsoft Research page). This supports the notion that preference is partly personal and physiological. The vendor connection of some of those authors is another reason to treat the effect sizes with caution.

### Implications for LTF

Two lessons transfer. First, benefits of rendering technology appear in tasks that require scanning many words and numbers, such as tables and checklists, more than in leisurely prose. LTF's checklist, performance-data and quiz-answer lists belong to that class, so their typography should assume rapid scanning: clear row separation, aligned numerals, adequate size. Second, individual differences are large, so there is no single rendering or style that suits everyone. LTF should therefore not fix its text appearance with fragile tricks such as ultra-thin weights that only look right on a particular display, and should offer user control where cheap: text size scaling, a high-contrast mode, and respect for the operating system's font settings. Because a learner may flip between a phone, a laptop and a large monitor, test body and table text across rendering pipelines instead of tuning to a single one.

## delgado2018dont

Delgado, P., Vargas, C., Ackerman, R., & Salmeron, L. (2018). Don't throw away your printed books: A meta-analysis on the effects of reading media on reading comprehension. Educational Research Review, 25, 23-38.

### Core claims

Across 54 studies published or presented between 2000 and 2017, reading comparable text on paper produced slightly better comprehension than reading it on a digital screen. The authors call this the "screen inferiority effect" and describe it as robust but modest and conditional. Three moderators were significant: the paper advantage was larger when reading time was constrained than when it was self-paced, it appeared for informational texts (and mixtures) but not for narrative-only texts, and it grew with publication year, the opposite of what one would expect if people were simply getting used to screens. The authors conclude that nobody should recommend avoiding digital devices, but that the evidence should not be ignored, and that there are predictable conditions in which the two media are equivalent.

### Specifics

All of the following is from the accepted manuscript on the University of Valencia site (uv.es/lasalgon/papers).
- Inclusion rules: readers read individually and silently; the texts were comparable across media in content, structure and images (so hyperlinks and web navigation were excluded); participants read in their daily language and were from a normative population; studies were from 2000 to 2017 and in English. The search found 1,840 records, which yielded 54 studies and 76 media comparisons.
- Design and sample: 38 between-participant studies (56 effect sizes after removing two outliers) and 16 within-participant studies (18 effect sizes), with 171,055 participants in total, of whom the large majority (165,778) came from four large-scale studies. Most comparisons were with undergraduates and used computers.
- Main effect: Hedges' g = -.21 for between-participant designs (95% CI -.28 to -.14), and -.21 for within-participant designs (95% CI -.37 to -.06). Negative values mean paper was better. Heterogeneity was medium-high (I-squared = 72.24 between participants), with a prediction interval of -.56 to .14, so in some populations the effect is trivial or reversed.
- Time frame moderator: g = -.26 with time constraints versus -.09 for self-paced reading.
- Genre moderator: g = -.27 for informational texts, -.30 for mixed texts, and .01 for narrative-only texts.
- Year moderator: the paper advantage grew by about .01 per year since 2000.
- Not significant moderators: educational level, text length, type of device, need for scrolling, and type of comprehension question. Numerically, the effect was larger for computers (g = -.23) than for hand-held devices (-.12, not significant), and larger when scrolling was needed (-.25) than when it was not (-.13).
- Bias checks: no evidence of publication bias by Egger's test, fail-safe N or published-versus-unpublished comparison.

### Replication and criticism

A meta-analysis of experiments is among the strongest forms of evidence, and the authors tested sensitivity (removing outliers, large studies and unpublished work did not change the result). Limitations they state: high unexplained heterogeneity, simplistic use of publication year as a proxy for generation, ten otherwise-eligible studies lost to missing statistics, and the deliberate exclusion of digital affordances such as hypertext and navigation. The comparison is with paper, not with other screen designs, so the paper does not tell us how to set type on screens, and the comprehension tests mostly measured text recall and inference after reading, usually with university students. The narrative-only null is based on few comparisons. The effect is small by usual standards, and an interpretation in terms of a "shallowing" hypothesis (that screens invite shallower processing) remains theoretical, not directly tested here. The authors cite Mangen and colleagues (2013) as one of the individual studies pointing the same way.

### Implications for LTF

The result matters most for the lesson page. LTF teaches informational content (procedures, numbers, relationships) under conditions that fall in the zone where the screen penalty is largest: dense, expository text, often read quickly. Three design consequences follow, none of which require pretending that learners can use paper. First, do not rely on reading alone for the facts that must stick: pair text with active tasks (predict-then-reveal, quiz questions, interactive widgets), which also address the overconfidence the authors discuss. Second, keep time pressure out of reading: in-sim challenges that ask learners to read instructions while flying are a time-constrained condition, so put critical text in short, glanceable units. Third, since scrolling showed a numerically larger penalty (not significant), keep long lessons chunked into short sections with clear progress markers instead of one long scroll. Offer a printable lesson summary or checklist for learners who study better on paper. Finally, do not use this paper to argue for any particular typeface: it is about the medium.
