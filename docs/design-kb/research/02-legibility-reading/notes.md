# Lane 02: Legibility and reading research

Evidence base for how people read type, in rough chronological order of the sources. Where a source could only be confirmed through its abstract or a secondary account, the section says so.

## cattell1886time

Cattell, J. McK. (1886). The time it takes to see and name objects. *Mind*, os-XI(41), 63-65. DOI 10.1093/mind/os-xi.41.63.

### Core claims

This three-page note summarizes reaction-time experiments Cattell ran in Leipzig. He wanted the time ordinary people need to see and name a letter, a word or a colour, using simple apparatus and tasks closer to everyday life than the complicated rigs of the day. The findings: connected material is read much faster than unconnected material, reading rate in a language tracks familiarity with that language, and letters and words are named faster than colours and pictures because naming them is automatic. Its lasting importance is the method. It treats reading as a process with a measurable time cost, the move every later legibility experiment relies on.

### Specifics

Drum experiment. Cattell pasted letters onto a revolving drum and read them aloud as they passed a slit in a screen. Nine people (university teachers and students) took part. With a 1 cm slit, one letter was always in view. Narrowing the slit to 1 mm forced seeing and naming to happen one after the other, which approximated the time to see and name a single letter. Widening the slit let readers start seeing the next letter while naming the last. Four of the nine read faster when five letters were in view but gained nothing from a sixth, three gained nothing from a fifth, and two gained nothing from a fourth. Cattell concluded that while one idea is in the centre of consciousness, two, three or four more can sit in the background.

Connected material. Reading aloud as fast as possible, it took about twice as long to read words with no connection as words that make sentences, and about twice as long to read letters that make no words as letters that make words. Capital and small Latin letters were read at the same rate in his tests.

Foreign languages. Using his own time per word in thousandths of a second as a gauge of familiarity: English 138, French 167, German 250, Italian 327, Latin 434, Greek 484. He suggested this could serve as a quick school test of language familiarity.

Colours and pictures took about the same time as each other and about twice as long as words and letters. Cattell explained the gap by automaticity: for letters and words the link from idea to name has been exercised so often that no choice is needed.

### Replication and criticism

The note is a preliminary summary and points to a fuller account in Wundt's *Philosophische Studien*, volume 2, part 4 (from the note's closing line). The sample is tiny and the apparatus crude. The note does not contain the word-superiority result often credited to Cattell (a briefly shown word recognized as well as a single letter). Huey (1908) and Larson (2005) report that claim from Cattell's longer papers, which I did not read, so the attribution is secondhand here.

The interpretation is contested. Cattell's successors read such data as support for recognition of whole word shapes. Larson's review argues the word-superiority effect comes from familiar letter combinations and that word-shape recognition is no longer a viable model (see larson2005science). The descriptive findings (connected text is faster, familiarity speeds reading) held up; the whole-word interpretation was replaced by parallel letter recognition. The foreign-language figures are single-subject numbers; do not treat them as norms.

### Implications for LTF

1. Familiarity is speed. Reading time falls with practice in a domain. Use one name for each thing ("airspeed indicator" every time) so learners process terms faster, and make fly mode reuse exactly the words lessons taught.
2. Connected text beats lists. Bare lists of items cost more to take in than the same items framed in a sentence or sequence. Put the reason or the order next to each checklist item on lesson pages.
3. Naming cost. Colour-only or icon-only encodings cost more than words. In widgets and status chips, pair colour with a word.
4. A cheap timed task (time to find or name a thing) is a legitimate way to compare two LTF layouts, but only with enough readers and counterbalanced order, which this note did not do.

Some fractions in the OCR were garbled, so only clearly legible figures are used. Huey (1908) and Larson (2005) were read for the secondhand claims.

## huey1908psychology

Huey, E. B. (1908). *The Psychology and Pedagogy of Reading: With a Review of the History of Reading and Writing and of Methods, Texts, and Hygiene in Reading*. New York: Macmillan.

### Core claims

Huey's book is the first large synthesis of laboratory work on reading. Its argument is that reading is an act of perception that can be studied: the eyes move in jerks and see only during pauses, a pause takes in far more than one letter, and print should be arranged to suit those facts. Part I is psychology (the eye, the extent perceived in a pause, perception, inner speech, meaning, rate). Part II is history, Part III pedagogy, Part IV "the hygiene of reading", ending in requirements for printers. Huey says some printing requirements were still uncertain and that "further experimental investigation rather than the present excess of opinion" was needed. That preference for measurement over craft opinion is why later researchers treat the book as a starting point.

### Specifics

Eye movements (Chapter II). Huey describes watching a reader's eyes in a hand mirror and reports that they move line by line in "a succession of quick, short movements", with three to five pauses per line at the length he used. He credits Javal with noting the discontinuity (with Javal's estimate of a pause about every ten letters), describes Lamare's microphone method and Landolt's estimate of 1.55 words per pause, and adapts Ahrens's cup-and-bristle recorder for his own recordings.

Perception (Chapters III and IV). Readers may take in twenty to thirty letters in a pause in sense material but average about ten, so reading cannot be letter by letter. Huey summarizes Cattell and Erdmann and Dodge as arguing for recognition of word wholes, with word length and general form the main cues for practiced readers.

Hygiene (Chapter XXI). These are the earliest typographic specifications drawn from experiments. Type size is "perhaps the most important single factor", with a minimum height of 1.5 mm for short letters. Vertical strokes at least 0.25 mm (Cohn), preferably 0.3 mm (Sack). A rough gauge of at least six or seven letters per running centimeter. On leading the sources conflict: Griffing and Franz found a small gain, Cohn and Sack wanted at least 2.5 mm, Javal thought the space better spent on larger letters. For line length, a consensus favored shorter lines, with a maximum of 90 mm and Huey preferring 60 to 80 mm. His own recordings: a column of 60.5 mm was read at 3.63 words per fixation, while columns of 98 to 121 mm needed about one fixation per two words.

A caution on letterforms: changes should not rest on isolated-letter tests, because "a letter whose legibility in isolation is bad may sometimes contribute most to the legibility of the total word-form".

### Replication and criticism

Much was later refined or overturned. The perceptual-span and word-recognition conclusions were superseded by gaze-contingent experiments (see rayner1998eye and larson2005science). His line-length figures are in millimeters with no control for type size, a confound Tinker (1963) addressed. Wade and Tatler (2009) argue that Huey's loose wording gave Javal credit for measuring saccades, which later writers copied (see wade2009javal). The evidence base is laboratory observation, small-sample recordings and a lot of secondhand opinion from physicians and school hygienists. Read it as history and a source of hypotheses.

### Implications for LTF

1. Reading comfort is a design requirement. Fly mode is used in short glances beside the sim, so size and stroke weight matter more there than on lesson pages.
2. Huey's plea for measurement applies to us: test LTF layouts with real learners instead of defending a style from taste. Test custom numerals or instrument typography inside words and readouts, not on a specimen sheet.
3. His observation that shorter lines let the reader see "what his eye has just passed as well as what is just coming" is a plausible reason for narrow measures in checklists, though modern line-length evidence is more mixed (see dyson2004physical).
4. Treat his numbers as history. Use later sources for sizes and line lengths.

## wade2009javal

Wade, N. J., & Tatler, B. W. (2009). Did Javal measure eye movements during reading? *Journal of Eye Movement Research*, 2(5), 1-7. DOI 10.16910/jemr.2.5.5.

### Core claims

The paper argues that the standard story, that the French ophthalmologist Louis-Émile Javal measured the jerky eye movements of reading in 1879, is wrong. Javal did use the word "saccade" in the context of reading, but he did not measure the movements. The measurements in his laboratory were made by a colleague, Lamare, and reported in passing by Javal. The earliest elegant description of discontinuous eye movements in reading that the authors found was by Hering in the same year. The authors trace the myth to the wording of Huey (1908) and say later authors copied Huey rather than going back to Javal's papers.

This is a lane-relevant correction because the "Javal" lead appears in nearly every history of reading research. The source to cite for the facts is this paper, not the textbooks.

### Specifics

Javal wrote eight papers on reading (1878 to 1879). Only two discussed eye movements in detail. In 1878 he reasoned from afterimages that the gaze glides horizontally along a line, slightly above the centre of the characters, and he believed the eye moved smoothly. In 1879 he wrote that the eye makes no vertical movements while reading, and in a footnote credited Lamare for the observation that the eye makes several saccades per line, about one for every 15 to 18 letters of text (a figure the authors quote from Javal's page 252 and attribute to Lamare's work).

The word "saccade" (in French an ordinary word for a jerk or twitch) first appears in the context of reading in the final 1879 article, in a passage about accommodation changes in short-sighted readers, not in a passage about measurement. The authors say its spread into English owes most to Dodge (1916).

Javal did try to measure eye movements objectively: he examined afterimages, considered attaching a feather to the eye with a smoked drum, and attempted to read the deflection of light from a mirror attached to his eye, without success. Lamare's method was a blunt point on the upper eyelid that triggered a microphone, so each jerk made a click the experimenter could count. Ahrens (1891) tried a cup on the cornea with a bristle writing on a smoked drum, which Delabarre (1898) and Huey (1898, 1900) later used for recordings.

The paper lists recent books and web pages that repeat the myth, including statements that Javal used mirrors to see saccades or that he measured them with a microphone circuit.

### Replication and criticism

This is a historical argument from primary texts, not an experiment, so the question is whether the readings of Javal's papers are accurate. The authors cite earlier papers of their own (Wade and Tatler 2005; Tatler and Wade 2003; Wade, Tatler and Heller 2003) and quote Javal directly with page numbers. I could not check Javal's French originals myself. The paper itself concedes that "myth" might be an overstatement but notes its persistence.

Secondary accounts such as encyclopedia entries had not been corrected at the time the paper was written. Anyone citing Javal for the first measurement of saccades should read this paper first. A fair summary is that Javal deserves credit for the term and for early reasoning about reading, Lamare for the first counting of pauses in his laboratory, and Hering for the earliest clear description.

### Implications for LTF

1. LTF's knowledge base and any "science behind the design" copy should not repeat the Javal myth. If a history sidebar is ever written, credit Lamare and Hering, and say Javal named the movements.
2. The wider lesson is a rule for LTF's own evidence handling: trace a popular claim back to the primary source before using it. The same applies to aviation sources, where a number repeated from a forum must be traced to the manual.
3. For design, the practical consequence of the early work is unchanged: the eye reads in jumps and takes in information only during pauses, so lines of text, step lists and instrument readouts should be arranged so that the next piece of information sits where the next pause will land.

Huey (1908) full text also read to check the passages the authors discuss.

## tinker1963legibility

Tinker, M. A. (1963). *Legibility of Print*. Ames: Iowa State University Press.

### Core claims

Tinker's book is the standard summary of the first half-century of experimental legibility research, mostly his own and Donald Paterson's work at the University of Minnesota. Its method claim is that legibility of continuous print should be judged by speed of reading meaningful text with comprehension checked, supplemented by eye-movement records. Its substantive claim is that most ordinary choices (between common type faces, say) matter little, while a few matter a lot: size of type, line width with leading, all capitals, italics, and contrast between print and ground. Nonoptimal choices combine into a large loss.

### Specifics

Method. Visibility and distance measures give acuity-like thresholds and are, Tinker says, unsatisfactory for choosing size, face, line width or leading. His preferred tests (Chapman-Cook, Tinker) used equivalent forms, controls and comprehension checks, with very large samples: the line-width and leading studies used 1,760 readers for 10-point type, 1,900 for 11, 1,800 for 12, 2,000 for 9, 2,000 for 8 and 1,960 for 6.

Type forms (Chapter 4 summary). Common type faces are equally legible; only a marked difference such as Cloister Black against Scotch Roman showed one. A sans serif face (Kabel Light) was read as fast as ordinary type but not preferred. Italic was read somewhat more slowly (96 per cent preferred lower case), and italic with marginal light or size slowed reading by more than 10 per cent. All capitals greatly retarded reading and took at least one-third more space. Boldface was read at the ordinary rate, but 70 per cent preferred ordinary lower case.

Size. Eye-movement records favored 10-point over 6-point or 14-point. In reader opinion 11-point ranked first, 10 and 12 close behind, 8 and 9 last. Size cannot be judged alone, he says.

Line width and leading (Chapters 6 and 7). Ten-point set solid was equally legible from 17 to 27 picas, and with 2-point leading from 14 to 31 picas. Very short lines needed more and longer fixations. A 43-pica line raised regression frequency by 56.7 per cent against a 19-pica line, which he ascribes to trouble finding the next line start. Leading mattered more for small type than for 12-point, and 8-point with 2-point leading was not read faster than 10-point set solid. He published "safety zones" per size, for example 10-point in 14-pica lines with 1 to 4 points of leading and 19-pica lines with 2 to 4 points.

Contrast (Chapter 9). Black on white was much more legible than white on black, because the latter needed more fixations; three-quarters of readers preferred black on white. Reversal cost the serif face (Scotch Roman) at every size tested, while the sans serif face lost little at 10 and 14 points but did lose at 6 points. Chapter 11 shows nonoptimal choices combine, though not strictly additively.

### Replication and criticism

The evidence is experimental and large, but gathered on printed paper of its period, so size and line-width figures do not carry straight to screens. Dyson (2004) notes that these print findings are largely unreplicated on screens and that characters per line, type size and physical width are confounded in many studies, and she cites Lund (1999) for the criticism that some early legibility research lacks internal validity because of poorly chosen typographic material. Legge and Bigelow (2011) re-expressed Tinker's size data as visual angle and found it consistent with modern work, with the fastest reading near an x-height of about 0.21 degrees.

One passage has not survived. Tinker explains the slowness of all capitals by word shape ("reading by word units") and calls word form distinctive in lower case. Larson (2005), drawing on Paap and colleagues, rejects word shape and says lower case is faster because readers have more practice with it (see larson2005science).

### Implications for LTF

1. Never set running text in all capitals. Keep capitals for short labels (an ident, "FLAPS"), not sentences or warnings a learner must read.
2. Use italic rarely; it costs some speed. Bold costs none.
3. Dark theme and fly mode: reversed print cost reading efficiency, especially at small size and with a serif face. Use light-on-dark at generous size and weight, and test it with learners rather than assuming parity with the light theme.
4. Do not expect type face choice to fix legibility. Spend effort on size, measure, leading and contrast.

## rayner1998eye

Rayner, K. (1998). Eye movements in reading and information processing: 20 years of research. *Psychological Bulletin*, 124(3), 372-422. DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.124.3.372.

### Core claims

This is the standard review of how the eyes behave during reading and other visual tasks, written by the researcher who, with George McConkie, built the modern field. The abstract says the review covers music reading, typing, visual search and scene perception but puts its main emphasis on reading as an example of cognitive processing. Reading topics are (a) the characteristics of eye movements, (b) the perceptual span, (c) integration of information across saccades, (d) eye movement control, and (e) individual differences, including dyslexia. The basic theme, in the abstract's words, is that eye movement data reflect moment-to-moment cognitive processes in the tasks examined.

The practical meaning: reading is not a smooth sweep. It is a series of pauses (fixations) separated by rapid jumps (saccades), information is taken in during pauses, and how long the eyes stay on a word depends on how hard the word is to process.

### Specifics

I could read only the abstract of the 1998 paper this session. The figures below come from Rayner's later summary in the Journal of Eye Movement Research (2009), which states that it draws on his 1998 and 2009 reviews, and from the Clifton et al. (2016) tribute paper.

Fixations and saccades. Average fixation duration is about 200 to 250 ms, with some under 100 ms and some over 500 ms. Saccades average about 7 to 9 letter spaces in alphabetic writing, with some under one letter space and some over 20. Readers make regressions (backward saccades) about 10 to 15 per cent of the time and skip about 25 to 30 per cent of words, short words far more often than long ones. Larson (2005) gives saccade durations of 20 to 35 ms.

Perceptual span. The span extends about 3 to 4 letter spaces to the left of fixation and 14 to 15 to the right (Rayner 2009). Clifton et al. report that the first experiments used the moving window technique (McConkie and Rayner 1975), in which only a set number of characters around the fixated letter are shown and the rest are masked. Smaller windows slowed reading and disturbed eye movements, with shrinking the window on the right hurting more than on the left. The asymmetry reversed for Hebrew and for right-to-left text, so it follows reading direction. The boundary technique (Rayner 1975) changes a word during the saccade that crosses an invisible boundary, and showed that letter information from the next word helps reading.

Individual differences. The abstract names dyslexia as one topic. Rayner (2009) notes that older readers have longer fixations, shorter saccades, and more skipping and regressions.

### Replication and criticism

The core findings have been replicated for decades across languages and methods, and gaze-contingent experiments overturned the idea (Goodman, Hochberg) that reading is mainly top-down guessing, since experienced readers look at most words (Clifton et al. 2016). The open arguments are about mechanism. Clifton et al. describe the debate over whether information from the next word influences the current fixation (parafoveal-on-foveal effects), which models that assume strictly serial attention shifts, such as E-Z Reader, did not predict. The authors say such effects are elusive in experiments and may be partly measurement error. The review is a narrative synthesis by an interested party, not a meta-analysis, and the numbers are averages for skilled adults that shift with text difficulty, reader skill and language.

### Implications for LTF

1. Plan for pauses. With saccades of about 7 to 9 characters and an average of one regression in every seven to ten jumps, LTF text will be re-read when it is hard. Put one idea per sentence and avoid sentences that force a re-read in lessons.
2. The span favors the right. Put the informative word early in a heading or label, so the first pause on it is enough for a glance at fly mode. Short words are skipped, so do not hang meaning on them (for example "not", "no") without emphasis.
3. Word spaces guide targeting (see Larson 2005). Do not break or hyphenate words in narrow columns, and do not set text in letter-spaced capitals.
4. Because eye movements track processing difficulty, unfamiliar aviation text will cost more time and re-reading; do not time-limit a first reading of a briefing.

Memory and Language (clok.uclan.ac.uk/26495). The 1998 full text was not accessible.

## rayner2016much

Rayner, K., Schotter, E. R., Masson, M. E. J., Potter, M. C., & Treiman, R. (2016). So much to read, so little time: How do we read, and can speed reading help? *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 17(1), 4-34. DOI 10.1177/1529100615623267.

### Core claims

This long review for the public asks whether speed reading works. Its answer, from the abstract: there is a trade-off between speed and accuracy, and it is unlikely that readers can double or triple their speed (for example from around 250 to 500-750 words per minute) while understanding as well as at normal speed. If thorough understanding is not the goal, speed reading or skimming gets through text faster with moderate comprehension. The way to keep high comprehension and get through text faster is to practice reading and become a more skilled language user, for example through larger vocabulary, because language skill is at the heart of reading speed.

The paper also serves as a short, authoritative description of what reading involves, and of why gadgets that speed up visual input do not help much. It was the last paper the first author worked on; Clifton et al. (2016) say he proposed it after the 2014 launch of the Spritz app, which claimed reading at up to 1,000 words per minute by showing one word at a time, and that the first draft was submitted days before he died.

### Specifics

I could read the abstract, the APS summary pages and the APS Observer report this session, not the full text. The details below are limited to what those sources state.

Reading rate. Skilled readers already average 200 to 400 words per minute (APS Observer). Eye movements account for no more than about 10 per cent of the time spent reading (APS Observer), so the main bottleneck is not getting visual input onto the retina but recognizing words and combining them into meaning.

Rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP). Many speed-reading products present one word at a time. The APS summary says these programs limit or change functions that depend on vision or aid comprehension. Removing the ability to go back and reread tends to make comprehension worse (APS Observer). Clifton et al. add that Schotter, Tran and Rayner (2014) tested the claim that regressions are harmful and found the opposite: inability to re-read impaired comprehension.

Claims about prodigious readers. Extreme claims fail when tested, and such readers generally already know the topic; without prior knowledge they recall little and struggle with substantive questions (APS Observer). Skimming works when the goal is gist.

Practice and vocabulary. The one reliable route to better reading is reading for comprehension. Broad exposure builds vocabulary and context that help readers anticipate upcoming words.

### Replication and criticism

This is a narrative evidence review of a large literature, not a new experiment, so its strength depends on the studies it summarizes. Its conclusion matches decades of eye-movement work (see rayner1998eye) and is not contested in the sources I read. A commentary by David A. Balota accompanies the paper (named in the APS Observer report). Gains from training are described as modest, and trained speed readers who gained speed generally lost comprehension (APS summary). Because I did not read the full text, I cannot say which individual studies it leans on, and I do not repeat figures I could not confirm.

### Implications for LTF

1. Do not build features that promise faster learning by faster reading. No word-at-a-time flashers, no speed-read modes. For beginners, comprehension, not rate, is the constraint.
2. Allow re-reading. Briefings should stay on screen and be scrollable or revisitable, and quizzes should never remove the lesson text during answering. The review's finding that forced forward-only reading hurts comprehension supports a visible "back" and easy re-reading in step lists.
3. Language skill drives reading speed. For a beginner audience, define terms at first use, keep a glossary within one tap, and reuse the same vocabulary across lessons. That is the lever the review identifies, and it is something LTF controls.
4. Use skimming on purpose. Headings, key terms and short summaries support gist reading, which is how learners will use lesson pages when they return to look something up. Details needed for exact recall (for example a checklist step) belong in short, set-apart lines, not buried in paragraphs.
5. Set honest expectations in marketing copy: LTF should claim clarity, not speed-reading style gains.

## legge2007case

Legge, G. E., Cheung, S.-H., Yu, D., Chung, S. T. L., Lee, H.-W., & Owens, D. P. (2007). The case for the visual span as a sensory bottleneck in reading. *Journal of Vision*, 7(2), article 9. DOI 10.1167/7.2.9.

### Core claims

The paper proposes that reading speed is limited, at the sensory level, by the visual span: the number of letters that can be recognized reliably without moving the eyes. Legge's Minnesota laboratory studied how reading speed varies with print size, contrast and spacing. This paper unites those results under one hypothesis: when a text property is changed (smaller letters, lower contrast, tighter spacing), the visual span shrinks, and reading speed shrinks in step. The authors call it a bottom-up limit, distinct from attention, eye movement control or language processing.

For design, it explains why print size, contrast and letter spacing matter, in terms of one quantity.

### Specifics

Measurement. The visual span is measured with letter-recognition profiles, in which a letter is shown at different positions to the left and right of fixation and the percentage correct is recorded at each position. The size of the span is the area under the profile in bits, with a letter at 100 per cent accuracy worth 4.7 bits. A sample profile spanning 13 letter slots transmits 50.6 bits.

Reading speed was measured with rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP), one word at a time. In one experiment (contrast) the visual span explained between 96 and 99.6 per cent of the variance in reading speed across conditions; in another (character size) between 80.9 and 95.5 per cent.

The key ratio. Across studies, one more letter of visual span corresponds to about a 39 per cent increase in reading speed (slopes of .024 to .036 log words per minute per bit, mean .030).

Crowding. The authors give Bouma's finding that crowding by neighboring letters reduces the functional visual field by a factor of 4. Applied to an acuity-based upper bound of 36 letters, that gives about 9 letters, close to what is measured under good viewing, and they state that crowding likely has a major influence on span size.

Other numbers the paper cites: RSVP averaged 1,171 words per minute against 303 for static text (Rubin and Turano 1992); in peripheral vision maximum speed fell from about 862 words per minute centrally to 143 at 20 degrees eccentricity (Chung et al. 1998); the critical print size is about 0.2 degrees, roughly three times the acuity limit; and critical contrast is about 5 to 10 per cent. A follow-up by Kwon and Legge (2012) found visual span accounted for 92 per cent of the variance in central-vision reading speed.

Legge's book *Psychophysics of Reading in Normal and Low Vision* (Lawrence Erlbaum, 2007; chapters on vision and reading, measuring reading speed, visual mechanisms, displaying text, and the MNREAD chart) is the broader reference. This paper cites its chapter 3 for the three sensory limits on the span and for a review saying word shape is less important than letter recognition. I did not read the book.

### Replication and criticism

The evidence is experimental and consistent across the paper's experiments, but the argument is correlational: manipulations that shrink the span also slow reading, which does not prove the span is the cause. Kwon and Legge say plainly that their results are "consistent with" the span limiting speed, not proof of it. The result also depends on RSVP, where eye movements are removed, and RSVP speeds are far higher than normal reading speeds (1,171 against 303). Rayner and colleagues emphasize that in ordinary reading, language processing, not vision, is the bottleneck (see rayner2016much). The two views are compatible: vision sets a ceiling and language skill often sits below it.

### Implications for LTF

1. Letter size, contrast and spacing all feed one quantity, so they trade off. If fly mode has to be small or dim, spacing must increase; a bigger or higher-contrast setting buys span, and span buys speed.
2. Design for the limit, not the average. Fly mode may be read from further away, in glare or at night, and by older readers. Set a high floor for size and contrast there, not just the minimum that passes an audit.
3. Sensory span is probably a smaller limit than vocabulary for beginners, so text size alone will not fix hard lessons; combine with plain language (see rayner2016much).

## legge2011print

Legge, G. E., & Bigelow, C. A. (2011). Does print size matter for reading? A review of findings from vision science and typography. *Journal of Vision*, 11(5), article 8. DOI 10.1167/11.5.8.

### Core claims

The paper joins two traditions that rarely talk to each other: vision science, which studies reading speed against angular print size, and typography, which sets type in points. The authors argue that human vision defines a "fluent range" of print sizes, the range over which text can be read at maximum speed, and that the distribution of print sizes in real books, newspapers and type specimens falls inside that range. The range spans a factor of 10 in angular x-height, from about 0.2 to 2 degrees. At a 40 cm reading distance that is an x-height of 1.4 mm (4 points) to 14 mm (40 points). They conclude that properties of vision constrain print size choices, alongside economics, technology and craft.

### Specifics

Definitions. The critical print size is the smallest size at which reading speed is still at its maximum. For normally sighted readers the consensus is about 0.2 degrees of x-height, with individual values mostly from 0.15 to 0.3 degrees. Below it, speed falls sharply; above the fluent range, speed declines more gradually, beginning around 1 to 3 degrees. The authors propose x-height as the shared unit, since body size in points says little about apparent size.

Tinker's data. Tinker's 1963 sizes, converted to angular x-height, fit a critical print size near 0.2 degrees. His fastest reading was at x-height 0.21 degrees (1.5 mm, 11-point body), slower at 0.19 and 0.15.

Modern survey. The authors measured 360 US newspaper front pages, 200 hardcover novels and 100 paperbacks published since 2000. Running text averaged 0.24 degrees for hardcovers, 0.24 for paperbacks and 0.23 for newspapers, with ranges from 0.20 to 0.28 degrees. Historical text sizes had similar means (0.30 degrees for 15th-century Italian roman, 0.23 for 16th-century French, 0.22 for 18th-century foundry type), although 27 per cent of the 16th-century French types and 37 per cent of the 18th-century types fell below 0.2 degrees, mostly used for notes and reference material.

Spacing, crowding and the visual span. The visual span (see legge2007case) correlates highly with reading speed across size, contrast, spacing and eccentricity. Crowding is the main limit on span, and Pelli and colleagues found the limiting factor is letter spacing, not letter size. Below the critical print size, spacing falls inside the crowding limit, the span shrinks and reading slows. Extra-wide spacing does not enlarge the span (Yu et al. 2007).

Screens. Online news headlines were much smaller than print headlines (home page 0.31 degrees, article headlines 0.37, against a print mean of 1.71), and text on home pages averaged 0.19 degrees, below the 0.2 threshold. Mean x-height fraction rises from hardcovers (0.42) to newspapers (0.48) and screen-designed faces (0.52). Sheedy et al. (2005) found Verdana (x-height fraction 0.55) most legible and Times New Roman (0.45) least.

### Replication and criticism

This is a review plus survey. The reading-speed evidence for the fluent range comes from a few studies with few subjects each (4 to 16), and the causal claim is hard to prove; the authors say they see "striking parallels" rather than proof. The surveys are modest and partly convenience samples, the 40 cm viewing distance is an assumption (a study they cite found preferred distances near 50 cm for screens and 36 cm for books), and historical specimens may not reflect use.

### Implications for LTF

1. Set sizes by x-height at the real viewing distance, not by nominal pixel or point size. Fly mode on a second monitor or tablet beside the sim is likely further than 40 cm, so its size must be larger than a normal page for the same angular size. Treat 0.2 degrees as a floor, not a target.
2. The authors found web home-page text below the threshold. Do not copy that in lessons or fly mode.
3. Prefer faces with a generous x-height for small text and instrument labels; compare candidates at equal x-height, not equal font-size.
4. When shrinking text, add spacing, not only size reduction. Crowding sets the limit, and tracking can protect the span.

## pelli2008uncrowded

Pelli, D. G., & Tillman, K. A. (2008). The uncrowded window of object recognition. *Nature Neuroscience*, 11(10), 1129-1135. DOI 10.1038/nn.2187.

### Core claims

The review argues that vision is usually limited by how close objects are to each other, not by how big they are. The visual system recognizes an object by detecting its features and then combining them. "Crowding" happens when objects are too close together, and features from several objects are combined into a jumbled percept. Surveying studies of crowding in grating discrimination, letter and face recognition, visual search, selective attention and reading, the authors find one universal principle, the Bouma law: the critical spacing needed to prevent crowding is the same for all kinds of objects, though the effect is weaker between dissimilar objects. Critical spacing in the visual field is proportional to the object's distance from fixation, and the region where spacing exceeds the critical value is the "uncrowded window". Observers cannot recognize objects outside it, and its size limits the speed of reading and search (all from the abstract).

### Specifics

Definitions. Critical spacing is the center-to-center distance at which a neighbor stops interfering with identifying the target. Bouma (1970) observed that for small letters it is roughly half the eccentricity (distance from the point of fixation). The generalized Bouma law says an object is crowded when the ratio of its spacing to the observer's critical spacing at that location is below one.

Properties. Critical spacing is independent of object size (halving letter size does not change it), the same for letters, gratings, animals and furniture, and varies with position and direction in the visual field. Crowding is distinguished from ordinary overlap masking: crowding impairs identification, counting and localization but not detection, and never makes the target vanish, while masking by overlapping objects can.

Reading. The authors model reading rate as the fixation rate times the characters taken in per fixation, with fixations about four per second, and propose that the span of characters inside the uncrowded window predicts reading speed for adults and children with no free parameters. Dyslexic readers had smaller spans and read at less than half of their span-predicted speed. Reading speed stays roughly constant across a 6:1 range of text size but drops sharply once letter spacing falls below a critical value in degrees, whatever the letter size. Example spans: 3 characters for "row", 4 for "crow", 5 for "crowd" and 9 for "uncrowded". End letters of words are less crowded than interior letters, giving them a larger uncrowded window.

Typography. Because spacing and not size sets the limit, tracking (adding letter spacing) can preserve legibility as text gets smaller, which typographers do in practice.

### Replication and criticism

Bouma's proportion has been reproduced across many studies, which is the review's point, though critical spacing varies with direction and observer. The review is by principal contributors to the field, so it presents their synthesis. What I could not confirm this session is how rival accounts of crowding mechanism (for example different pooling or substitution models) are treated, and the reading-speed model with a fixed four-per-second rate is a simplification of eye-movement data (see rayner1998eye). Legge et al. (2007) agree that crowding limits the visual span but treat it as one of three sensory factors (see legge2007case). An erratum to the paper exists (Nature Neuroscience, December 2008); I did not read it.

Larson (2005) notes that the Bouma (1973) paper most often cited for "bouma shapes" shows that end letters are recognized better than interior letters away from fixation, not that words are recognized by outline (see larson2005science).

### Implications for LTF

1. Letter spacing matters more than size at small sizes. For small labels and instrument legends, use looser tracking or a wider face rather than only increasing font size, and avoid tight negative tracking in headings.
2. Dense instrument panels crowd. In a gauge widget, scale numerals, tick labels and needles close to each other can crowd out the number the learner needs. Increase clearance between numerals and neighbors, and between a readout and anything beside it.
3. Peripheral reading is crowded. Fly mode will often be read out of the corner of the eye, where critical spacing is large. Use sparse layouts, one number per element, large gaps, and avoid strings of similar short items.

## larson2005science

Larson, K. (2005). The science of word recognition. Microsoft Typography. https://learn.microsoft.com/typography/develop/word-recognition

### Core claims

Kevin Larson, a cognitive psychologist on the Microsoft ClearType team, wrote this essay to answer typographers who insisted that readers recognize words by the outline of the word shape, sometimes called the "bouma". He reviews why psychologists moved from a word shape model to a model in which the letters in a word are recognized in parallel and the letter information is then used to recognize the word. His conclusion: "Word shape is no longer a viable model of word recognition", and the legibility of a typeface should not be judged by its ability to produce a good bouma shape. It began as a talk at ATypI in Vancouver in 2003.

### Specifics

Three models: word shape (oldest, from Cattell 1886), serial letter recognition (Gough 1972), and parallel letter recognition, which Larson says most psychologists accept.

Evidence offered for word shape, then answered. (1) Word superiority: letters are recognized better in words than alone (Reicher 1969). McClelland and Johnson (1977) found the same advantage for pronounceable pseudowords like "mave", which have no familiar shape, so the cause is regular letter combinations. (2) Lowercase is read 5 to 10 per cent faster than uppercase (Smith 1969; Fisher 1975). Larson calls this "the weakest evidence" and attributes it to practice, noting that reading speed in uppercase, and even mirror-reversed text, rises with practice (Kolers and Perkins 1975). (3) Proofreading: misspellings that keep the word shape were missed more often (13 per cent against 7, Haber and Schindler 1981), but Paap, Newsome and Noel (1984) separated word shape from letter shape and found the effect came from letter shape (for "than": "tnan", different shape, same letter shape, 19 per cent missed; "tdan", same shape, different letter shape, 8 per cent). (4) Alternating case slows reading, but Adams (1979) found that words and pseudowords were equally hurt, so it is not about word shape.

Eye-movement evidence for parallel letter recognition. In moving window studies (McConkie and Rayner 1975) with 3 letters visible the rate was 207 words per minute, with 9 letters 308, and with 15 letters 340, as fast as with no window; showing the whole word did not help beyond the letter count. In boundary studies (Rayner 1975) the changed word "chart" was fixated for 210 ms when intact, 240 ms in a look-alike with shared letters ("chovt"), 280 ms for shared letters with a different shape ("chyft"), and 300 ms with matching shape and no shared letters ("ebovf"). Capitalizing "CHART" removed shape information but gave the same fixation times as the control. Larson notes that letters in common played the larger role but that shape together with shared letters still helped.

Bouma. Bouma (1973) tested recognition of first and last letters of words and letter strings away from fixation, finding that letters to the right of fixation are named better, and end letters better than first letters.

### Replication and criticism

This is an expert essay, not a study. Its strength is that every claim traces to named experiments, and gaze-contingent window results of the kind it describes are standard in Rayner's reviews (see rayner1998eye). Beier and Larson (2010) cite Pelli and Tillman as finding letter-by-letter recognition the strongest of three operations. Weaknesses: it is not peer reviewed, it is written by a company researcher, and it leaves one door open (the boundary results still show shape helping a little). It lists Rayner (1998) as appearing in "Psychological Review" when the article is in Psychological Bulletin, a minor error to correct when citing. Tinker (1963) used the word-shape explanation for the case against all capitals, and this essay is the main correction (see tinker1963legibility). Go to the cited papers for any figure that matters.

### Implications for LTF

1. Do not justify design decisions by word shape. Arguments such as "capitals look like boxes, so lower case reads better" or "avoid all caps because of the loss of word shape" should be replaced with "avoid long runs of caps because readers are less practiced and they take more space".
2. Judge a face on letter distinctness (see beier2010design): can learners tell I, l and 1, O and 0, in a readout?
3. Mixed case in running text because readers are practiced with it; but short capital labels read by letter, so give them room.
4. Because word spaces guide the next saccade (Pollatsek and Rayner 1982, cited here), do not remove spaces or squeeze word gaps in fly mode.

## arditi2005serifs

Arditi, A., & Cho, J. (2005). Serifs and font legibility. *Vision Research*, 45(23), 2926-2933. DOI 10.1016/j.visres.2005.06.013.

### Core claims

Do serifs make letters easier to read? The usual answers are opinion: some typographers say serifs help the eye along the line, others that sans serif is cleaner. Arditi and Cho tested it directly by building lowercase fonts that differ only in serif size. Their abstract reports that fonts with serifs at 5 per cent of cap height were slightly more legible than sans serif in size-threshold tests, but that the extra inter-letter spacing that serifs themselves impose predicts more improvement than was observed, and RSVP and continuous reading speeds showed no effect of serifs at all. Overall: "our data exhibited no difference in legibility between typefaces that differ only in the presence or absence of serifs" (abstract). Where serifs help at all, they may do so by adding space between letters when text is small or distant.

### Specifics

Stimuli. Nine fonts were generated with parametric software, varying only serif size (0, 5 or 10 per cent of cap height) and inter-letter spacing (0, 10 or 40 per cent of cap height). Stroke thickness was 10 per cent of cap height and x-height 55 per cent. Spacing was added at the letter edges, so glyph shapes stayed the same across conditions. This avoids the confounds of comparing real fonts, which differ in many ways at once.

Participants. Four normally sighted observers and two with age-related maculopathy (acuity of 1.0 and 0.6 logMAR).

Experiment 1, size thresholds. Random five-letter lowercase strings were shown on a monitor, and a staircase found the letter size at 68.6 per cent correct. Spacing had a strong effect (F[2,8] = 143.9), with thresholds falling as spacing grew, a crowding effect. Serif size had a smaller, systematic effect (F[2,8] = 10.1, p = 0.006), and the 5 per cent serif usually gave the lowest threshold. Vision status was not significant.

Experiment 2, RSVP. Sentences from the MNREAD set (56 characters) were presented word by word using the three fonts with 10 per cent spacing. No systematic serif effect and a non-significant ANOVA.

Experiment 3, continuous reading on paper. Three scrambled passages of about 400 words at roughly ninth-grade difficulty, printed in 18-point type and read aloud (low-vision readers used magnifiers). No systematic serif effect.

The spacing arithmetic. Fourteen of 26 lowercase letters carry baseline serifs, so serifs add on average 2.69 per cent of cap height of spacing for the 5 per cent serif and 5.38 per cent for the 10 per cent serif. Using the measured slope of threshold against spacing, spacing alone would predict threshold reductions of 6.88 and 13.77 arc minutes. The observed reductions were much smaller, so the authors infer that serifs may slightly interfere at small sizes, offsetting part of the spacing benefit.

### Replication and criticism

The study is a careful controlled experiment but small: four normal and two low-vision observers, a single font design and one language. The authors state that subtle effects cannot be excluded, and that the two low-vision observers do not support firm conclusions about low vision. It sits among prior work that was inconclusive in both directions: they cite McLean (1980) arguing sans serif letters look more alike, and Rubinstein (1988) arguing serifs help and reduce fatigue, as opinion, and Morris et al. (2002), who found RSVP slowing with serif type only at very small sizes. Richardson (2022) reviews the wider literature and reaches a similar conclusion, that there is no difference in legibility between serif and sans serif typefaces on paper or screen (see richardson2022legibility). The spacing finding is consistent with crowding research (see pelli2008uncrowded).

### Implications for LTF

1. Do not pick a typeface by claiming serifs or sans serif are "more legible". The best evidence says the difference is negligible at ordinary sizes. Choose for personality, language support, numeral design and weight range, and test the choice.
2. Spacing is the active ingredient. At small sizes, loosen spacing; a face with built-in generous spacing is a better bet for small gauge labels than adding serifs.
3. Serifs may matter a little at small or distant sizes. For fly mode text seen at a distance, favor open spacing and test with real viewing distances.
4. Whatever is chosen, keep it consistent across lessons and fly mode.

## beier2010design

Beier, S., & Larson, K. (2010). Design improvements for frequently misrecognized letters. *Information Design Journal*, 18(2), 118-137. DOI 10.1075/idj.18.2.03bei.

### Core claims

Most legibility studies compare one typeface with another, which makes it impossible to know which of the many differences (weight, width, proportion, serifs, letterforms) caused a result. Beier and Larson instead built three fonts, each containing several variants of the lowercase letters that readers misidentify most often, and tested the variants against each other inside the same font. Their abstract reports that some variants were more legible than others despite similar size, weight and personality, that narrow letters benefit from being widened, and that x-height characters benefit from using more of the ascending and descending area.

### Specifics

Test material. Prior letter-recognition work finds two groups with high error rates: x-height letters built from mixed straight and curved strokes (e, c, a, s, n, u, o) and narrow letters with a single stem (i, j, l, t, f). Variants were designed to increase differentiation between letters, drawing on Legros and Grant (1912), who measured how far similar letter pairs overlap. They included open and closed apertures on c, e, a and s; a single-storey a; tailless u; broad and narrow forms of j, l, t, f and i; serifs on i; and unfamiliar forms such as a taller a, a descending s, and reduced capitals used as lowercase. The three test fonts were named after legibility researchers (SpencerTest, PykeTest, OvinkTest).

Short-exposure study. The first test showed a single letter for about 43 ms in parafoveal view (2 cm right of fixation at 50 cm), followed by a mask, with 41 participants aged 19 to 34 from two London universities. Letters were shown at about 45 pixels vertical size, and participants named each one.

Distance study. The second test found each participant's threshold distance (4.5 to 9 meters, average about 6 meters) with the letter d, then had them name each letter from that distance. Of 41 participants 7 were disqualified for acuity, leaving 34. Each variant was shown 60 times in the distance study and 63 or 69 times in the short-exposure study. Differences were tested with chi-square, only within a font.

Results (from the conclusion). The authors recommend wide versions of narrow letters: the wide j, l and t performed better than the narrow forms, as did the wide f in one font; only the i showed no clear benefit. Extending letters into the ascending or descending area helped: the ascending a and descending s beat the x-height versions. The single-storey a was less legible than the double-storey a, with many misreadings of o and q. They could not conclude that differentiating n and u helped. The hypothesis that more open c and e apertures help was not confirmed (extremely closed e versions did worse), and the open-aperture a gave no reliable improvement.

### Replication and criticism

The within-font design is a real methodological strength, but there are limits the authors name. Single-letter recognition removes crowding and word context, which may overstate letter-level differences compared with words (they note the choice was deliberate). Effects were reported as error counts and chi-square tests, with Post-hoc comparisons, but the participants were young design and science students and the fonts were test designs, so results may not carry to other faces. The authors warn that wide j and l would create spacing problems, for example overlap with letters to the left, and need kerning. Unfamiliar letterforms performed well in recognition, but whether readers would accept them in running text is untested; Beier and Larson (2013) later found that exposure time, not the commonness of letter shapes, drove familiarity effects on reading speed, and that readers disliked uncommon shapes (abstract of that paper, Crossref). Fox et al. (2007) and Waller (2007), the cross-font studies the authors criticize, offer real-world typefaces but with confounds.

### Implications for LTF

1. Choose an interface or instrument typeface for its distinguishable characters. Test the pairs that matter in flying: 0 and O, 1 and l and I, 5 and S, 8 and B, and letters in runway designators and frequencies. Check them at the smallest size used and in the dark theme.
2. Narrow characters (i, l, t, f, 1) are the weak point, so avoid very condensed faces, and use tabular numerals with open shapes in readouts.
3. Avoid single-storey a in beginner text. Use a face with two-storey a and with open apertures (c, e, s) unless the design has been tested.
4. Do not adopt unusual letterforms for brand reasons in content learners must read quickly.
5. When testing LTF candidates, copy this method: vary one thing within a family and measure confusions, not just preference.
