# Lane 02: Legibility and reading research

Evidence base for how people read type, in rough chronological order of the sources. Where a source could only be confirmed through its abstract or a secondary account, the section says so.

## cattell1886time

Cattell, J. McK. (1886). The time it takes to see and name objects. *Mind*, os-XI(41), 63-65. DOI 10.1093/mind/os-xi.41.63.

### Core claims

This three-page note summarizes a set of reaction-time experiments Cattell ran in Leipzig. Its aim was to measure the time ordinary people need to see and name a letter, a word or a colour, using simple apparatus and tasks closer to everyday life than the complicated reaction-time rigs of the day. The headline findings are that connected material is read much faster than unconnected material, that reading rate in a language tracks familiarity with that language, and that letters and words are named far faster than colours and pictures because naming them is automatic.

For the history of legibility research the note matters less for any one number than for the method. It treats reading as a measurable mental process with a time cost, which is the move that every later legibility experiment relies on (speed of reading as the dependent variable).

### Specifics

Method. In the first experiment Cattell pasted letters onto a revolving drum and read them aloud as they passed a slit in a screen. Nine people (university teachers and students) took part. With a slit 1 cm wide, one letter was always in view, and the readers named letters at a steady pace. Narrowing the slit to 1 mm forced seeing and naming to happen one after the other, and the time then approximated the time to see and name a single letter. Widening the slit let readers begin seeing the next letter while still naming the last. Four of the nine read faster when five letters were in view at once but gained nothing from a sixth, three gained nothing from a fifth, and two gained nothing from a fourth. Cattell took this to show that while one idea is in the centre of consciousness, two, three or four more can sit in the background.

Connected versus unconnected material. Reading aloud as fast as possible, Cattell found it takes about twice as long to read words that have no connection as words that make sentences, and about twice as long to read letters that make no words as letters that do. He argued that with connected material one mental effort can recognize a whole group and one act of will can set the speech movements, so the limit becomes the speed of the speech organs. Capital and small Latin letters were read at the same rate in his tests, while German script letters were slower.

Foreign languages. Cattell used his own time per word, in thousandths of a second, as a gauge of familiarity: English 138, French 167, German 250, Italian 327, Latin 434, Greek 484. He suggested the method could serve as a quick school examination of language familiarity.

Colours and pictures. Seeing and naming colours and pictures took about the same time as each other and about twice as long as for words and letters. He explained the gap by automaticity: for letters and words the link from idea to name has been exercised so often that it needs no choice, while for colours and pictures the name must be chosen.

### Replication and criticism

The note is a preliminary summary, and it points the reader to a fuller account in Wundt's *Philosophische Studien*, volume 2, part 4 (confirmed from the note's closing line). The sample is tiny by modern standards and the apparatus is crude. The note does not contain the word-superiority result often credited to Cattell (that a briefly shown word is recognized as well as a single letter). That claim is reported by Huey (1908) and by Larson (2005) from Cattell's longer papers, which I did not read, so treat the attribution as secondhand here.

The larger interpretive story is contested. Cattell and his successors read the results as support for recognition of whole words. Larson's review of the later literature argues that the word-superiority effect is explained by familiar letter combinations, not word outlines, and that the claim of whole-word shape recognition is not viable (see the larson2005science section). So the data (connected text is read faster, familiarity speeds reading) have held up as a description, while the whole-word-shape interpretation has been replaced by parallel letter recognition models.

The foreign-language figures are single-subject numbers for the writer himself, with a statement that experiments on other people confirmed the pattern. Do not treat 138 or 484 as norms.

### Implications for LTF

1. Familiarity is speed. Cattell's foreign-language gradient is the oldest demonstration that reading time falls with practice in a domain. For LTF this argues for consistent, repeated vocabulary: if "airspeed indicator" is always called that, learners process it faster than if it is sometimes "ASI" and sometimes "speed gauge". Fly mode should reuse exactly the words the lessons taught.
2. Connected text beats lists. Because unconnected items cost roughly double, checklists read as bare lists of items will be slower to take in than the same items framed in a sentence or a flow. In lesson pages, put the reason or the sequence next to each item.
3. Naming cost. Cattell's colour and picture result implies that icon-only or colour-only encodings cost more than words for naming. In instrument widgets and status chips, pair colour with a word.
4. Method lesson for in-house testing. A cheap timed task (time to name or find a thing) is a legitimate way to compare two LTF layouts, but only with enough readers and with order counterbalanced, which this 1886 note did not do.
