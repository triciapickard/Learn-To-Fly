## Norman1988
**Core claims.** Everyday objects fail because designers ignore how people form understanding. The book first appeared in 1988 as The Psychology of Everyday Things (POET) and was revised and expanded in 2013 as The Design of Everyday Things (DOET). Norman's thesis is that good design makes things discoverable (the user can work out what actions are possible and how to perform them) and understandable (the user can tell what it all means and how the product is meant to be used). Bad design shifts blame to users ("human error") when the cause is a design that gave no cues, no constraints and no feedback. The 2013 edition organizes the argument around fundamental principles of interaction: affordances, signifiers, constraints, mappings, feedback and the conceptual model.
**Specifics.** (1) The Seven Stages of Action: form a goal; plan; specify; perform; then perceive the state of the world, interpret it, and compare it with the goal. The first three are the Gulf of Execution side, the last three the Gulf of Evaluation side. A designer asks at each stage whether the user can tell what to do and what happened. (2) Gulf of Execution: the gap between what the user wants to do and what the system allows or shows. Gulf of Evaluation: the effort needed to interpret the system's state against the intention. (3) Three levels of processing introduced in the 2013 edition (building on his Emotional Design, 2004): visceral, behavioral, reflective. (4) Four classes of constraint: physical, cultural, semantic and logical. Forcing functions (interlocks, lock-ins, lockouts) are strong physical constraints. (5) Mapping: the spatial relationship between controls and what they affect; natural mappings use spatial analogy or cultural standards, for example controls arranged like the burners they operate. (6) Feedback must be immediate, informative and planned; too much feedback, or feedback that is poorly prioritized, is as bad as none. (7) Conceptual models: the designer's model, the user's model and the system image (everything the user can see, hear and read about the product). Users build their model only from the system image. (8) Error: slips (right intention, wrong execution) and mistakes (wrong intention). Slips include capture, description and mode errors. Design guidance: make errors easy to detect and reverse, use undo, constraints and sensibility checks, and do not blame the user. (9) Knowledge in the world versus knowledge in the head, with the trade-off between ease of learning and ease of use. (10) The 2013 edition replaces the loose 1988 use of "affordance" with a split: affordances are the relationship between an object's properties and an agent's capabilities; signifiers are the perceivable cues that show where action should take place.
**Quotation.** None given, to avoid misquoting.
**Criticism and status.** The book is a synthesis of cognitive psychology and observation, not an experimental report; evidence for individual claims lies in the work he cites. Norman himself retracted the 1988 usage of affordance after the 1990s misuse of the term. Critics note a bias toward physical products, and recent writers argue that "knowledge in the world" can overload screens. The Gulf model remains a standard teaching framework with wide use in usability evaluation.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Give every widget a mapping check: does the on-screen control move and respond like the real yoke, throttle, trim wheel or flap lever? (2) Audit each screen against the two gulfs: can a beginner see what they can do next, and can they tell what state the lesson, checklist or fly mode is in? (3) Treat the app's system image as the learner's only model: if fly mode looks like study mode, learners will build the wrong model. (4) Design for slips: undo for checklist ticks, progress and quiz answers instead of "are you sure" dialogs. (5) Feedback within a perceptual beat of every tap, proportionate to the action.

## Norman1999
**Core claims.** Norman wrote this short essay in interactions (May/June 1999, vol. 6 no. 3, pp. 38-43) because the term affordance, which he had borrowed from Gibson, was being used in HCI for any visual property of a screen element. He distinguishes real affordances, which are the actual physical possibilities of an object, from perceived affordances, which are what a person believes can be done. On a computer screen, he argues, the real affordances belong to the hardware (the screen can be touched, the mouse can be pressed and moved). A graphical button does not literally afford clicking; what the designer controls is the perceived affordance, and that depends largely on learned conventions and on feedback, not on any inherent property.
**Specifics.** (1) Search results summarizing the paper report his claim that affordances matter less in screen design than cultural conventions do, and his example that a cursor changing to a hand shape is a convention (a signal) rather than an affordance. (2) He also argues that a graphic suggesting an action is symbolic communication that only works if the user knows the convention. (3) He treats conventions as cultural constraints: arbitrary but learned and shared (for example the underlined blue link in the early web, or the shape of a scrollbar), and warns that breaking a convention imposes a learning cost. (4) He recommends that designers talk about perceived affordances, constraints, conventions, feedback and visible cues rather than "affordance" as a catch-all, and that they treat screen design as the design of cues and conventions. (5) He argues that the real/perceived distinction helps identify cases where a design looks as if it affords something it does not (a false perceived affordance). (6) The article is the bridge between POET (1988) and the signifier vocabulary of 2013. Exact page-level quotations were not retrieved this session, so none are given.
**Criticism and status.** Hartson (2003) and McGrenere and Ho (2000) argue the real/perceived dichotomy blurs useful distinctions, for example between the physical ability to act, the information that shows it, and the sensory ability to perceive it. Norman himself later abandoned "perceived affordance" for "signifier" (Norman 2008 in interactions and the 2013 edition of DOET). The essay is opinion, not empirical research, but it is the most cited correction of the term in HCI.
**Use for LTF.** (1) In the book and the codebase, specify signifiers (a visible handle, underline, chevron, shadow, cursor change, label) instead of claiming a component "has affordance". (2) Prefer established web conventions for clickable things: a filled primary button, an underlined link, a chevron for expansion. Put LTF's identity into color, type, motion and illustration, not into inventing new signals for what is tappable. (3) Test with a first-time user: can they tell what is tappable without hovering? Touch screens have no hover, so signifiers must be visible at rest. (4) Keep signifiers valid in both Day and Night themes: an outline or fill difference that disappears in one theme is a broken convention. (5) When a rule is deliberately broken, such as a draggable aircraft symbol, add an explicit cue (grab handle, first-use hint).

## Gibson1979
**Core claims.** James J. Gibson's The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception (1979) proposes that perception is the direct pickup of information from the ambient optic array, without inferential processing of retinal images. His central new concept is the affordance: what the environment offers the animal, what it provides or furnishes, for good or ill. Affordances are relational, defined jointly by the properties of the environment and the capabilities of the animal. A flat, rigid, extended surface affords support to a human walker but not to a water bug in the same way. An affordance exists whether or not it is perceived; it is an invariant combination of environmental properties, and perceiving it needs no mental construction. Gibson first outlined the theory in a 1977 chapter, "The theory of affordances," in Shaw and Bransford's Perceiving, Acting, and Knowing.
**Specifics.** (1) The ecological approach rejects the retinal-image snapshot as the unit of vision in favor of the moving observer in a structured array. (2) Concepts central to the book: the optic array, optic flow (the pattern of motion produced by movement of the observer, with a focus of expansion in the direction of travel), texture gradients and invariants (properties that stay constant over change of viewpoint). (3) Gibson's earlier work includes The Perception of the Visual World (1950), which introduced gradients of texture as the stimulus for depth, and his research in the US Army Air Forces aviation psychology program during the Second World War. The relation of that work to landing aircraft is part of the intellectual background, but the specific details of the wartime studies were not re-verified this session and are not stated here. (4) Examples in the book: a surface that affords walking, a cliff edge that affords falling, a tool that affords grasping and cutting, and the nested structure of affordances (a doorway affords passage for an animal of a given size). (5) Gibson insisted that affordances are neither objective physical properties alone nor subjective phenomenal ones; they cut across the dichotomy.
**Criticism and status.** Ecological psychology remains influential, particularly in motor control and perception-action research, but is contested by representational and computational accounts, which argue that some form of inference is needed. Gibson never meant the term for artifacts with symbolic displays. Norman's HCI adoption (see Norman1988 and Norman1999) departs from Gibson by making affordance depend on perception and mental models; McGrenere and Ho (2000) show how the definitions differ.
**Use for LTF.** (1) The aviation link is direct: flight relies on optic flow, horizon position and texture gradients, so interactive diagrams of landing and attitude should show those invariants instead of only numbers. (2) Think of the learner's affordances relationally: what a screen lets this person with this skill do now. A beginner and a licensed pilot see different possibilities in the same panel. (3) Prefer showing the effect of action over explaining it, in line with direct pickup: let the learner drag pitch and watch the horizon move. (4) Use the term affordance in the book only in Gibson's relational sense, and use signifier for the visible cue.

## Gaver1991
**Core claims.** William W. Gaver's "Technology affordances" (CHI '91, New Orleans, pp. 79-84) argues that designers can use the concept of affordance to think about how technology supports action, and that Norman's use leaves out a distinction between an affordance and the information that specifies it. Affordances are properties of the world defined with respect to people's interaction with it; they exist independently of perception, while perception of them is what design must support. Gaver also argues that the concept applies to multimodal and auditory interfaces, not only visual ones.
**Specifics.** (1) The central four-way table separates the existence of an affordance from the presence of perceptual information for it: perceptible affordance (affordance present, information present, for example a door handle that shows how to pull); hidden affordance (present but no information, so users cannot find it, for example a drawer with no pull); false affordance (information suggests an affordance that does not exist); and correct rejection (neither is present). (2) Complex actions involve nested affordances: affordances grouped in space, such as a scrollbar's arrow, track and thumb inside a window. (3) Sequential affordances: acting on one affordance reveals or creates a new one in time, for example turning a handle then pulling a door; interfaces such as menus use this to reveal options progressively. (4) Gaver draws on Gibson's ecological approach, extending it to what he calls technology affordances, and discusses how information about affordances can be offered across vision and sound; sounds can specify events and actions (a tap on a surface specifies material and force). (5) He gives guidance for design: look for what an interface lets people do and make the possibilities perceptible; identify where the information is missing or misleading. The paper is short and conceptual; it reports no experiment and no sample.
**Criticism and status.** The paper is an early conceptual contribution; Hartson (2003) and McGrenere and Ho (2000) refine and sometimes disagree with the typology. The four-way table has been widely reused in teaching. Gaver later wrote on ambiguity and on auditory icons, which extend the same logic.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Audit every widget with the table: hidden affordances (a drag handle with no cue, a swipe gesture) and false affordances (a card that looks clickable but is not) are defects. (2) Use sequential affordances for multi-step widgets: show the next control only after the first action, so a beginner meets one decision at a time. (3) Use nested affordances in a compound instrument, with clear boundaries between the draggable parts. (4) For fly mode, where the learner's eyes are on the simulator, consider audio cues for acknowledged steps, following Gaver's point that sound can specify an event; keep any sound optional and muted by default.

## Hartson2003
**Core claims.** H. Rex Hartson's paper (Behaviour & Information Technology 22(5), 315-338, DOI 10.1080/01449290310001592587) is a response to Norman's 1999 essay. Crossref records show it as a Virginia Tech concept paper that has been cited several hundred times. He argues that Norman's real versus perceived split is not enough for interaction design and proposes four types of affordance, which correspond to the stages of the user's action cycle: cognitive affordance (design feature that helps users know something; roughly Norman's perceived affordance), physical affordance (feature that helps users do something physically; roughly Norman's real affordance), sensory affordance (feature that helps users sense something, such as legibility, audibility or noticeability), and functional affordance (feature that adds purpose to the physical action, linking it to the work the user wants done).
**Specifics.** (1) Physical affordance has, in Hartson's framing, a mandatory component of utility or purposeful action: it matters only if the physical action helps the user do something useful. (2) Sensory affordances support cognitive and physical ones: a label that cannot be read (low contrast, small size) cannot afford understanding, and a control that cannot be perceived cannot be used. (3) The framework maps onto the interaction cycle: planning (cognitive), translating the plan into action and physical action (physical), assessing the outcome (sensory then cognitive). (4) A single control can be analyzed on all four: a button's label is a cognitive affordance, its size and spacing a physical affordance (relevant to Fitts' law), its contrast and position a sensory affordance, and the task step it advances a functional affordance. (5) Hartson links the concepts to usability evaluation, saying that problems found in testing can be classified by which affordance failed. (6) The paper reports no experiment; it is a conceptual contribution with worked examples.
**Criticism and status.** The paper is conceptual and untested empirically; its categories are used mainly in teaching and in analysis of usability problems. The terminology is not the same as Norman's later signifier vocabulary, and some authors find four categories overlapping. Its main value is as a checklist for review.
**Use for LTF.** (1) A ready review checklist for any control: can it be seen (sensory), understood (cognitive), operated (physical; thumb reach, target size, usable with one hand beside a keyboard in fly mode), and does it advance the learner's goal (functional)? (2) Link to accessibility: contrast and size failures are sensory failures, target-size failures are physical failures; reviewing by these four labels makes issues easier to triage. (3) Use the four names as the shared vocabulary on the book's design-review pages, so critique says which affordance failed. (4) Functional affordance is a reminder to cut controls that look good but do not advance learning.

## McGrenere2000
**Core claims.** Joanna McGrenere and Wayne Ho (Graphics Interface 2000, Montreal, 15-17 May 2000, pp. 179-186) compare Gibson's, Norman's and Gaver's definitions of affordance and propose a clarified, more useful framework for HCI. Their main point is that the three authors use the word differently: Gibson's affordance is an action possibility in the environment, independent of the actor's experience, culture and perception; Norman's (1988) is bound up with the person's perception and mental model; Gaver separates affordance from the information that makes it perceivable.
**Specifics.** (1) The paper argues that affordance itself should be kept separate from the information that specifies it; much confusion arises when a design is described as having "good affordances" when the real issue is that information about an existing affordance is poor. (2) It proposes that affordances come in degrees, with some more strongly offered than others, and that design can be evaluated by how strongly an affordance is offered and how detectable it is. (3) It stresses the difference between usefulness (does the tool afford the actions the user needs?) and usability (can the user find and perform them?), so design must attend to both the set of affordances and how they are conveyed. (4) It applies the framework to the analysis of a software tool, using a drawing-style application to show how a feature may be supported in the code yet hidden from the user. (5) The authors say the concept is easily over-extended, and recommend using it for analysis of what a design permits, and information for what it shows. Exact numbered claims and quotations from the paper were not retrieved, so none are given.
**Criticism and status.** Conceptual; cited as a basis for later clarifications such as Hartson's four types and Norman's signifier. It does not provide an empirical test of the degrees-of-affordance idea.
**Use for LTF.** (1) When a user test shows confusion, diagnose in two steps: is the action possible, and is there information showing it? That avoids the vague "it is not intuitive". (2) Rank controls by strength of affordance: primary actions (continue lesson, tick checklist item) must be strongest and most detectable; rare actions can be quieter. (3) Keep the word "intuitive" out of the book's vocabulary; use detectability, discoverability and learnability. (4) Treat usefulness and usability as separate review questions for each widget: does the widget let the learner do what the lesson requires, and can they find how?

## Carroll1987
**Core claims.** John M. Carroll and Mary Beth Rosson's chapter "Paradox of the active user" (in Carroll, ed., Interfacing Thought, MIT Press, 1987, pp. 80-111) describes a bind they observed in studies of people learning office software at IBM. Learners are active: they want to get on with real work immediately, and they skip or only skim manuals and tutorials. Yet that very activity produces errors and detours that make learning slower. The paradox has two parts. The production bias: people are motivated by the task they came to do, so they under-invest in learning even when a small investment would pay off over time. The assimilation paradox: people interpret new systems through what they already know, which helps them start but also leads them to misread the new system.
**Specifics.** (1) The authors' proposed response is to design for the active user instead of fighting the behavior: support learning by doing, make the system forgiving, and shrink the manual. (2) The training-wheels idea (Carroll and Carrithers, 1984, in Communications of the ACM; exact citation details not re-verified this session) is the best known application: a word-processor mode that blocked advanced functions while novices were getting started, so that they could not wander into trouble. The reported finding was that learners with the restricted system got through the early material more efficiently than learners with the full system; exact figures are not confirmed and are not given here. (3) Carroll's later minimalist instruction (with others at IBM) follows from the same idea: get learners working on real tasks quickly, keep reading to a minimum, include error recognition and recovery, and let the material reference the real system. (4) The chapter draws on thinking-aloud and observational studies of beginners with word processors at IBM in the early 1980s. Sample sizes were small, as in most qualitative work of the era. (5) Nielsen Norman Group has since restated the paradox as a standing finding of usability testing: users start without reading instructions.
**Criticism and status.** The core observation has held up in usability testing for decades, but the evidence base is observational and from office software. Modern help systems and short onboarding tours change the surface, not the bias. The training-wheels result is from a controlled comparison but with a limited number of learners; it should be cited as supporting evidence, not a universal law.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Assume the learner will skip the introduction; the first screen of each lesson should be a task, with the minimum needed to start it. (2) Training wheels for widgets: show a simplified instrument first (for example an airspeed indicator with the green arc only) and unlock detail as the lesson progresses. (3) Provide safe exploration: a reset button, undo, and a "try it" state where nothing is recorded. (4) Put instruction at the moment of need beside the step, not on a separate manual page. (5) Because prior knowledge misleads (assimilation), ask a prediction question ("what will the nose do if you pull back?") before the demonstration so that wrong models surface.

## Kieras1984
**Core claims.** David E. Kieras and Susan Bovair's "The role of a mental model in learning to operate a device" (Cognitive Science 8(3), 255-273, 1984; bibliographic details confirmed by search; University of Michigan's DeepBlue repository lists it) tests whether giving learners a model of how a device works helps them learn to operate it. A related technical report from 1983 describes studies of learning to operate a simple control-panel device and of how learning depends on understanding the device's internal structure. The finding, as summarized in the sources retrieved, is that people who understood how a simple device worked learned and inferred operating procedures more efficiently than people who learned the procedures without that understanding.
**Specifics.** (1) The experiments used a simple control-panel device, with participants learning multi-step operating procedures. Reports I could not check describe the device as a fictional panel of switches and indicators; the exact cover story and apparatus are not confirmed here. (2) One group received a device model, an account of how the device's components are connected and how the internal state flows between them, in addition to the procedures; the other group received the procedures alone. (3) The authors' account is that a model helps in two ways: it makes the procedures easier to learn because steps can be derived from how the device works instead of memorized as arbitrary sequences, and it lets the learner find efficient shortcuts and handle unusual cases. (4) The reported results are the model group's faster learning of procedures, better retention, and more efficient inferred procedures. Exact times, percentages, number of participants and effect sizes were not retrievable this session, so none are stated. (5) The work is tied to Kieras and Polson's production-system approach to modeling the cognitive complexity of learning a procedure, where fewer, more regular rules mean faster learning. (6) A condition for the effect: the model must be connected to the procedures to be learned; knowledge of structure that does not bear on the task gives little help.
**Criticism and status.** The results come from laboratory studies using artificial devices, with small samples typical of cognitive psychology; later work on instructions, including studies in the same research tradition, shows the benefit depends on the kind of model and the task. It is regarded as a foundational, well-cited demonstration of the value of a device model for procedural learning, but a reader should not treat it as showing that more explanation always helps.
**Use for LTF.** (1) This is the strongest empirical case for teaching a short, functional model of each aircraft system (the fuel path, the pitot-static system, how the elevator trim works) alongside the step list. (2) Keep the model small and tied to the procedure: show how the model explains each checklist step. (3) Make the model interactive: let the learner operate a simplified diagram and then run the procedure. (4) Test transfer with a case not in the lesson, such as a failed instrument, to see whether the model supports inference. (5) Do not add model content that no step uses.

## Gentner1983
**Core claims.** Dedre Gentner and Albert L. Stevens edited Mental Models (Lawrence Erlbaum, 1983), the volume that gathered cognitive scientists around the idea that people understand systems by building internal representations they can run to predict behavior. The chapters include Donald Norman's "Some observations on mental models," contributions on reasoning about physical systems such as qualitative physics and naive physics, and Gentner and Gentner's work on analogy. The volume established the term mental model in cognitive science and, through Norman, in HCI. A chapter list was not re-checked in this session.
**Specifics.** (1) Norman's chapter distinguishes four things: the target system, the designer's conceptual model of it, the user's mental model, and the scientist's conceptualization of the user's model. He lists properties of users' mental models: they are incomplete, people can run them only in limited ways, they are unstable (people forget details), they have no firm boundaries (similar devices get confused), they are unscientific (superstitions persist because they cost little), and they are parsimonious (people prefer extra physical action to mental effort). Norman's observations are drawn from studies of calculator users. (2) Gentner and Gentner's work on electricity compares the analogy of flowing water with the analogy of a crowd moving through gates. In their studies, people who used the water analogy did better on questions about batteries in series and in parallel, while those who used the crowd analogy did better on questions about resistors in series and in parallel, because each analogy maps some relations well and others poorly. The size of the effects was not retrievable this session. (3) The wider point is that analogy is a structure mapping: relations carry over, surface properties do not. (4) The book also illustrates that models can be run mentally ("envisioning") to make predictions.
**Criticism and status.** "Mental model" is a loosely defined construct. Critics argue it is hard to falsify and that it covers too many kinds of knowledge. Gentner's structure-mapping theory is better specified and well supported. The Kieras and Bovair paper is the most direct experimental test of the usefulness of a device model in HCI.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Choose analogies deliberately, and record in the content notes what each analogy gets right and where it misleads, because each supports some inferences and hurts others (for example, lift explained by a single mechanism). (2) Elicit the learner's prior model with a quick prediction question before teaching, drawing on driving or gaming experience, and correct the misleading parts (steering a plane is not like steering a car). (3) Expect incomplete and unstable models; use spaced revisits and repeated, varied examples to firm them. (4) Present one consistent model at a time to avoid creating a mixed system image.

## Cooper1995
**Core claims.** Alan Cooper's About Face (1995; fourth edition 2014 with Robert Reimann, David Cronin and Christopher Noessel) argues that software built by programmers for programmers is hostile to ordinary users, and that design should be driven by the goals of specific users. His method, Goal-Directed Design, runs through research (ethnographic interviews and observation), modeling (personas and goals), requirements, a framework (the interaction structure), refinement, and support. His earlier popular book The Inmates Are Running the Asylum (1999) makes the argument for non-specialists.
**Specifics.** (1) Personas are archetypes synthesized from research, described with a name, a picture and a set of goals; they are used as design targets instead of "the user". (2) Goals are in three layers: life goals, experience goals (how the user wants to feel) and end goals (what the user wants to accomplish). Tasks are seen as a means to those goals. (3) Cooper advises designing for one primary persona at a time, whose needs the design must satisfy fully, while secondary personas must not be badly served. The point is that a product for "everyone" satisfies no one. (4) Posture: applications are sovereign (used for long periods), transient, daemonic or parasitic, and the visual and interaction design should fit the posture. (5) Perpetual intermediates: most users are neither beginners nor experts for long, so design should make the first use easy but optimize for the long middle. (6) Behavioral principles: be polite, forgiving and informative; avoid blocking the user with dialog boxes; prefer undo to confirmation ("don't stop the proceedings with idiocy"); use modeless feedback in place of modal error boxes. The exact page for each statement was not checked.
**Criticism and status.** Personas are a practitioner technique with little controlled evidence of effect. Chapman and Milham (2006) argued that the method cannot be falsified and that personas are often invented. Later practitioners advise that personas be grounded in real research data and kept to a small set. Cooper's critique of dialog boxes and confirmation is widely adopted, but "undo instead of confirm" does not work for irreversible actions.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Build two or three data-based learner personas (for example the cautious adult beginner, and the experienced gamer new to real procedures), documented with the research behind them and revisited. (2) Name the primary persona for each screen: the lesson page, the dashboard and fly mode have different primary users and goals. (3) Optimize repeat-use screens for the perpetual intermediate: the checklist and dashboard must be quick for someone on their fiftieth visit. (4) Replace confirmation prompts with undo. (5) Write goals in three layers for each major flow: what the learner wants to feel (calm, capable), to accomplish (fly a pattern) and the life goal (get a pilot certificate someday).

## Saffer2013
**Core claims.** Dan Saffer's Microinteractions: Designing with Details (O'Reilly, 2013) argues that the quality of a product is decided in small, single-purpose moments (setting an alarm, toggling a switch, liking a post, pull-to-refresh) and that these moments can be designed with a repeatable structure. The book's thesis is that "the details are not the details"; they make the product. Saffer is a veteran interaction designer and the author of Designing for Interaction (2006).
**Specifics.** (1) Structure of a microinteraction in four parts. Trigger: what initiates it; manual triggers are user-initiated (a button, a gesture) and should be discoverable and easy to hit; system triggers start when conditions are met (an incoming message, a time, a sensor). Rules: what happens once triggered; the rules should be simple, constrain the possibilities and make the next step obvious. Feedback: how the user learns what is happening, through visual, audible or tactile cues; he advises feedback proportionate to the action's importance, and feedback should explain the rules. Loops and modes: how long it lasts, what changes with repeated use (long loops), and any alternate states (modes), which should be minimized. (2) Principles: don't start from zero (use defaults, remembered settings and context), prevent errors by making the right thing easy, make the most important parts unobtrusive, and "less is more". (3) The signature moment: a memorable detail that carries the product's personality (his examples include the interplay of status and animation in well-known apps). (4) He discusses how data and sensors can feed rules, and how to avoid the "big flashy" feature that fades after repeated use; a microinteraction seen hundreds of times should be quick and quiet. (5) Examples are taken from commercial products; no experiments are reported.
**Criticism and status.** A practitioner book, not an empirical source; the four-part structure is a useful taxonomy and not a validated model. Some advice (delight through animation) can conflict with accessibility needs such as reduced motion.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Specify each repeated interaction (tick a checklist item, mark a step done, reveal an answer, save progress) in the four parts and review them as a set. (2) Use the signature-moment idea for identity: a small, consistent, well-made detail (for example the way a completed checklist item settles) gives the product character without changing layout. (3) Because learners repeat these moments constantly, keep them short, and make the feedback quiet on the hundredth use. (4) Provide a reduced-motion variant of every animated feedback. (5) Remember settings and progress so the learner never starts from zero.

## Raskin2000
**Core claims.** Jef Raskin, who started the Macintosh project at Apple, wrote The Humane Interface (Addison-Wesley, 2000) to argue that an interface is humane if it is responsive to human needs and considerate of human frailties. A central argument is that modes are a major source of error, and that the cure is to eliminate modes or make them kinesthetic. He defines an interface as modal with respect to a gesture if the same gesture has different results depending on the system's state, and the user's locus of attention is not on that state.
**Specifics.** (1) Mode errors: the user performs an action appropriate to one state while the system is in another. The caveat "locus of attention" matters: a mode is harmless if the user is looking at its indicator while acting, and dangerous if the indicator is elsewhere. (2) Remedies: make the interface modeless; use quasimodes, in which the mode lasts only while the user holds a physical action, such as the Shift key (the user cannot forget it because the body maintains it); or make the mode visible at the locus of attention. (3) Monotony: have one way to do each task, to reduce choice and speed up habit formation. (4) Habituation: repeated warnings and confirmations are quickly dismissed without being read, so an "Are you sure?" dialog that appears every time protects nothing; the better answer is undo. (5) He applies quantitative efficiency analysis: GOMS-style keystroke counts and information-theoretic measures of the minimum effort for a task. (6) Larry Tesler's law of conservation of complexity, as discussed in the HCI literature, states that every application has an inherent amount of complexity that cannot be removed, only moved between the user and the system; designers should move it into the system when many users share the cost. Tesler's own source for the law was not confirmed this session. (7) Other ideas in the book include a zooming interface, universal text search (LEAP), and removing separate applications; these were not adopted widely.
**Criticism and status.** The mode-error analysis is widely accepted and supported by studies of slips (Norman's classification). Several of Raskin's larger proposals did not reach the mainstream, and some are seen as unrealistic for complex products. His claim that quasimodes are best is a design opinion.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Avoid hidden modes in widgets. If a gauge has "explore" and "test" states, show the state right at the point of action. (2) Prefer quasimodes for peeking: hold to reveal the answer, release to hide. (3) Fly mode versus study mode is the app's largest mode: make it unmistakable in layout, not only color, so a learner beside the simulator cannot mistake it. (4) Do not ask for confirmation of routine actions; offer undo. (5) Apply Tesler's idea to content: let the system hold the complexity (unit conversions, V-speed tokens) so the learner does not.

## BeaudouinLafon2000
**Core claims.** Michel Beaudouin-Lafon's "Instrumental interaction: an interaction model for designing post-WIMP user interfaces" (CHI 2000, pp. 446-453, DOI 10.1145/332040.332473; details confirmed by search) argues that direct manipulation, as defined by Shneiderman and analyzed by Hutchins, Hollan and Norman, does not scale to the many commands and objects of modern applications. The proposed replacement generalizes it: interaction is mediated by instruments, which are reified tools that sit between the user and the domain objects, like the pen, ruler or brush of the physical world. The model extends and generalizes the principles of direct manipulation and is meant for post-WIMP interfaces, that is, interfaces beyond windows, icons, menus and pointer.
**Specifics.** (1) Components: the domain object (the thing being edited), the instrument (the mediating tool, with a physical part that the user holds and a logical part that acts on the object) and the reaction of the object, which gives feedback. (2) Three properties that can be measured for any instrument. Degree of indirection: the spatial and temporal offset between the instrument and the object it acts on (a scrollbar acts at a distance on the document; dragging the document acts without offset). Degree of integration: the ratio between the degrees of freedom of the logical part of the instrument and the degrees of freedom of the input device (a mouse has two; a slider using one of them integrates poorly with a 2D movement). Degree of compatibility: the similarity between the user's action and the object's response (drag to move is highly compatible; typing a number to move is not). (3) Three design principles: reification (turn commands, concepts or relations into first-class objects that can be manipulated, as palettes and tool handles do), polymorphism (one instrument works on many object types, as copy and paste does), and reuse (keep input and output so earlier work can be applied again, such as styles and macros). (4) The paper demonstrates the model by redesigning search and replace as an instrument, with a demo that reifies the command into a manipulable object. (5) The model is a design space with criteria that let designers compare alternatives, and it was developed through prototypes in the author's lab.
**Criticism and status.** The paper offers an analytical model and demonstrations, not controlled experiments; the three degrees are useful for comparison but are not strictly quantified for all cases. It has been widely cited, including in a later HCI handbook chapter by the same author, and is a standard reference in post-WIMP design.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Design each interactive widget as an instrument acting on a domain object: the learner uses a knob, handle or slider on a model of the airplane or instrument. (2) Score proposed controls by indirection and compatibility: dragging the nose of an aircraft silhouette to set pitch has high compatibility; a number field does not. (3) Reify repeatable operations: a "reset", a saved scenario or a recorded attempt becomes an object the learner can replay. (4) Polymorphism helps consistency: the same drag-to-set gesture should work across all instruments (heading bug, altimeter setting). (5) Use integration to choose input for touch: if the instrument needs two degrees of freedom, a two-dimensional drag fits better than two sliders.

## Victor2006
**Core claims.** Bret Victor's essay "Magic Ink: Information software and the graphical interface" (2006, worrydream.com) argues that most software people use is information software, used for learning, comparing and deciding, not for manipulating things, and that designing it as an interactive machine is a mistake. The essay holds that such software should be designed mainly as graphic design (the presentation of information on the page), with as little interaction as possible, supported by the software's ability to infer context, which is the "magic ink". He treats interaction as a cost, since each click, wait or memory demand is effort the user pays.
**Specifics.** (1) He proposes that the software should use context, the user's history and the environment, to show the relevant view directly, rather than requiring the user to navigate to it. (2) A recurring example is a train timetable: instead of a grid of every departure, software that knows the time and the user's location can show the next departures and the information needed for the decision. I could not re-check the exact examples used in the essay, so details beyond this are not stated. (3) Three themes in the essay: context-sensitivity, the primacy of the information graphic, and learning from the user's history so that fewer choices must be re-entered. (4) He criticizes the habit of treating every program as a tool with menus and buttons, and the emphasis on interface widgets instead of information. (5) His separate essay "A Brief Rant on the Future of Interaction Design" (2011) argues that touchscreen "pictures under glass" ignore the rich capabilities of human hands and haptic feel, and his talk "Inventing on Principle" (2012) argues for immediate visible feedback when creating. These are essays and talks, not papers, and are cited here only as context.
**Criticism and status.** Not peer-reviewed; an argued position by a respected researcher and designer with prior work at Apple, whose arguments are cited widely in design circles. Context-sensitivity raises privacy and error concerns the essay discusses only partly. Its claim that interaction is a cost is plausible and consistent with usability work on interaction cost but is not tested here.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Show state instead of asking for it: the dashboard should show what to do next from the learner's history, not a menu of lessons to choose among. (2) Replace a list-and-compare flow with a single graphic that makes the comparison visible (for example airspeed bands for each configuration). (3) Count the interaction cost of each step and remove steps that exist only because of the UI. (4) For fly mode, show glanceable, context-aware information and ask for minimal input. (5) Treat graphic design of explanations as primary: a lesson page is information software, so spend design effort on how the information looks before adding widgets.

## Suchman1987
**Core claims.** Lucy Suchman's Plans and Situated Actions: The Problem of Human-Machine Communication (Cambridge University Press, 1987; second edition 2007 titled Human-Machine Reconfigurations) challenges the view in cognitive science and AI that action is the execution of plans. She argues that plans are resources for action, not its cause: people act in response to the circumstances of the moment, and what counts as a plan is often reconstructed afterward. The book is the founding statement of situated action in HCI and an influence on ethnography-based design and CSCW.
**Specifics.** (1) The study base is ethnographic, at Xerox PARC: she observed and recorded people learning to use a photocopier with an on-line help system built on a model of the user's plan. Details of the machine and participants are not confirmed here. (2) The central finding is that the machine had access to only a small part of the user's actions and drew inferences from them, while people relied on rich context and could not easily repair misunderstandings with the machine; this produced breakdowns, described as asymmetry in the resources for conversation between human and machine. (3) Suchman argues that interaction is a kind of communication that needs mutual intelligibility and the ability to repair misunderstanding; a machine's actions must be accountable, meaning visible and interpretable by the user. (4) Instructions and help are resources that users consult and interpret as they go, so designing them as controllers that dictate the next step ignores what people actually do. (5) The book contrasts the plan-based model of AI planning with ethnomethodology and conversation analysis.
**Criticism and status.** The evidence is qualitative and from one setting. Vera and Simon (1993) defended symbolic planning against the situated-action critique, and the debate shaped cognitive science for years. In HCI the book's influence is large, and its practical lessons (design for repair, make machine state visible) are widely accepted.
**Use for LTF.** (1) A lesson is a plan; the learner in the sim improvises. Design for recovery: when the learner drifts, show how to rejoin the sequence instead of marking failure. (2) Make the app's responses intelligible: if an answer is marked wrong, say why in the learner's terms. (3) Do not assume the learner followed the intended path; allow jumping in at any step, resuming and skipping, and keep progress state honest about what was actually done. (4) Treat checklists and lesson steps as resources the learner interprets, with context-sensitive notes, not as commands. (5) In fly mode, expect that the learner's attention is divided; support short interactions and easy re-entry.

## Wensveen2004
**Core claims.** Stephan Wensveen, Tom Djajadiningrat and Kees Overbeeke's "Interaction frogger: a design framework to couple action and function through feedback and feedforward" (DIS 2004, Cambridge, MA, pp. 177-184, DOI 10.1145/1013115.1013140; details come from a dblp record and were not cross-checked on the ACM page) addresses a weakness of purely function-oriented interface design: when electronic products separate the action from the function, the user has to learn arbitrary links. The Delft/Eindhoven group argues that the product should show, through its own form, what actions it allows (feedforward) and show the result of an action in a way that is tied to the action (feedback), so that action possibilities are coupled to function in a natural way.
**Specifics.** (1) Feedforward is information before action, about what the action can do and how; Norman's affordances are one source, but feedforward also includes information about the purpose and the result. (2) Three kinds of feedforward and feedback are separated: inherent (arising from the action itself, such as the resistance of a knob), augmented (added by the product, such as a light or sound) and functional (information about the function itself). (3) The framework lists aspects along which action and its effect can be coupled: time, location, direction, dynamics, modality and expression. A natural coupling means the effect appears at the same time and place as the action, moves in the same direction, with matching dynamics (force, speed), in the same sensory modality, and with an expression that mirrors the manner of the action. (4) The design example is an alarm clock whose controls are shaped so that the user's manipulations map onto the settings. (5) The same research group published related work, including "But how, Donald, tell us how?" (DIS 2002), a critique of reliance on function-based affordance. I did not verify that paper this session.
**Criticism and status.** The framework is illustrated by prototypes and design argument, not controlled evaluation. It extends Norman's feedback concept in a way that is mainly relevant for tangible and physical interaction, and its value for screens is by analogy.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Give feedforward before commit: show a ghost of the result while the learner drags a control. (2) Couple time, place and direction: when the learner turns the virtual trim wheel, the pitch change must appear immediately, near the control, and in the matching direction. (3) Match dynamics: use damping and inertia in widgets that echo the real control's feel, and avoid instant snaps for continuous controls. (4) Use inherent feedback where possible (the control itself moves) before adding augmented feedback (sounds, highlights). (5) For fly mode, tie visible state to the learner's action so they never wonder whether a tap registered.

## Hutchins1985
**Core claims.** Edwin Hutchins, James Hollan and Donald Norman's "Direct manipulation interfaces" (Human-Computer Interaction 1(4), 311-338, 1985, DOI 10.1207/s15327051hci0104_2; details confirmed by search; a version appeared in Norman and Draper's User Centered System Design, 1986) gives a cognitive account of what makes direct manipulation interfaces feel direct, and of their costs. Shneiderman (1983) had described the style; this paper explains it with the idea of distance and engagement.
**Specifics.** (1) Distance between the user's goals and the interface, split into semantic distance (how much of the work of expressing a goal in the system's language the user must do; does the interface support what the user wants to say in terms they think in) and articulatory distance (how well the form of the input and output expressions resembles their meaning). Reducing either lowers the cognitive effort needed to bridge the gulfs of execution and evaluation. (2) Engagement: the feeling of acting directly on the objects of the task, the "model-world" metaphor, as opposed to the "conversation" metaphor in which the user talks to an intermediary who carries out the work. Direct manipulation creates first-person engagement. (3) The authors state that directness is not simply good: it can be costly when the task is repetitive or abstract, since a command language can express a repeated or generalized operation more compactly. (4) They tie the style to the gulf concepts that Norman developed. (5) Direct manipulation features they discuss include continuous representation of objects, physical actions in place of complex syntax, and rapid, incremental, reversible operations with immediate feedback (from Shneiderman's account, which this lane's other source, Beaudouin-Lafon, extends).
**Criticism and status.** The analysis is conceptual and has been very influential. It does not provide numeric evidence, and later work (including Beaudouin-Lafon's) notes that pure direct manipulation does not scale to rich tools. Experts often prefer commands and shortcuts for repeated operations.
**Use for LTF.** (1) Widgets should be model-world: the learner turns the heading bug on a dial instead of typing a heading. (2) Reduce semantic distance: label controls with the terms the lesson has taught, and introduce a pilot term only when its meaning is on screen. (3) Keep articulatory distance low: a rotary control should be turned, a lever pushed or pulled, mirroring the real control's motion. (4) Offer commands and shortcuts for repeated operations ("mark all steps done", keyboard shortcuts in fly mode) instead of forcing direct manipulation. (5) Keep operations incremental and reversible so exploration is safe.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** Across Norman, Gaver, Hartson, McGrenere and Ho, and Wensveen and colleagues, the shared position is that a person acts well only when two things are present: the possibility of action exists, and there is perceivable information that specifies it. Every author who revisits Norman's 1988 term ends up separating the two, whether as real versus perceived affordance, affordance versus information, or affordance versus signifier. Norman, Hutchins and colleagues, and Beaudouin-Lafon agree that feedback must be immediate and tied to the action, and that the user's model of the system comes only from what the system shows. Kieras and Bovair, and Gentner and Stevens, supply the learning evidence: a small, functional model helps people learn procedures and make inferences, while analogies help some inferences and hurt others. Carroll and Rosson, Suchman and Raskin agree that real users do not follow instructions or plans as designed, so design must expect shortcuts, improvisation, skipped text and mode slips, and must make recovery cheap.
**Where they conflict.** Gibson holds that perception of affordances is direct and needs no mental model, while Norman's account relies on mental models and conventions. Victor argues interaction is a cost to be minimized, while Beaudouin-Lafon and Hutchins treat well-chosen manipulable instruments as the route to understanding. Suchman challenges the idea that instruction can control action, which sits uneasily with step-by-step lesson design. Cooper's personas are widely used but weakly evidenced, while the most empirical sources (Kieras and Bovair, Carroll and Carrithers) are small, old and laboratory-based. Raskin's more radical proposals were not adopted.
**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Audit each control for hidden and false affordances, and show a signifier for each possible action.
2. Use web conventions for what is clickable, and put identity in style and motion instead.
3. Review every control on four counts: sensory, cognitive, physical and functional.
4. Diagnose confusion in two steps: is the action possible, and is it shown?
5. Make feedback immediate, near the action and in the matching direction, and give feedforward before commitment.
6. Teach one small functional model per aircraft system alongside the procedure, and choose analogies knowingly.
7. Assume learners skip text: start with a task, simplify first (training wheels), and put help in context.
8. Avoid modes; where unavoidable, show the state at the locus of attention or use quasimodes. Make fly mode unmistakable.
9. Prefer undo to confirmation, and design recovery for off-path use.
10. Show state instead of asking for it, and ground personas in real data.
