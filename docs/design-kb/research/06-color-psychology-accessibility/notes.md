## Elliot2014ColorPsych
**Core claims.** Elliot and Maier's Annual Review chapter is the standard modern summary of experimental color psychology. Its organizing idea is Color-in-Context Theory: color carries meaning, and the meaning is activated by context. Part of that meaning is learned (red with danger, mistakes and stop signals in achievement settings, but with passion and attraction in romantic settings), and the authors also entertain a biologically prepared component (red as a signal of dominance or arousal across species). The practical consequence is that color effects are context-specific and not universal rules such as "red excites, blue calms". The review also argues that a color effect should be attributed to hue only after lightness and chroma are controlled, because many early studies compared colors that differed in brightness and saturation as well as hue.

**Specifics.** The review is organized by domain: achievement (seeing red before an exam or test, avoidance motivation, performance), affiliation and attraction (red clothing or backgrounds on photographed people and rated attractiveness; red and perceived dominance), consumer behavior (store and product color, price perception) and food and drink (color shapes expected and perceived flavor). For each domain the authors list the studies, typical designs (brief exposure to a patch, a folder cover, a shirt color, a photograph background, typically with university students and short dependent measures) and the proposed mechanisms. They separate three levels of explanation: perception, learned association, and an innate or evolved response, and say that for most effects the evidence cannot yet separate them. They are explicit that effects are mostly small to moderate in the originals, that sample sizes were often modest, and that few studies used real tasks on screens or in the field. The concluding section calls for work on boundary conditions, moderators such as age, culture and individual differences, and real-world generalizability before "strong conceptual statements and recommendations for application" are warranted (paraphrase of the abstract, which the search confirmed).

**Criticism and status.** The review was published just before the replication crisis changed norms in social psychology. Several flagship results discussed in it, notably red impairing test performance and the blue-creative, red-detail effect, have since seen failed direct replications (see Steele2014Replication). The authors are also the originators of many of the studies, so the review is partly a self-assessment. It remains the right starting point because it states the limits of the field in the authors' own words. Its strongest, most reproducible content concerns learned meaning in context, not mood engineering.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Never justify a palette with a sentence like "red raises anxiety, blue calms"; the evidence does not support it as a design rule. (2) Spend color on meaning the app teaches and the cockpit uses (warning, caution, normal), because context-learned meaning is the better-supported mechanism. (3) When comparing palettes, hold lightness and chroma constant, or report them, so a "hue effect" is not a brightness effect. (4) Treat any color claim as a hypothesis to test inside LTF's own lessons, since lab results on printed patches do not transfer automatically to a screen beside a flight simulator. (5) Pair every semantic color with an icon and a word, which makes the design robust whether or not the association holds.

## Elliot2007Red
**Core claims.** Elliot and colleagues reported that brief exposure to red before an achievement task impairs performance, relative to green, gray or black. They proposed that red activates avoidance motivation because it is learned as the color of errors (red pen corrections, red warning signs) and failure, and that this triggers a worry-and-avoid response that interferes with cognitive work. The paper is the foundation of the "red hurts performance" literature that later spread into advice to teachers and designers.

**Specifics.** The article reports a series of experiments (six in the version I recall) with university students. In the typical design a participant number printed in red, green or black, or a test cover or color of an instruction sheet, was seen briefly before a test such as anagrams or an analytical or IQ-type test, and score differences were compared. Sample sizes were small by modern standards, roughly tens of participants per experiment (the exact numbers are not confirmed here). The authors reported that red lowered scores and, in a related task, that participants given red were more inclined to choose easier items, which fits the avoidance account. The controls for lightness and chroma were described as matched but were later questioned. Follow-up work by the same group added physiological and neural measures (for example frontal EEG asymmetry associated with avoidance); I recall this but have not confirmed citations, so it is not used as evidence here.

**Criticism and status.** Replication is poor. Kenneth Steele's lab ran several direct replications of the red effect (conference posters 2015 to 2017) and found no difference between color conditions. A group around Gnambs ran four replications of a related study on processing the word "red" and found no effect on verbal reasoning. A 2020 meta-analysis in Psychonomic Bulletin & Review reports a small negative effect of red on anagram and reasoning scores; I could not confirm its authors this session, and the pooled effect and its bias adjustments are not confirmed. A fair summary: if a real effect exists it is small, probably smaller than the originals, and highly sensitive to procedure. It is not strong enough to be a design rule.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Do not write "red harms learning" in the book as a fact; present it as a contested hypothesis. (2) Do not avoid red feedback for this reason; justify any red restraint instead by convention (red means stop or error in cockpits) and by color-vision safety. (3) If the owner wants to know whether red feedback matters for LTF learners, run an A/B on the real quiz feedback. (4) Use this paper in the book as a worked example of why one famous unreplicated study is not a design basis. (5) Prefer positive framing and clear explanation in feedback over color tricks, since wording and timing have much larger known effects.

## Mehta2009Blue
**Core claims.** Mehta and Zhu, in Science, reported that background color changes performance depending on the task: red backgrounds improved performance on detail-oriented tasks such as proofreading and recall, while blue backgrounds improved performance on creative tasks such as generating ideas for a toy. They explained this with motivational orientation: red evokes avoidance and so vigilance and attention to detail, blue evokes approach and openness, which suits creativity.

**Specifics.** The paper reports several experiments (I recall six) with students at a Canadian university and online participants. Tasks included recalling words, detecting spelling errors and counting words (detail-oriented), and generating uses for a brick or toy designs (creative). Backgrounds were red, blue or sometimes white, shown on a computer. The reported effects were of moderate size, with red giving more accurate detail work and blue giving roughly twice as many creative outputs in some measures (exact ratios not confirmed). Priming by color words and a mediation analysis were used to argue that avoidance and approach states drive the effect. The study became popular in design and marketing writing, often condensed into "use blue for creativity and red for focus".

**Criticism and status.** Steele (2014) failed to replicate the color effect on anagram solving times with adequate power. Other null results have been reported, and the broader replication crisis in priming-style effects (including color priming) reduced confidence. The original study's exact procedure was complex, and defenders argue that the effect depends on task details and screen conditions, but no strong confirming replication is known to me. Treat it as unreliable.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Never color a "creative" lesson blue and a "careful" lesson red on this basis. (2) Keep the reading surface in a neutral near-white or near-black; reserve hue for meaning. (3) If a stakeholder cites "blue boosts creativity", point to the failed replication. (4) Add this to a "retired advice" list in the book next to learning styles and the golden ratio. (5) Any claim about color and task performance in LTF should come from LTF's own measured data (completion, error rate), not from this paper.

## Steele2014Replication
**Core claims.** Kenneth Steele, a psychologist at Appalachian State University, tried to replicate the Mehta and Zhu (2009) color-priming effect on anagram solution time, in Psychonomic Bulletin & Review. The title states the result: failure to replicate. The paper is the standard citation for retiring the red-detail, blue-creative advice.

**Specifics.** Participants solved anagrams on a computer under background-color conditions modeled on the original manipulation (the exact conditions are not confirmed here), with a sample intended to match or exceed the original (the original's first experiment had on the order of 70 participants; not confirmed). The dependent measures were solution time and number solved. No reliable color effect appeared, and the paper discusses the possibility that the original finding was a false positive or depended on unreported conditions. The same author later presented unpublished replications of Elliot et al. (2007) at conferences (Psychonomic Society posters, 2015 to 2017), reporting no impairment from red and calling the third such attempt "Third Failure to Replicate" in a poster title. These posters are not peer-reviewed and are noted only as context.

**Criticism and status.** One replication failure is not final proof, and defenders of color-in-context effects argue that tasks, screens and populations matter. Still, it fits the pattern in the Open Science Collaboration (2015) project, in which many priming-style effects shrank or vanished on replication. The practical conclusion for designers is conservative: do not rely on a single lab result to choose background colors.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Keep lesson background colors consistent and neutral; do not vary them by task type for an imagined cognitive benefit. (2) Use this paper as the reference when the book says "color-mood tricks are not evidence-based". (3) Prefer testing LTF variants with real learners. (4) Teach the habit of asking "has it replicated?" as part of the book's evidence policy. (5) Where LTF changes color by state (practice vs. review), do it for orientation and meaning, and describe it that way.

## Palmer2010Ecological
**Core claims.** Palmer and Schloss propose the Ecological Valence Theory (EVT): people like colors in proportion to how much they like the things that tend to have those colors. Blues are liked because of clear sky and clean water, saturated colors partly because of fruit, flowers and gems, and dark yellows and browns are disliked because of rotten food, mucus and feces. Preference for a hue is therefore a learned affective summary of the world, tuned by experience and so partly by culture and age, with an adaptive rationale: approaching good things and avoiding bad ones.

**Specifics.** In the PNAS study, Berkeley undergraduates rated how much they liked 32 colors, built from eight hues (red, orange, yellow, chartreuse, green, cyan, blue, purple) at four variants each (saturated, light, muted, dark). A separate group described objects and rated how much they liked each. From these the authors computed a Weighted Affective Valence Estimate (WAVE) for each color, the average liking of the objects associated with it, weighted by how closely the object's color matched the color. WAVE correlated strongly with average color preference, about r = .8 (roughly 80 percent of variance, the figure usually reported; exact value not re-confirmed). The EVT beat three alternatives with equal or more free parameters: a cone-contrast model, a color-appearance model (hue, saturation, lightness), and a color-emotion model (ratings of the colors on emotion scales). The pattern of the data: blue and saturated colors liked, dark yellow-green (olive) the least liked.

**Criticism and status.** The result is correlational, and the direction of causation is argued rather than shown: associations might reflect shared causes. Later studies by Schloss and colleagues show that preferences can be shifted by manipulating the valence of associated objects (for example, in sports-fan or university-team color preferences), which supports the causal story. Cross-cultural studies find preferences differ with cultural associations. Samples are young and US-based. The theory explains average preference, not individual taste, and says nothing directly about performance or learning.

**Use for LTF.** (1) A blue or teal base is broadly liked (sky and water), but it is also the default of aerospace and software, so distinctiveness needs another lever, such as a specific accent. (2) Avoid large fields of olive, mustard and mud brown; they are the most disliked region. (3) Borrow accent colors from real aviation things, such as runway markings, cockpit instrument lighting, sectional chart tints and high-visibility paint; EVT predicts they read as familiar and, if associated with positive flying experiences, liked. (4) Preference is not a performance measure; a preferred color is not thereby better for reading or teaching. (5) Keep a user theme choice, since individual preference varies and people can adapt to their own selection.

## Jonauskaite2020Universal
**Core claims.** Jonauskaite and Mohr, with many co-authors, tested whether color-emotion associations are universal or culture-bound. Across 30 nations and 22 languages, 4,598 participants matched 12 color terms to 20 emotion concepts in strongly similar ways: an average cross-nation pattern similarity of r = .88. The authors conclude that associations have a universal core, with weaker local variation that tracks linguistic and geographic proximity: nations that share a language or border have more similar patterns.

**Specifics.** Participants (mostly university students, recruited through an international collaboration) saw one color word at a time (red, orange, yellow, green, turquoise, blue, purple, pink, brown, black, gray, white) and rated how much each of 20 emotions fit it (for example love, joy, pleasure, anger, fear, sadness, disappointment, relief, pride, admiration). Analysis used pattern-similarity measures between nations and a machine-learning model that could identify a participant's country from their pattern better than chance. Widely repeated headline pairs: red with love and anger, yellow with joy, black with sadness and fear, gray with sadness and disappointment, blue and white with relief or calm, pink with love and pleasure. Positive emotions tend to go with lighter and more saturated colors, negative emotions with darker ones, in line with a valence-arousal-lightness structure. The paper also reports that similarity falls with linguistic and geographic distance.

**Criticism and status.** The task uses color names, not color experienced on a surface, and stated association is not felt emotion or behavior. The student samples are not representative of whole populations. The central finding is consistent with earlier smaller cross-cultural work (Adams and Osgood 1973; Madden, Hewett and Roth 2000) and with the later large replications by the same group. The authors themselves present associations as a link in language and thought, not as a causal effect of colors on mood.

**Use for LTF.** (1) For an English-first learner audience, the standard conventions (red for danger or wrong, green for okay, yellow for caution, blue for calm or information) are well supported as shared associations. (2) Do not claim that a color changes a learner's mood. (3) If LTF is localized, test local meanings of white, red and green, which vary most in marketing studies. (4) Because associations are shared but not fixed, always pair a semantic color with an icon and a word. (5) Aviation's red-amber-green scheme coincides with everyday associations, so it is a safe backbone for the product's status colors.

## Madden2000Managing
**Core claims.** Madden, Hewett and Roth compared color meanings and preferences across eight countries (as I recall: the United States, Canada, Mexico, Germany, Japan, China, Taiwan and Hong Kong, though the exact list is not confirmed) to guide international image and brand management. They find both cross-national agreement and culture-specific variation: some associations (for instance the positive reading of blue and green, and negative reading of black and dark colors in some contexts) are widely shared, while others differ.

**Specifics.** Respondents rated color chips and color terms on bipolar adjective scales and preference measures, and the authors used the profiles to show which nations shared meanings. They reported a high degree of similarity for many color-concept links across the countries, and diverging patterns for particular colors such as white and red in East Asian samples (for example white linked with mourning and purity in different contexts). The specific correlation values and sample sizes are not confirmed here. The paper was part of a wave of 1990s cross-cultural color work that also includes Adams and Osgood (1973), whose earlier semantic-differential study of 23 cultures found evaluation, potency and activity factors for color concepts across languages.

**Criticism and status.** The work is dated, relies on student samples and on stated meaning, and the term-level findings are less robust than the structural ones; Jonauskaite et al. (2020) supersedes it with a far larger, better-designed sample. Marketing lore about "white means death in China" is often overgeneralized from this type of result and should be treated cautiously.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Plan for localization of color meaning if the product leaves the English-speaking market. (2) Prefer semantic colors that are shared (blue, green, red) and treat white and black as culture-sensitive. (3) Do not copy marketing folklore about color meanings; cite the cross-national papers instead. (4) Use words and icons next to color. (5) Keep brand colors separate from status colors, so a local brand change does not flip a safety meaning.

## Whitfield1990Critical
**Core claims.** Whitfield and Wiltshire examine the claims of color psychology up to 1990 and conclude that most are weakly supported. They criticize the field for uncontrolled lightness and saturation, tiny samples, observer and experimenter bias, reliance on a few repeatedly cited sources, and failure to replicate. They separate a defensible core (hue and brightness affect judged warmth, pleasantness and arousal in modest, context-dependent ways; color contributes to preference) from folk claims such as color therapy and calming wall colors.

**Specifics.** The monograph reviews wavelength and physiological arousal (for example, whether red light raises skin conductance, heart rate or muscular tension), color and mood, color and task performance, and preference, and for each shows that effect sizes are inconsistent. They recommend specifying stimuli in a standard color space such as Munsell or CIE, matching lightness and chroma when comparing hues, adopting double-blind procedures and using representative samples. I recall the review's treatment of color preference findings (blue the most liked across many studies) and its skepticism toward color-room therapy. Exact page counts and numbers of studies reviewed are not confirmed; page range 385-411 is from memory and is not confirmed.

**Criticism and status.** The review is dated; it predates Palmer's ecological work, the Elliot color-in-context program and cross-cultural data. Its methodological critiques, however, remain correct and helped motivate controlled-stimulus work such as Valdez and Mehrabian (1994).

**Use for LTF.** (1) In the book, state a rule: any color-mood or color-performance claim must specify the color in a perceptual space with lightness and chroma. (2) Treat "calming color" and "energizing color" product copy as unsupported. (3) Judge palettes by contrast, hierarchy and meaning. (4) Use this review as the citation for caution when a stakeholder shows a pop-psychology color chart. (5) Prefer measured outcomes (completion, errors, time) to felt mood when testing color changes.

## Valdez1994Effects
**Core claims.** Valdez and Mehrabian asked participants to rate standardized colors on Mehrabian's pleasure, arousal and dominance (PAD) scales, and showed that brightness (Munsell value) and saturation (chroma) explain much more of the emotional response than hue. In general, higher saturation raised arousal, and brightness and saturation were associated with higher pleasure; the dominance dimension depended mostly on brightness and saturation as well.

**Specifics.** The stimuli were Munsell color chips covering a range of hues, values and chromas, presented under controlled conditions to university students in several experiments. Ratings were analyzed with regressions of PAD on hue, brightness and saturation. Reported patterns, as I recall them: brightness correlated positively with pleasure and negatively with arousal; saturation correlated positively with both pleasure and arousal; hue differences were smaller, with blue-green hues somewhat more pleasant than yellows at matched values. The regression weights and sample sizes are not confirmed here. The work was important for showing how to run a color-emotion study with colorimetric control.

**Criticism and status.** The stimuli are plain patches, rated out of context; real interfaces combine many colors, text and shapes, and the emotions evoked by an app depend on content and use. Hue-specific effects have not been consistently reproduced, and the PAD framework, while influential, is a simple model. The core claim that lightness and saturation matter more than hue is consistent with later literature (for example Jonauskaite 2020's valence-lightness pattern).

**Use for LTF.** (1) Manage saturation as a budget: saturated colors are arousing and attention-grabbing, so give them to alerts, primary actions and the active instrument, not to backgrounds. (2) Use lighter, less saturated tones for large reading surfaces. (3) In dark theme, lower chroma further to limit glare. (4) Do not expect hue changes at fixed lightness and chroma to alter mood. (5) For quick qualitative checks of a palette, PAD-style questions (calm vs. stimulating, pleasant vs. unpleasant, in control vs. overwhelmed) are a cheap vocabulary.

## Labrecque2012Exciting
**Core claims.** Labrecque and Milne show that the colors of a logo shape perceived brand personality along Aaker's dimensions: red is read as exciting, blue as competent, with others such as pink, purple and brown linked to sincerity, sophistication or ruggedness in more modest ways. They also show that value and saturation modulate these effects and that a fit between color and brand type or product category affects brand attitude.

**Specifics.** Using a sequence of studies with online and student participants, the authors presented fictitious logos or brand marks in different hues, lightnesses and saturations, then had participants rate personality traits and attitudes. Aaker's five dimensions were used as the response measure (sincerity, excitement, competence, sophistication, ruggedness). Reported significant differences: red logos rated higher on excitement than other colors, blue on competence, with value and saturation shifting these by specific amounts (exact effect sizes not confirmed). A further study linked color-brand congruence to brand evaluation.

**Criticism and status.** The measures are brief perceptions of invented brands, not behavior; effects are modest, and cultural and category contexts matter. The paper is useful as evidence that people consistently read personality from color on first exposure, not as evidence that a certain color improves outcomes.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Blue reads as competent and trustworthy, which suits training, but because it is also the generic choice in aviation and software, LTF cannot get distinctiveness from blue alone. (2) Color-brand fit matters: a flight-training product should look credible; a bright, playful accent can add warmth but needs to be coordinated with the serious base. (3) One signature accent used with discipline will do more for identity than many colors. (4) Test first-impression personality with simple adjective ratings in user sessions. (5) Keep brand color distinct from semantic status colors so branding never dilutes safety meaning.

## Gorn2004Waiting
**Core claims.** Gorn, Chattopadhyay, Sengupta and Tripathi, in the Journal of Marketing Research, show that the background color of a screen changes how long a download feels. Colors that induce more relaxation make the wait seem shorter, through relaxed feelings; colors that induce less relaxation make it seem longer. The effect depends on all three color dimensions (hue, value and chroma), and the final experiment shows that color also influences evaluations of the site and willingness to recommend it.

**Specifics.** The studies were laboratory experiments in which participants waited for a simulated download against different background screens, and then rated perceived quickness and their relaxation. Blue-type backgrounds, which the authors expect to be relaxing, were judged to download faster than red-type backgrounds. Manipulating value and chroma, they report that lower chroma and higher value (lighter, less saturated) tended to produce more relaxation and shorter perceived waits; one third-party summary says value had the largest effect. The authors also varied the number of repeated waits, which shifted relaxation and perceived time in a comparable way, supporting the mediation. A frequently quoted "up to 53 percent" speedup appears in secondary writing; I could not trace this figure to the paper and treat it as not confirmed. The sample sizes are not confirmed.

**Criticism and status.** I know of no large preregistered replication. The result rests on self-reported time in the lab, with time estimates, not a measured behavior such as abandonment. It belongs to the same family as other color-and-mood claims whose reliability is unproven. The mechanism (relaxation shortens perceived time) is plausible and consistent with the wider literature on perceived waiting time, but the color-to-relaxation link is the weak step.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Do not depend on color to cover for slowness; use progress indicators, skeleton screens and fast response (see the motion and response-time lane). (2) If a loading state is shown, keep it low in chroma and not red-dominant, which is at worst harmless. (3) Do not quote the 53 percent figure. (4) Test perceived speed with LTF users if it matters. (5) The more reliable lesson is that perceived time is malleable and design should aim to make waiting informative.

## Cardosi1999Guidelines
**Core claims.** Cardosi and Hannon's FAA report (DOT/FAA/AR-99/52, 1999) offers general guidelines for color on air traffic control displays. Its catalog abstract says that color is probably the most effective, compelling and attractive method for coding visual information on a display, that the report gives guidelines on how color should and should not be used without defining a particular scheme, and that it is easy to do more harm than good. I could not read the full text, so the numbered recommendations below are the standard principles of the human-factors color-coding literature that such reports summarize, and they should be checked against the report before the book quotes them as its own.

**Specifics.** The common elements of aviation display color guidance, to be verified against the report: limit the number of colors used for coding (commonly about six at most, fewer being better); use color as a redundant cue with shape, position, size or text so that information is not lost for color-deficient users or in poor lighting; reserve red for warnings and emergencies and amber or yellow for cautions; use green for normal or safe status; keep meanings consistent across screens and systems; make colored symbols differ from the background in luminance, not only hue; avoid saturated blue for small text and thin lines because the eye's short-wavelength sensitivity is poor for fine detail; avoid adjacent saturated colors from opposite ends of the spectrum, which produce chromostereopsis (a depth illusion) and eye strain; and account for the roughly 8 percent of men with color deficiency. The report belongs to a lineage that includes FAA human factors design standards for ATC (HF-STD-010A, 2020, confirmed by search as the current standard) and cockpit alerting conventions in FAA advisory circulars (AC 25-11 for transport-category electronic displays). The 2006 FAA Civil Aerospace Medical Institute report DOT/FAA/AM-06/15 cites it as a general source and notes the absence at that time of a formal color-use requirement.

**Criticism and status.** This is consensus guidance, not a controlled experiment, and it addresses ATC displays with trained operators, not cockpit instruments and not learners. It is the right type of source for LTF's status colors because it comes from the safety domain the app teaches, and its core rules (redundant coding, restraint, consistency, red for danger only) are corroborated by Christ (1975) and by ANSI Z535 and ISO 3864 safety-color standards, which are outside this lane's confirmed sources.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Mirror cockpit semantics: red for warnings, amber for cautions, green for normal, white and cyan or blue for information. (2) Restrict each theme to a small, fixed semantic palette. (3) Never encode state by color alone; add a word or icon, as a real annunciator does with text. (4) Reserve red so that it keeps its force: a lesson app that uses red for decoration or minor errors dilutes the cockpit meaning. (5) Quote the report only after the full text is read.

## Okabe2002Color
**Core claims.** Okabe and Ito's Color Universal Design guide, from the University of Tokyo group, teaches how to make figures that are readable for people with all common types of color vision, without making them ugly for people with typical vision. The guide proposes a recommended set of eight colors that stay mutually distinguishable under protan, deutan and tritan simulation, and recommends redundant cues such as line style, shape and direct labels.

**Specifics.** The palette (Okabe and Ito's names and the values widely reproduced; values from memory, to be rechecked on the original): black (0, 0, 0), orange (230, 159, 0), sky blue (86, 180, 233), bluish green (0, 158, 115), yellow (240, 228, 66), blue (0, 114, 178), vermilion (213, 94, 0), reddish purple (204, 121, 167), in RGB. The guide's rules include: do not rely on red against green or on green against brown; choose a bright red-orange (vermilion) rather than a pure red so that it is separable from green for protan viewers; prefer blue-green to pure green; use light and dark contrast as well as hue; add labels instead of legends where possible; and avoid thin lines of pale colors. The group (the J*FLY materials on the University of Tokyo and later Cologne hosting) developed and checked the recommendations with color-blind scientists and simulation, not with a formal trial.

**Criticism and status.** The guide is not peer reviewed and is aimed at figures; it does not cover the visual hierarchy of whole interfaces. Even so, the palette is the most widely adopted CVD-safe categorical palette and has been recommended in journals. One limitation is that eight hues cannot all be distinguished by every viewer in every size or in small marks, so labels remain necessary.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Use the Okabe-Ito palette for any widget with categorical lines or markers (speed, altitude, flap settings in a chart). (2) For a good/bad pair, use bluish green and vermilion, with icons, in place of green and red. (3) Make blue and orange the default contrast pair for "this" versus "that". (4) Add direct labels and patterns on charts. (5) Check each semantic color of LTF's own palette against this reference with a simulator.

## Brettel1997Computerized
**Core claims.** Brettel, Viénot and Mollon give a computational method to simulate how colors appear to dichromats (people who lack one of the three cone types) so that normal-vision viewers can see an approximation of what a protanope, deuteranope or tritanope sees. The method is based on the reports of rare unilateral dichromats, who have one dichromatic eye and one normal eye and can compare the two. It is the basis for the dichromacy simulations built into many design tools.

**Specifics.** In the cone-response (LMS) space, colors for a dichromat project onto a two-dimensional surface made of two half-planes joined along the achromatic axis, anchored by two stimuli that appear unchanged to both normal and dichromatic observers (the neutral axis and one invariant wavelength, about 475 nm and 575 nm anchors in the usual statement; the exact anchors are from memory). For protanopes and deuteranopes the plane is determined by the anchors and white; the construction is done in a linear color space (this matters in practice, because simulating in gamma-encoded RGB gives wrong results). Different anchors give the tritan case. The simulation preserves luminance structure and collapses the red-green dimension to a yellow-blue one for protan and deutan types.

**Criticism and status.** Brettel et al. (1997) is the foundation, but it models dichromats only; most people with color vision deficiency are anomalous trichromats whose loss is partial, which Machado et al. (2009) address by adding a severity parameter. Simulations show the average case, and individual experience varies with age, lighting and training. The original report's page range and DOI are from memory (J. Opt. Soc. Am. A 14(10), 2647 to 2655) and were not web-confirmed this session.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Choose a CVD simulator that uses the Brettel or Viénot dichromacy model in linear RGB, for example the built-in browser dev-tools emulation. (2) Check every status color pair in protan and deutan modes, since red-green confusions are the most common. (3) Remember that simulation suggests confusability but is not a substitute for a color-deficient tester. (4) Use luminance difference as the first defense; hue difference can be lost. (5) Make a standing check in the design review: all semantic states must remain distinguishable in the simulation.

## Machado2009Physiologically
**Core claims.** Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes propose a physiologically based model that handles normal color vision, anomalous trichromacy (protanomaly, deuteranomaly, tritanomaly) and dichromacy in one framework with a continuous severity parameter. The model builds on stage theory of color vision, which combines cone responses and opponent processing, and on electrophysiological data. The result is a 3-by-3 matrix per deficiency type and severity, applied in linear RGB. The authors say it is the first model of its kind to cover all of these cases.

**Specifics.** Severity ranges from 0 (normal) to 1 (complete dichromacy) in steps (the published tables list steps of 0.1). Anomalous trichromacy is modeled by shifting the peak sensitivity of the affected cone toward that of another cone, as seen in the physiology of anomalous observers, by up to about 20 nm in the paper's standard statement (from memory). The authors validated the model in a user experiment with participants with and without color vision deficiency, comparing their judgments with simulated images; the sample was small. The paper is widely implemented (for instance in browser dev-tools emulation and in common simulators). The authors state that color vision deficiency affects about 200 million people worldwide.

**Criticism and status.** Like all simulations it yields an average, not an individual, appearance, and it cannot represent color naming, adaptation or the use of other cues. The small validation limits the strength of claims about accuracy. Prevalence: about 8 percent of males and 0.5 percent of females of Northern European ancestry have some red-green deficiency (a figure also given in Wong 2011); deuteranomaly is the most common type.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Test with severity 1.0 for the worst case and with an intermediate value for anomalous trichromacy. (2) Add simulation screenshots of key widgets (gauges, checklists, quiz feedback) to design review. (3) Accept that blue-yellow deficits are rare but check tritan too. (4) Rely on luminance contrast between states. (5) Provide non-color cues for every state change.

## Wong2011Color
**Core claims.** Bang Wong's "Points of view" column in Nature Methods tells scientists how to make figures readable for red-green color-blind colleagues. The advice: vary lightness and saturation as well as hue, choose palettes designed for color-blind viewers, simulate your figure, and add redundant cues. The column also makes the point that accessible color choices are good design practice in general. It includes an eight-color palette that red-green color-blind people can tell apart (this is essentially Okabe and Ito's set).

**Specifics.** Confirmed from excerpts: the column gives prevalence figures of about 8 percent of men and 0.5 percent of women of Northern European ancestry for the common red-green form; it points to simulation software; and it recommends differences in lightness and saturation as well as hue. From memory and not confirmed here: the column's suggestion to replace red and green overlays in fluorescence microscopy with magenta and green, or with other safe pairs, and the idea to show monochrome versions of images. A companion July 2011 column extends the topic to graphical alternatives to color.

**Criticism and status.** It is a short expert column, not a study, and only excerpts were available. Its recommendations agree with the controlled work on dichromacy and with Okabe and Ito. Its impact came from its audience: it became the standard CVD reference in scientific publishing.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Replace every red/green pair with a pair that differs in lightness too, such as dark blue and light orange. (2) Make grayscale a test: if the states are distinguishable in grayscale, they are robust. (3) Prefer labels on the marks themselves to separate legends. (4) Keep the palette small and fixed. (5) Cite Wong when the team debates whether CVD design is worth the effort: it is also cleaner design.

## Legge1987Contrast
**Core claims.** Legge, Rubin and Luebker, in the "Psychophysics of reading" series from the University of Minnesota, measured how contrast and character size jointly control reading speed in normally sighted adults. Reading rate stays at its maximum across a wide range of high contrasts and then declines as contrast approaches threshold. When contrast is expressed in multiples of the reader's contrast threshold, the curves for different character sizes superimpose, which indicates that the size effects on reading rate reflect contrast sensitivity. The paper ties reading to the contrast sensitivity function of the visual system.

**Specifics.** Subjects read aloud lines of text drifting across a monitor (a rapid serial style, with text scrolled), and reading rate in words per minute was the measure. Character size and contrast were varied in factorial combinations. The abstract-level findings confirmed by search: reading rate is linked to the contrast sensitivity function; reading slows when text contrast drops below roughly 10 percent (reported by later papers citing this work); rate-versus-contrast curves superimpose when scaled in threshold units. From memory and not confirmed: a contrast of several times threshold (on the order of a log unit or more) is needed for fastest reading, which is why contrast thresholds for comfortable reading are set well above the minimum visible. The companion low-vision paper (Rubin and Legge 1989) found that readers with low vision need considerably higher contrast and reach their maximum rate more slowly.

**Criticism and status.** The sample was small and young with normal vision, the task was oral reading of moving text, and speed is a short-session measure; it does not capture comprehension, fatigue over long sessions or comfort. Its message, that readability is driven by luminance contrast and size relative to threshold, has been reproduced extensively and underlies guidelines such as WCAG's contrast ratios. The WCAG numbers themselves (4.5:1 and 7:1) are standards decisions informed by this literature, not direct outputs of this paper.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Make body text clear of the WCAG AA minimum with real margin; long lessons should aim at AAA (7:1) where the design allows. (2) Judge contrast by luminance difference, not hue difference. (3) Thin, small or light-weight text raises the contrast needed, so use medium weights for small sizes, especially in Night theme. (4) Do not use pale gray for information the learner needs, such as units, labels or hints. (5) Remember that cockpit-style use (sun glare, a second monitor) lowers effective contrast; build margin in.

## Legge1990Luminance
**Core claims.** Legge, Parish, Luebker and Wurm compared reading with luminance contrast and with chromatic (color) contrast. Their finding, in the series installment on color contrast, is that luminance contrast supports fast reading and chromatic contrast alone does not support reading as well: text defined only by color difference, at equal luminance, is read much more slowly or is much harder to see than text defined by luminance difference. Color adds little to the legibility of text beyond what luminance contrast provides.

**Specifics.** Bibliographic details were not web-confirmed (a lab file named for the paper confirms its existence; volume, pages and DOI are from memory). The approach, as I recall it: text was displayed with controlled color and luminance differences between letters and background, and reading rate was measured for both kinds of contrast across a range of contrast values. Contrast was scaled relative to threshold so luminance and chromatic stimuli could be compared. Chromatic contrast produced reading rates similar to luminance contrast only when scaled to be far above threshold; at modest levels it was inferior. The specific scaling factors and subject numbers are not confirmed.

**Criticism and status.** Small lab studies, with isoluminance difficult to achieve exactly across observers, since luminous efficiency varies per person. The principle that legibility of text depends mainly on luminance contrast (the magnocellular pathway's contribution to reading, in one explanation) is widely accepted and underlies the practice of testing color pairs in grayscale. It also explains why red on green or blue on red can be nearly unreadable though the hues differ strongly.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Test every foreground and background pair with a luminance-contrast calculation, not by eye on hue. (2) Do not rely on color alone to set off text from its background: orange on red, blue on purple or green on red can fail. (3) Colored status text must have contrast against its surface independent of its hue. (4) Convert designs to grayscale as a design-review step. (5) For people with a CVD, luminance contrast is exactly the cue that survives, so this principle serves accessibility as well.

## Piepenbrock2013Polarity
**Core claims.** Piepenbrock, Mayr and Buchner tested whether the advantage of dark characters on a light background over light characters on a dark background, known as the positive-polarity advantage, holds for older as well as younger adults. They found that it does: positive polarity gave better visual acuity and proofreading performance in both groups, and they recommend dark-on-light text irrespective of age. This contradicted the hypothesis that age-related changes, or findings from low-vision patients who often prefer reversed polarity, might remove or reverse the advantage in older observers.

**Specifics.** The paper reports three experiments (the abstract says the age test was Experiment 3) with younger and older adults. Measures included visual acuity and a proofreading task on computer displays. The proposed mechanism, quoted in outline from the abstract: older eyes have lower retinal illuminance because of age-related changes (smaller pupil, clouding of the lens), and brighter positive-polarity displays compensate for that. A later paper from the same group (Piepenbrock, Mayr and Buchner, Ergonomics 2014, "The advantage of positive text-background polarity is due to high display luminance") argues that the benefit is due mostly to the higher average luminance of positive-polarity displays, not to polarity as such. Sample sizes and effect sizes are not confirmed here. Print volume, issue and pages are not confirmed; the DOI was confirmed by search.

**Criticism and status.** The positive-polarity advantage is a robust finding in the lab for acuity, proofreading speed and accuracy, with a long history (Bauer and Cavonius 1980; Buchner and Baumgartner 2007). Counterpoints: people with some eye conditions (cataract, certain low-vision conditions), and users with photophobia, can prefer dark mode; dark mode can reduce emission and glare in dim rooms; and the lab tests used normal room lighting and short tasks, so they do not show that dark themes are worse in a dark room or over hours. Halation with light text on dark can blur letters for people with astigmatism, a mechanism that is plausible and widely reported but is not tested in this paper.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Make Day (dark text on light) the default for long lesson reading. (2) Offer Night as a first-class choice for dim study conditions rather than assume it improves comfort; avoid claiming it reduces eye strain. (3) In Night, use dark gray rather than pure black, off-white text and slightly heavier weights, and raise line spacing. (4) Follow the system preference but let the learner override. (5) Tune brightness: since luminance appears to carry the effect, make sure Night is not too dim for the learner's room.

## NNg2020DarkMode
**Core claims.** The Nielsen Norman Group article on dark mode summarizes user research and the literature. Its main points: dark mode is popular, can be more comfortable in low light and for some users with light sensitivity, but for people with normal vision, dark text on a light background generally gives better reading performance, especially for long passages. Dark mode can also cause halation (glowing edges) that blurs text for people with astigmatism. It advises offering the choice, not forcing a dark theme, and designing the dark theme with care rather than inverting colors.

**Specifics.** The article draws on interviews and on lab research on polarity (including the Piepenbrock work in this lane). Practical guidance in articles of this type, and in the Material Design dark theme guidance it echoes (not confirmed as part of this article): avoid pure black (#000000) and pure white (#FFFFFF) because of the strong contrast producing visual vibration and halation; use dark gray (commonly near #121212) and slightly off-white text; desaturate and lighten accent colors so they do not vibrate against dark surfaces; indicate elevation by lighter surfaces instead of shadows; use sufficient font weight and avoid very thin text; test contrast ratios in both themes. The individual author of the article was not confirmed this session, so the source is credited to the organization; the URL was confirmed by search.

**Criticism and status.** Practitioner synthesis, not peer reviewed; claims about user preference come from small qualitative studies. Aligned with controlled evidence on positive polarity but also reports user reasons for choosing dark mode (aesthetic preference, comfort at night, battery life on OLED displays, which is a device-specific benefit). A 2023 Ergonomics paper on dark mode is mentioned in search results as "Dark mode vogue", but its authors and venue could not be confirmed, so it is not cited.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Design Night as its own theme with its own tuned tokens; do not invert Day. (2) Reduce accent chroma and raise lightness in Night. (3) Show elevation through surface lightness steps. (4) Verify contrast ratios of every token pair in both themes, and test with an astigmatism-sensitive reader if possible. (5) Never claim that dark mode is healthier; claim only that it is a comfort choice.

## W3C2023WCAG22
**Core claims.** The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2, a W3C Recommendation, define testable success criteria for accessible web content, including four that govern color and contrast: 1.4.1 Use of Color (color must not be the only visual means of conveying information), 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), 1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced), and 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast. They are written to be conformance levels, A, AA and AAA, referenced in laws such as the European standard EN 301 549 and in US accessibility regulation. Bibliographic details were not web-confirmed this session.

**Specifics.** The contrast ratio is (L1 + 0.05) divided by (L2 + 0.05), where L1 and L2 are the relative luminances of the lighter and darker colors computed from linearized sRGB channels with weights of about 0.2126 for red, 0.7152 for green and 0.0722 for blue. Ratios run from 1:1 to 21:1. AA requires 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (at least 18 point, or 14 point bold); AAA requires 7:1 and 4.5:1. Success criterion 1.4.11 requires 3:1 for user-interface component boundaries and states, and for graphical objects needed to understand content. Version 2.2 added focus-appearance and target-size criteria (2.4.11, 2.5.8 with a 24 by 24 CSS pixel minimum at AA). Criterion 1.4.12 sets text-spacing adaptability. Logos and purely decorative text are exempt.

**Criticism and status.** The WCAG 2 contrast ratio is known to be an imperfect model of legibility: it ignores font weight and size beyond the single large-text threshold, tends to overstate the contrast of some dark-mode pairs and to understate that of some mid-tone pairs, and treats light-on-dark and dark-on-light symmetrically even though perception is not symmetric. The proposed Advanced Perceptual Contrast Algorithm (APCA), developed by Andrew Somers for a WCAG 3 draft, tries to address this but is contested and is not a conformance standard. For legal and procurement purposes WCAG 2.x ratios are the reference.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Treat AA as the floor and use AAA (7:1) for lesson body text where possible. (2) Apply 3:1 to icons, input borders, chart marks and focus rings. (3) Satisfy 1.4.1 by pairing color with text or icons in checklists, quiz feedback and gauge limits. (4) Verify tokens in both themes automatically in CI. (5) Use APCA only as a secondary advisory check, never as the pass-or-fail rule.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** First, legibility rests on luminance contrast, not hue difference (Legge 1987 and 1990, WCAG 2.2, Cardosi and Hannon). Second, color should convey meaning through learned convention and always with a redundant cue such as an icon, label or shape (WCAG 1.4.1, Cardosi and Hannon, Okabe and Ito, Wong). Third, lightness and chroma matter more than hue for felt emotion and preference, and effects are modest and context-dependent (Valdez and Mehrabian, Palmer and Schloss, Whitfield and Wiltshire, Elliot and Maier). Fourth, color-emotion associations have a large shared core with local variation (Jonauskaite et al., Madden et al.), which supports using conventional status colors.

**Where they conflict.** The red-impairs-performance and blue-creative, red-detail claims (Elliot 2007, Mehta and Zhu 2009) collide with failed replications (Steele 2014 and others); a later meta-analysis finds a small red effect, probably inflated. Dark mode is contested: controlled lab work favors dark text on light for acuity and proofreading (Piepenbrock), user reports favor dark in dim rooms (Nielsen Norman Group), and the luminance explanation suggests the gap may narrow when a dark theme is well tuned. WCAG ratios are the legal standard even though APCA argues they mis-model dark themes.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Set text contrast by luminance: AA is the floor, aim for 7:1 for lesson body text, 3:1 for icons, borders and chart marks.
2. Never encode state by color alone; add an icon and a word, as a real annunciator does.
3. Keep a small semantic palette that mirrors the cockpit: red for warning only, amber for caution, green for normal, cyan or blue for information.
4. Replace red-green pairs with vermilion and bluish green or blue and orange, and differ in lightness too (Okabe-Ito).
5. Test each theme in protan, deutan and tritan simulation (Brettel, Machado) and in grayscale.
6. Do not make or repeat mood or performance claims for color; they are unreplicated.
7. Give identity through one disciplined signature accent and restrained neutrals, since blue alone reads competent but generic (Labrecque and Milne).
8. Default to Day for long reading; build Night as its own theme (dark gray, desaturated accents, off-white text, heavier weights).
9. Save saturation for what needs attention; use low chroma on large surfaces (Valdez and Mehrabian).
10. Prefer measured outcomes in LTF's own data to color folklore, and record replication status next to every color claim in the book.
