## Sweller1988
**Core claims.** John Sweller's 1988 paper in Cognitive Science is the originating paper of cognitive load theory (CLT). Its thesis is that conventional problem solving is a poor way for novices to learn. The dominant novice strategy is means-ends analysis: compare the current state with the goal, find a difference, and pick an operator that reduces it. That search keeps the goal, the current state, the difference and the candidate operators active together, and so occupies working memory. The capacity it uses is not available for building schemas, the long-term memory structures that let experts see a problem type at a glance and know the move. A learner can therefore solve problems, even many problems, and still learn little about their structure. Learning (schema acquisition) and performing (reaching the goal) come apart.

**Specifics.** The key empirical move is to change the problem format so that means-ends analysis is not possible. In goal-free problems, learners are not told a specific target ("find the value of angle X") but are asked to calculate as many unknowns as they can. Without a target there is nothing to measure the difference against, so learners move forward from givens, noticing which rules apply. In the experiments reported in this line of work (Sweller and colleagues, with geometry, trigonometry, physics and similar structured tasks), learners given goal-free problems made fewer errors and showed more evidence of schema acquisition than those given conventional goal-specific problems. The paper also frames the worked-example idea (worked examples from Sweller and Cooper 1985 follow the same logic): studying a solved problem shows the solution structure without the search cost. The measures are typically solution time, error counts, and the number of moves that reflect the use of the correct schema. I could not re-confirm sample sizes or exact test statistics, so these are not quoted.

The paper also fixes terminology that later work adopts: schemas as the units of knowledge; automation as the shift from controlled to automatic processing, freeing capacity; and the principle that instructional design should avoid activities that impose load unrelated to schema acquisition. Working memory limits are treated as a premise drawn from cognitive psychology rather than something the experiments measure. Later sources in this lane give numbers: Baddeley and Hitch's model for structure, and the general literature (Miller 1956, Cowan 2001) for the number of chunks. Those numbers belong to other lanes and are not attributed to this paper.

**Criticism and status.** The goal-free effect is real in well-structured domains with few possible operators and weak in open domains, where learners without a goal wander. The worked-example effect that grew out of the same logic has replicated widely and is a mainstay of CLT; it reverses for experienced learners (Kalyuga et al. 2003). De Jong (2010) argues that CLT explains results after the fact because load is not measured independently. The "load" in 1988 was inferred from performance differences, not measured. The cognitive-science vocabulary of schemas, chunks and automation is widely accepted, but a strict reading that working memory is the only bottleneck is contested.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Open with a fully worked flight for every maneuver (narrated, step by step) before the learner attempts it, because a beginner who tries to fly a pattern with only a goal ("land on the runway") spends their capacity on search, not on learning the structure of a landing.
2. Treat the learner's first sessions as capacity-starved. Do not add interface decisions (what does this button do, where is the menu) on top of the aviation content.
3. Use low-stakes, goal-free exploration inside the sim only after the schema exists ("watch what the nose does as you add power"), and ask the learner to describe what changed.
4. Teach recognition schemas by name (the scan: attitude, power, trim, cross-check) so later lessons can refer to one chunk rather than several steps.
5. When writing challenges, avoid giving only a final target; give the target plus the procedure and the tolerance, then fade the procedure.

## Baddeley1974
**Core claims.** Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch proposed working memory as a multicomponent system to replace the earlier single short-term store. A central executive controls attention and coordinates two storage systems: the phonological loop, which holds speech-based information through rehearsal, and the visuospatial sketchpad, which holds visual and spatial information. A third storage component, the episodic buffer, was added later by Baddeley (2000), linking working memory to long-term memory. The model matters to this lane because it supplies the rationale for the dual-channel assumption in multimedia learning: pictures and spoken words may load different subsystems, whereas pictures plus printed words compete for the visual system, and printed words converted to inner speech load the phonological loop as well.

**Specifics.** The evidence base in the chapter comes from dual-task experiments in which participants held a verbal load (such as a string of digits) while doing reasoning, comprehension or learning tasks. If working memory were a single store, a concurrent digit load should have produced a steep drop in performance; instead the decline was modest, suggesting separate resources for the verbal rehearsal and the reasoning processes. Later work (Baddeley, Thomson and Buchanan 1975, from memory, not confirmed) found the word-length effect: the number of words recalled in order is a function of how many can be said in roughly two seconds. The phonological similarity effect (confusable-sounding letters recalled worse) and the articulatory suppression effect (saying "the" repeatedly removes the effect) are standard demonstrations that the loop is speech-based. For the visuospatial sketchpad, tasks like tracking a moving spot disrupt visual imagery tasks more than they disrupt verbal tasks. The chapter is long and technical (it is about 40 pages) and presents these experiments to support the claim that the system has multiple parts. I could not confirm the exact page range this session, so it is omitted.

**Criticism and status.** The multicomponent model is the dominant textbook account but it has rivals. Cowan's embedded-processes model treats working memory as the activated part of long-term memory with a limited attentional focus, and it explains many of the same data without separate slave systems. Baddeley himself revised his model, and the strict separation of visual and verbal subsystems is weaker than the early dual-task results suggested, because tasks often use both. For instructional design, the step from "two subsystems" to "narration plus picture beats text plus picture" is an inference by Mayer and Sweller, supported by experiments in their own right (the modality effect), but not proven by the model. Effects from modality studies also reverse in some conditions (long or difficult narration; learner paced text), which a simple dual-store account would not predict without further assumptions.

**Use for LTF.**
1. When a lesson explains a spatial idea (the traffic pattern, the attitude indicator), prefer a diagram with short audio narration or a narrated animation, instead of a diagram with a paragraph under it. This is a testable hypothesis rather than a guarantee.
2. Do not show text and also read it out verbatim. Both go through the phonological route and compete.
3. During active flying the learner's eyes and spatial attention are used by the simulator. Use brief audio or very short written cues in fly mode, not paragraphs.
4. Keep spoken or written instruction chunks short enough to be held (a callout, a value and a unit); avoid asking for mental arithmetic mid-maneuver.
5. Remember that executive attention is limited too: avoid asking the learner to monitor two unrelated things (a checklist and a countdown timer) at once.

## Chandler1991
**Core claims.** Paul Chandler and John Sweller's 1991 paper in Cognition and Instruction examined how the format of instruction, not its content, changes learning, as a test of cognitive load theory. The central finding is the split-attention effect: when a learner must mentally integrate two or more sources of information that are each unintelligible alone (for example a diagram and a separate block of text that refers to the diagram), the work of finding, holding and matching them uses working memory that could have gone into understanding. Physically integrating the explanatory text into the diagram, so that each statement sits next to the part it describes, removes that overhead and improves learning, for novices, when both sources are required.

**Specifics.** The experiments used technical instruction: electrical and engineering diagrams with accompanying text, geometry material, and similar. In the integrated condition, text labels or statements were placed on or next to the relevant component. In the conventional condition, the diagram was on one side and the explanation below or aside. Outcomes reported across the experiments include test performance, time spent studying, and subjective ratings of difficulty; the integrated format typically led to better or faster performance and lower rated difficulty. A second finding is the boundary condition: when the diagram was self-explanatory and the text only repeated what the diagram conveyed, integration brought no benefit and could hurt, because the learner had to process redundant text. This is the root of the redundancy effect that Chandler and Sweller (1992) developed in a follow-up in the British Journal of Educational Psychology (not re-confirmed this session, so details are omitted). Ginns (2006) later ran a meta-analysis of the spatial integration effect in Learning and Instruction; I recall a medium-to-large pooled effect but the exact value was not confirmed this session and I do not quote it.

The mechanism is stated in terms of element interactivity: when the two sources of information interact (one is meaningless without the other), the learner must hold elements from both at once. Integration moves part of the matching job from the learner's working memory to the page layout. Three practical forms are described: labels on the diagram, numbered callouts that match a key (a weaker form because the match is still required), and sequential reveal in which each text appears when its part is highlighted.

**Criticism and status.** The effect is well replicated for novices and complex, interdependent material, and weak or absent for experts and simple material (an instance of expertise reversal). It also depends on the integration being done well. Cluttered labels, long leader lines, or occlusion of the diagram can add their own search cost. Studies on web pages with hyperlinks and on animation (temporal split attention) extend the idea but with mixed results. A common misreading is that all text near images helps; the effect holds when the information must be combined.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Label instruments on the instrument, with short leaders, not through a legend under the picture.
2. Place checklist and procedure steps beside the cockpit control or panel area they refer to.
3. For tap or hover explanations, show them adjacent to the target and keep them visible without scrolling away from the figure.
4. Keep captions inside the figure frame so the eye does not travel; do not repeat in the caption what the label already says.
5. In fly mode, where the learner's eyes move between app and simulator, avoid layouts that need the learner to cross-reference two screen areas.

## Sweller1998
**Core claims.** Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas (1998), in Educational Psychology Review 10(3), pp. 251-296, is the standard statement of cognitive load theory of its first period. The paper builds the theory on a cognitive architecture: a very large long-term memory that stores schemas, a working memory that is limited in capacity and duration when dealing with novel information, but able to handle large amounts of familiar, schema-organized information as if it were a single element. Learning is the construction and automation of schemas. Instruction should be designed to place the learner's limited working memory resources on that task. The paper distinguishes three kinds of load: intrinsic load, set by the number of interacting elements in the material (element interactivity) and the learner's expertise; extraneous load, imposed by the way information is presented; and germane load, the effort that goes into schema construction.

**Specifics.** The article reviews a set of instructional effects and the experiments behind each, and links each effect to load: the goal-free effect, the worked-example effect, the completion-problem effect (partially solved problems), the split-attention effect, the redundancy effect, and the variability effect (varied problems promote schema formation). It discusses measurement through subjective rating scales (the nine-point mental effort scale associated with Paas), performance measures, dual-task methods, and physiological measures, and introduces efficiency scores that combine performance and effort. Element interactivity defines intrinsic load: learning chemical symbols one by one has low interactivity, whereas learning to balance an equation involves many interacting elements. The paper also sets out implications for design of complex tasks that van Merriënboer later builds into the 4C/ID model, and it contains the idea that as learners gain expertise, guidance should fade. The authors review studies from mathematics, physics, computer programming and technical training.

The same authors published a follow-up, "Cognitive architecture and instructional design: 20 years later," in Educational Psychology Review 31(2), 261-292 (2019, confirmed in search), which refines the theory. The main changes are to treat germane load as the allocation of working memory resources to intrinsic load rather than a third independent source, and to ground the architecture in evolutionary educational psychology (biologically primary knowledge, acquired without instruction, versus biologically secondary knowledge such as reading, which needs instruction). That evolutionary framing is also a point of criticism because it is hard to test.

**Criticism and status.** The paper is among the most cited in the field. It is also a review by the proponents, so the selection of studies is not independent. De Jong (2010) criticizes the circular definition of germane load and the weak measurement of load types. Most effects come from short laboratory tasks with novices and well-structured domains; evidence for long-term authentic learning settings is thinner. Meta-analytic work on single effects (for example worked examples, modality) supports the direction of the effects.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Count element interactivity per lesson step; a step that introduces a new instrument, a new control and a new term at once is too heavy for a beginner.
2. Remove extraneous load first (decorative art, split labels, unclear navigation) before adding anything.
3. Sequence from isolated elements to interacting ones: airspeed indicator alone, then airspeed with pitch and power.
4. Use completion problems (a half-filled checklist, a partly flown pattern) between demonstration and independent practice.
5. Vary the practice scenarios (different runways, wind, weight) so schemas generalize, as the variability effect predicts.
6. Add a single mental effort question after a lesson as a cheap load measure.

## Mayer2001
**Core claims.** Richard Mayer's Multimedia Learning (Cambridge University Press, 2001; second edition 2009) presents the cognitive theory of multimedia learning (CTML) and a set of design principles drawn from experiments. The theory has three assumptions: dual channels (a visual-pictorial channel and an auditory-verbal channel, drawing on Paivio and Baddeley), limited capacity in each channel, and active processing, in which the learner selects relevant words and images, organizes them into coherent verbal and pictorial models, and integrates them with each other and with prior knowledge. The multimedia principle follows: people learn better from words and pictures than from words alone, provided the pictures are relevant and the design supports these processes.

**Specifics.** Mayer sorts principles by the type of processing they manage. Extraneous processing (wasted effort) is reduced by coherence (exclude interesting but irrelevant material), signaling (cue the organization), redundancy (do not add on-screen text to narrated graphics for most learners), spatial contiguity (place words near corresponding graphics) and temporal contiguity (present corresponding narration and pictures at the same time). Essential processing (needed to understand the material) is managed by segmenting (give the lesson in learner-paced parts), pre-training (teach the names and characteristics of the key components first) and modality (use spoken rather than printed words with graphics). Generative processing (the learner's sense making) is fostered by personalization (conversational rather than formal style), voice (a friendly human voice rather than a machine voice) and, in later editions, embodiment, and the learner's own generative activities. Typical experiments: a short narrated animation on how lightning forms, how a bicycle pump works, or how car brakes work, followed by a retention test (write down what you remember) and a transfer test (solve novel problems, such as how to make lightning less intense). Mayer reports effect sizes as medians across studies, mostly in the range of about 0.4 to 1.0 or above; the individual medians I recalled earlier (for example around 0.8 for coherence, spatial contiguity and modality) are not confirmed this session and should be cited from the book or the Cambridge Handbook table before use.

**Criticism and status.** Most of the experiments come from Mayer's lab or close collaborators, use college students new to the topic, short lessons, and immediate tests. Several principles have boundary conditions that Mayer himself reports: the modality and redundancy principles weaken or reverse for learners with prior knowledge, for very difficult or foreign-language material, for long segments, and when the text is short and displayed with learner control. The dual-channel premise is disputed (Schnotz's integrated model of text and picture comprehension uses descriptive and depictive representations instead), and Sweller's group treats several of the principles as consequences of CLT. De Jong (2010) and others note the narrow set of materials. Reviews in the field note a pattern of strong effects in the lab and smaller or less consistent effects in classrooms.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Pair words with relevant pictures for any spatial or mechanical idea; do not explain the cockpit with text alone.
2. Offer narration over the diagram as an option; do not repeat the narration as full text on screen (provide captions as an accessibility option, which is a separate need).
3. Pre-train names (pitch, bank, yaw, trim) before the lesson that uses them in motion.
4. Break long lessons into learner-paced segments with a clear "continue" control.
5. Write in a conversational second-person voice, which matches the app's copy rules.
6. Cut anything that does not serve the lesson objective (coherence), and test each principle on LTF's own audience before treating it as law.

## Mayer2003
**Core claims.** Mayer and Moreno (2003), Educational Psychologist 38(1), 43-52 (confirmed), start from the cognitive theory of multimedia learning and ask what to do when the learner's intended cognitive processing exceeds the learner's available capacity (cognitive overload). The abstract describes five overload scenarios and, for each, one or two theory-based suggestions for reducing load, with a summary of the research testing each. The article's conclusion is that cognitive load is a central design consideration in multimedia instruction. It is the clearest bridge between the theory and a practical checklist.

**Specifics.** The nine suggestions are commonly grouped by scenario. Scenario 1: both channels are fine but one is overloaded by essential processing; here the remedy is off-loading, moving some processing from the overloaded channel to the other (for example presenting words as narration rather than on-screen text when the visual channel is already busy with an animation); this is the modality effect. Scenario 2: both channels are overloaded by essential processing; the remedies are segmenting (present the lesson in learner-paced parts) and pre-training (give the names and behaviors of the components first). Scenario 3: essential processing plus extraneous processing arising from material that is interesting but irrelevant; the remedy is weeding (eliminate extraneous material) and signaling (give cues to the structure of the essential material). Scenario 4: extraneous processing arising from confusing presentation, where the learner must search; the remedies are aligning (place printed words next to the part of the picture they describe) and eliminating redundancy (do not present the same words as both narration and on-screen text). Scenario 5: extraneous processing from the need to hold information in working memory until the other part arrives; the remedy is synchronizing (present narration and animation simultaneously rather than one after the other). The paper reports research for each: for instance comparisons of narrated animation versus animation with on-screen text, and simultaneous versus successive presentation, scored on retention and transfer tests. A secondary source cites a median effect size of about 0.69 for eliminating redundancy; I have not been able to confirm that number against the paper and so do not rely on it.

Notable details: the framework depends on the learner being a novice working with system-paced, short, complex material. The paper acknowledges that segmenting and pre-training matter most when material is fast and complex, and that the overload categories are theoretical (load is inferred from learning outcomes, not measured).

**Criticism and status.** The principles have been tested repeatedly and several have meta-analytic support (Ginns 2005 on modality and 2006 on contiguity; Schneider et al. 2018 on signaling, with effect sizes I could confirm only at second hand). Boundary conditions are real: narrated text can be worse for fast-paced, long or difficult narration, for learners with hearing or language limits, and for learners who want to review. Expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2003) applies to most of these remedies. The five-scenario taxonomy is a convenient teaching device, but few studies measure which scenario a learner is in.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use the five scenarios as a design review: ask of every widget which channel is loaded, and whether the load is essential or extraneous.
2. For an animated explanation (lift and angle of attack, the traffic pattern), narrate or highlight in sync rather than putting a paragraph under it.
3. Weed: remove decorative aircraft art around quizzes and checklists.
4. Align: put the label on the part; synchronize: highlight the part when it is named.
5. Pre-train terms in a short glossary strip before the first motion explanation.
6. Provide a transcript and captions as accessibility alternatives, accepting that for deaf learners the modality advice does not apply.

## Kalyuga2003
**Core claims.** Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller (2003), Educational Psychologist 38(1), 23-31 (confirmed), review a pattern in cognitive load research: instructional techniques that are highly effective with inexperienced learners can lose their effectiveness and even have negative consequences when used with more experienced learners. They call this the expertise reversal effect. In CLT terms, learning reduces working memory limits because schemas in long-term memory let the learner process information more efficiently. For a novice, an explanation or integrated label supplies information the learner lacks. For an expert, the same material duplicates what the expert's schema already provides, and the expert must reconcile the external information with the internal knowledge, which adds extraneous load.

**Specifics.** The studies reviewed include worked examples versus problem solving, integrated versus split-source diagrams and text, text with redundant explanations, and guidance in a variety of domains (electrical engineering, mathematics, computer use, apprenticeship-like tasks). The recurring signature is an interaction: design A beats design B for low-knowledge learners, the difference shrinks as knowledge rises, and in many studies it reverses. Typical illustrations: novices learned better from a diagram with integrated text; more knowledgeable learners did as well or better from the diagram alone, because the text was redundant. Worked examples helped novices, but as skill grew, problem solving became better, which led to the practice of guidance fading (gradually replacing worked steps with problems to solve). The remedy proposed is adaptive instruction: measure the learner's expertise and match the format to it. Kalyuga and colleagues developed rapid diagnostic tests in which the learner sees a problem state and indicates the next step; these take seconds and correlate with longer tests, so they can place a learner without a long pretest. The authors suggest dynamic adjustment, since expertise changes within a session. Kalyuga (2007) extended the review in Educational Psychology Review (details not re-confirmed this session).

**Criticism and status.** The pattern replicates across many studies, but the expertise reversal is a gradual trend rather than a threshold; the knowledge level at which it occurs depends on the domain and the specific technique. Poor diagnosis of expertise can make adaptive systems worse than a fixed design. Most work is in structured technical domains. There is limited evidence in domains where learners' prior knowledge is spotty (a hobbyist who knows weather but not instruments), which describes many flight learners.

**Use for LTF.**
1. LTF will have complete beginners and returning or knowledgeable users. Provide a "skip" and "I know this" route, and a way to collapse hints.
2. Fade checklists and prompts as accuracy rises; show the full checklist first, then a skeleton, then none.
3. Add a one-question placement check at the start of a module ("what happens to airspeed if you pitch down with the same power?") to choose the starting version.
4. Fly mode, used during practice, should be leaner than the lesson, because by then the learner has seen the explanation.
5. Allow users to hide labels and tutorial overlays on widgets, and remember the choice.
6. Because a learner's prior knowledge may be uneven (strong in weather, weak in instruments), adapt by topic, not by one global level.

## Kirschner2006
**Core claims.** Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006), Educational Psychologist 41(2), published a long argument that instruction with minimal guidance, such as discovery learning, problem-based learning, inquiry learning, experiential learning and constructivist-inspired approaches, does not work well for novices and intermediate learners. The argument rests on cognitive architecture: working memory is limited when processing novel information, long-term memory stores schemas, and novices who are asked to search a problem space spend their limited capacity on search rather than on schema construction. Direct, strongly guided instruction that explains concepts, shows procedures and provides worked examples is more effective and more efficient.

**Specifics.** The paper reviews decades of controlled comparisons across domains. It cites the worked-example effect, studies of programming, and experiments on science process skills (for example work by Klahr and Nigam on teaching control of variables, in which direct instruction outperformed discovery for the learning of the strategy). It argues that the expertise of a scientist, who can work by inquiry, is a different thing from the learning of a novice, and that the constructivist inference "learn as the discipline works" is a confusion between epistemology and pedagogy. It also notes that unguided learners may acquire misconceptions or incomplete knowledge, that learners often prefer unguided formats yet learn less, and that the more guided groups in the cited studies were usually ahead on tests. The authors also acknowledge that for experts, additional guidance can be useless or harmful, which is the expertise reversal effect. I could not re-confirm specific effect sizes or study counts and do not quote any.

**Criticism and status.** The paper provoked a response in the same journal (Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn 2007; Schmidt, Loyens, van Gog and Paas 2007), arguing that problem-based and inquiry learning are in fact heavily scaffolded and so do not fall under "minimal guidance". Others, such as Kapur's work on productive failure, report that attempting problems before instruction can improve conceptual learning and transfer in some tasks, which gives a qualified counter-case. The paper is a position article with a selective review, not a meta-analysis. The central claim is strongest for novices learning well-structured content and weaker for ill-structured domains and for expert learners. It remains one of the most cited and debated papers in educational psychology.

**Use for LTF.**
1. For a first encounter with a maneuver, show and explain it first; let the learner try only after they have seen the sequence and the targets.
2. Label free-flight practice as the final stage, entered after guided stages, not as the starting point.
3. In-sim challenges should state the goal, the procedure and the tolerances, with a worked demonstration available.
4. Do not infer learning from enjoyment: pair engagement features with performance checks.
5. Build scaffolds that fade (hints, then prompts, then nothing) in place of an empty sandbox.
6. Keep a "try it first" option for experienced learners, informed by the productive-failure literature, but do not make it the default.

## Harp1998
**Core claims.** Harp and Mayer (1998), Journal of Educational Psychology 90(3), tested why adding interesting but irrelevant material ("seductive details") to a scientific explanation reduces learning. Three hypotheses compete. The arousal view predicts that interesting details increase attention and so improve learning. The distraction view says details draw attention from the main ideas. The disruption view says details break the coherence of the text so that the learner cannot build a connected structure. A third, the diversion view, says that details prime inappropriate prior knowledge, which the learner then uses to organize the material. Harp and Mayer's results favor diversion over arousal, and their theory is of "cognitive interest": interest from understanding the material, as against "emotional interest" from vivid details.

**Specifics.** The materials were a short explanation of how lightning forms, with versions that added interesting-but-irrelevant illustrations and text (for example, facts about lightning striking people or aircraft, or photographs and captions about such events). Participants were college students who knew little about meteorology. Tests included retention (recall of main points) and transfer (problem solving questions). Across a series of experiments the authors compared versions with and without seductive text and illustrations, versions with the seductive material placed before the main explanation versus after, and versions with highlighting of main ideas. The pattern reported: learners who got the seductive details recalled fewer main ideas and performed worse on transfer than those who got the base lesson; the harm was greater when the seductive material came first (consistent with priming the wrong schema) and when it was in the form of illustrations; adding signaling (highlighting) to the main points reduced the damage. I could not re-confirm sample sizes, effect sizes or experiment numbering, so I do not give them.

The paper's contribution is a mechanism: details do not just waste time, they steer how the learner organizes the lesson. It also gives one of the tests (rating of interest) showing that the seductive version was rated more interesting but learned less, so liking and learning diverged.

**Criticism and status.** Later syntheses (Rey 2012; Sundararajan and Adesope 2020) support a negative effect on average, but with moderators: stronger for low prior knowledge, system-paced presentation, and irrelevant pictures; weaker or absent when the details relate to the topic or when learners control pace. A few studies find motivational benefits of interesting details with no learning cost. The experiments are short and use a small set of passages, so generalization to long courses is uncertain.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Do not put anecdotes or fun facts before the concept they decorate; if used, place them after the main explanation and label them as asides.
2. Test each piece of rich media (hero images, stock photos of aircraft) against the lesson objective and cut those that do not serve it.
3. When a story is used (an accident case study), tie it to the principle it teaches, since topical details do less harm than irrelevant ones.
4. Highlight the main point so the seductive material is visually subordinate.
5. Do not use learner ratings of "interesting" as evidence that a lesson works; add a performance check.

## Rey2012
**Core claims.** Günter Rey's review and meta-analysis (Educational Research Review 7(3), 216-237, 2012, confirmed) synthesizes experiments on the seductive detail effect: the finding that adding interesting but irrelevant material to a lesson reduces learning. The meta-analysis, based on 39 experimental effects covering text passages, illustrations and other types of seductive details, found a significant effect, with a small-to-medium effect on retention and a medium effect on transfer (confirmed from a summary of the paper's abstract).

**Specifics.** Rey reviews the theoretical accounts: the arousal theory (details increase attention), the distraction, disruption and diversion hypotheses introduced by Harp and Mayer (1998), and cognitive load explanations (details use working memory that should go to the main content). He analyses moderators including the form of the details (text, pictures, sounds or music), whether presentation is system paced or learner paced, the learner's prior knowledge and working memory capacity, and the type of outcome measure. The reported patterns (from my reading of the abstract and secondary summaries, with exact moderator values not confirmed): effects are larger for transfer than for retention; harm appears larger when presentation is system paced; and learners with lower prior knowledge or working memory capacity appear more vulnerable. Rey also discusses a methodological problem: the term "irrelevant" is hard to define, and some studies label details as seductive when they are loosely related to the content. A later meta-analysis, Sundararajan and Adesope (2020) in Educational Psychology Review, titled "Keep it coherent," confirmed a negative effect on learning and examined additional moderators.

**Criticism and status.** There is heterogeneity between studies, and many are small. The result is not that every extra element harms learning; it concerns interesting, irrelevant elements. Studies on emotional design (Um 2012, Brom 2018) are not in conflict, because there the affective features are built into the instructional graphics instead of adding new content. Some authors (for example Park and colleagues, whose work I recall but could not confirm) argue that seductive details can have positive effects on motivation or on recall in some conditions. The overall picture is that the coherence principle is a good default for novices, not an absolute law.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Run a coherence audit before publishing each lesson: for each element on the page, record which learning objective it serves; remove or demote the rest.
2. Let the learner control pace so that any necessary extras do less damage.
3. Be stricter with the first lessons, where learners have little prior knowledge.
4. Keep decorative sound and motion off by default in lessons (motion and sound also matter for accessibility).
5. Distinguish brand and atmosphere (theme, tone, color) from added content, since the evidence is about added content.
6. When adding a topical story, keep it short and connected to the lesson's rule.

## Tversky2002
**Core claims.** Tversky, Morrison and Bétrancourt (2002), International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 57(4), 247-262 (confirmed), review the evidence on whether animated graphics improve learning over static graphics. Their answer: the evidence for a general advantage is thin, because many experiments confound animation with other differences, such as extra information, interactivity, or different procedures. They state two principles for effective graphics: the congruence principle (the structure and content of the external representation should correspond to the desired structure and content of the internal representation) and the apprehension principle (the structure and content of the external representation should be readily and accurately perceived and comprehended). Animation often violates apprehension because it is too fast or too complex to be perceived accurately, and may violate congruence because people tend to conceptualize continuous events as discrete steps.

**Specifics.** The paper reviews studies on animations of mechanical systems (pulleys, brakes, pumps), biological systems, and algorithms or computer programs. Many compared an animation with a single static picture, which is an unfair comparison since a well-designed series of static diagrams can convey sequence. The authors report cases where animation helped, particularly when the task concerns movement, change or procedures, such as learning to tie knots, where seeing the movement is the content, and where interactivity was provided. Cases where it did not help include complex systems with fast, simultaneous changes. They argue for design remedies: allow learner control (pause, replay, slow), segment the animation, use a series of key frames, and make the visual cues direct attention to the relevant part. They also discuss "the reorientation advantage": animation can help people keep track of changes in viewpoint or location over time. They emphasize that learners often watch the animation passively and overestimate their understanding.

**Criticism and status.** The paper's skeptical conclusion has been partly overtaken by meta-analyses showing a small but reliable advantage for animation over static pictures (Höffler and Leutner 2007: reported overall effect of about 0.37; Berney and Bétrancourt 2016: about 0.23), particularly for procedural-motor and representational animations. The confound critique remains valid, and several later studies (Mayer, Hegarty and others) tested the segmenting and interactivity recommendations. The principles are conceptual rather than quantified.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use animation where change over time is the content (airflow, the pattern legs, stall onset), and use annotated static panels where the content is structural (the panel layout).
2. Give every animation play, pause, scrub and speed controls; never autoplay a long loop that cannot be stopped.
3. Offer a keyframe strip (four to six stills) beside or below each animation so the learner can compare states.
4. Apply congruence: let the attitude indicator widget behave like the real instrument, with the horizon moving as the real display moves.
5. Apply apprehension: slow down fast events, and direct attention to the part that changes.
6. Do not assume learners understood because they watched; follow animations with a prediction or a check question.

## Hoffler2007
**Core claims.** Höffler and Leutner (2007), Learning and Instruction 17(6), 722-738 (confirmed), is the main quantitative answer to the question Tversky et al. raised. It synthesizes studies comparing instructional animation with static pictures, covering 26 primary studies and 76 pairwise comparisons published between 1973 and 2003 (confirmed). The overall result is a medium-sized advantage for animation; later reviews cite it as d = 0.37, though I have not re-checked the figure against the paper itself. The authors examine moderators, including the type of knowledge being tested and the type of animation, to explain where the advantage appears.

**Specifics.** The authors coded the animation's function and design. A key distinction is between representational animations (which depict the content, such as showing how a mechanism moves), decorational animations (which add visual interest but no content) and animations that guide attention. As I recall the paper, representational animation had the clearest benefits, while decorational animation gave little or none; the exact moderator values were not re-confirmed this session. Another distinction is the type of knowledge: procedural-motor knowledge (learning to perform a movement sequence such as tying a knot or assembling an object) tended to show the largest animation advantage, with declarative and problem-solving knowledge showing smaller effects (also from memory, not confirmed). The authors considered the realism of the animation (video-like versus schematic), whether the animation was accompanied by narration or text, and the comparison picture: a single static image versus a series of static images. They also discussed that learner interaction, such as controlling pace, may change the picture. The paper highlights possible confounds in the primary studies (differences in information content, instruction time and learner activity) that the meta-analysis cannot remove.

An update by Berney and Bétrancourt (2016, Computers and Education) found a smaller overall effect of Hedges's g about 0.23 from a larger set of studies (confirmed in search as a citation inside another source), and identified moderators such as the use of narration and the content type. These two meta-analyses together suggest a real but modest animation benefit that depends on design.

**Criticism and status.** The meta-analysis spans very different animations and tasks. Many included studies are small and short. Publication bias is possible. The result does not say that every animation helps; it says that on average, well-matched animations give a modest advantage, especially where movement is the subject. The broader literature (Tversky et al. 2002; Mayer's segmenting and signaling principles) suggests design details such as pacing, segmenting and cueing decide whether the benefit appears. Learners often overestimate their understanding from animation (an illusion of understanding), which test-based studies partly capture.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Show real motion for motor and procedural skills (how the yoke moves during a flare, how to trim): short, realistic clips or sim recordings are better than diagrams for those.
2. For conceptual explanations, a stepped static sequence may match an animation; choose animation only when motion carries information.
3. Avoid animation that only adds visual interest; it brings no benefit and has costs for attention and accessibility.
4. Use the sim's own visual style in clips so that what the learner sees in the lesson matches what they will see in the simulator.
5. Keep stills as a fallback for reduced-motion settings and low bandwidth, and test that the lesson works with the still alone.
6. Include narration with animation where a verbal explanation is needed, following the modality guidance.

## Um2012
**Core claims.** Um, Plass, Hayward and Homer (2012), Journal of Educational Psychology 104(2), tested the claim that the emotional tone of the visual design of a multimedia lesson affects learning. Their emotional design manipulation used warm colors and round, face-like shapes to induce positive emotion in learners. The key finding was that learners in the positive emotion design condition reported more positive emotions and scored higher on comprehension and transfer measures than those with a neutral design, and reported lower perceived difficulty. The paper argues against a pure cognitive load view in which any non-essential design feature is a distraction, proposing that positive affect can aid learning through motivation and a change in cognitive processing, and it gave rise to the "emotional design principle" in the Cambridge Handbook of Multimedia Learning (confirmed that the Handbook includes a chapter titled "Emotional design principle in multimedia learning").

**Specifics.** The lesson was a computer-based multimedia lesson on a science topic (I recall immunization or a similar biology topic; the exact topic was not confirmed). Students were university undergraduates. The neutral condition used grayscale and default shapes; the emotional design condition used warm saturated colors (yellow, orange) and anthropomorphic, rounded shapes in the graphics. The dependent measures were self-reported mood, perceived task difficulty, mental effort, intrinsic motivation, comprehension and transfer tests. Sample sizes were not confirmed this session and are omitted. In a subsequent paper, Plass, Heidig, Hayward, Homer and Um (2014, Learning and Instruction) varied shape and color separately, and found that the combination of warm color and round shape produced the strongest positive emotion and improved comprehension; the details of that study were not confirmed here. The effects were largest on comprehension, with transfer effects varying across experiments.

The theoretical point is that emotion in multimedia learning has been treated as a source of extraneous processing, while here design that induced a positive mood was integrated into the instructional graphic (the colors and shapes were part of the diagrams, not separate decorative items) and did not add content. That distinguishes it from seductive details.

**Criticism and status.** Replication is mixed. The Brom, Stárková and D'Mello (2018) meta-analysis found positive average effects of facial anthropomorphisms and pleasant colors, but with heterogeneity, small samples and signs that the self-reported mood did not clearly mediate the learning effects. Some independent attempts reported no effect. The manipulations tested are narrow (warm colors, round faces), so general claims about "beautiful" design improving learning go beyond the data. The learners were mostly undergraduates in short sessions, not adults learning a physical skill over weeks.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Warm, rounded treatment of key diagram elements is a defensible choice for a beginner audience that may feel anxious about flying; adopt it as part of the identity, not as added decoration.
2. Keep the affective features inside the instructional graphics, not as extra images around them.
3. Do not rely on mood effects for learning gains; treat them as a modest motivational support and test them in the app.
4. A "Night" theme should keep warmth (warm accent colors against dark surfaces) while meeting contrast requirements.
5. Measure outcomes with in-sim performance, not just "did you enjoy this".

## Brom2018
**Core claims.** Brom, Stárková and D'Mello (2018), Educational Research Review 25, 100-119 (confirmed), is the meta-analysis of emotional design in multimedia learning, restricted to facial anthropomorphisms (adding faces to graphic elements) and pleasant colors. It pooled 33 independent samples with a total of 2,924 participants (confirmed). The authors conclude that the manipulations appear to be useful design principles for learning, with small to medium cognitive effects and a robust effect on intrinsic motivation.

**Specifics.** Under a random-effects model, the paper reports significant positive effects for retention (k = 18, d+ = 0.387), comprehension (k = 14, d+ = 0.317) and transfer (k = 27, d+ = 0.327) (all confirmed from the search summary). On affective-motivational outcomes, the effect on intrinsic motivation was robust, the effect on liking or enjoyment was weaker, and the effect on positive affect was marginal. Perceptions of learning and effort were not significantly changed, but perceived difficulty was reduced. A notable observation is that self-reported positive affect did not clearly mediate the cognitive effects, which raises doubts about the proposed mechanism (design creates positive emotion that then improves learning); the authors discuss alternative accounts, such as increased motivation or social cues in the case of faces. They also examine moderators, including learning duration and the type of learner, and discuss heterogeneity and possible publication bias. Wong and Adesope (2021, Educational Psychology Review) later replicated and updated the meta-analysis with newly published studies (confirmed that this follow-up exists; its findings were not confirmed here).

**Criticism and status.** The pooled effects are small to moderate, measured in short laboratory settings, and based on a narrow definition of emotional design. Heterogeneity is considerable, so the average hides studies with no effect. The unclear mediation by emotion means the effects may come from something else (such as attention or the social cue of a face). The authors themselves frame the findings as promising and call for tests with longer lessons, other populations and other emotional design features.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Expect modest gains. Pleasant color and friendly forms are cheap, but they will not replace good explanation.
2. Use the motivational benefit deliberately: reduce the intimidation of cockpit complexity with approachable shapes and color.
3. Never trade contrast or legibility for "pleasant color"; check accessibility lanes for thresholds.
4. Do not use learner ratings of liking as proof of effectiveness, since the meta-analysis shows liking and learning effects differ in size.
5. If LTF adds a mascot or a face, evaluate it with a transfer test because the evidence for faces is mixed, and keep it from adding competing text.

## Schneider2018
**Core claims.** Schneider, Beege, Nebel and Rey (2018), "A meta-analysis of how signaling affects learning with media," Educational Research Review 23, 1-24 (confirmed), synthesizes the effect of signaling, that is, cues that guide attention to relevant information or highlight its organization (color coding, arrows, bold or underlined text, headings, spotlights, verbal emphasis), on learning. The conclusion is that signaling improves learning, with larger gains on retention than on transfer, and that the benefit is moderated by learner and material characteristics.

**Specifics.** A secondary summary reports about 103 studies and about 12,200 participants, with pooled effects of roughly g = 0.53 for retention and g = 0.33 for transfer; I could not confirm these against the paper, so treat them as not confirmed. The paper also examines attention measures (eye-tracking) and cognitive load measures, finding on the whole that signals increase attention to the signaled material and reduce perceived load. Moderators discussed in the literature include prior knowledge (learners with low prior knowledge benefit more), the complexity of the material, whether the presentation is system paced, the type of cue (for example color versus arrows versus labeling), and the number of cues. An earlier meta-analysis by Richter, Scheiter and Eitel (2016, Educational Research Review 17) addressed signaling in text-picture materials, and Mayer's earlier tables summarized the signaling principle as having a moderate effect. A practical point from this line of work is that signals work when they direct attention to relevant content and mirror its structure, and fail when they are too many or unrelated to the organization.

**Criticism and status.** The effect is moderate and varies with design. Signaling can attract attention (visible in eye movements) without improving learning, because attention to a feature does not guarantee understanding. Overcued displays make everything salient and thereby nothing. Experts may not need signals (expertise reversal). Publication bias is a possible concern, as in most meta-analyses of this field.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Spotlight the one instrument under discussion and dim the rest (a clear, structure-based cue).
2. Use consistent color coding: the V-speed arcs on the airspeed widget match the color of the matching terms in the text.
3. Limit cues per view to one or two; remove the cue once the point has been made.
4. Use headings, step numbers and progress indicators as structural signals in lessons.
5. Let advanced users switch cues off.
6. Check that color-based signals have a non-color backup for color-blind learners, per the accessibility lanes.

## Larkin1987
**Core claims.** Jill Larkin and Herbert Simon (1987), Cognitive Science 11, 65-99 (confirmed, both at Carnegie-Mellon), analyze why a diagram can be worth far more than the equivalent text. Their central claim is that two representations can be informationally equivalent (all the information in one can be derived from the other) and yet not computationally equivalent (the inferences are not equally easy or fast to make). A diagram's advantage lies in how it organizes information in space, supporting efficient search and perceptual inference. In their words, whether a diagram is worth 10,000 words depends on what productions are available for searching the representation (confirmed from the search summary).

**Specifics.** The authors build computational models using production systems, one that works from a diagram and one that works from sentences, and apply them to physics problems (a pulley system and a geometry problem) and others. They show that the diagrammatic model needs fewer search steps and less symbolic matching, for three reasons. First, locational indexing: information that is used together is stored near each other, so the process can find related elements without searching a list. Second, the diagram avoids the need to match symbolic labels, since the position of an element is itself its identity (it need not be named and then looked up). Third, perceptual inferences: some inferences (adjacency, connection, relative position) are available directly from perception with little effort, which in a sentential representation require explicit rules. They also note that diagrams are not always better: the advantage depends on the diagram being arranged to support the inferences needed. They open by noting that "a picture is worth ten thousand words" is attributed in Bartlett's Quotations to a Chinese proverb, but that on inquiry the proverb seems not to be Chinese (confirmed from the search summary). A later study (Cheng 2004) built an experiment on the pulley problem.

**Criticism and status.** The paper is a computational analysis, not a study of human learners, although it has been cited as theoretical support for empirical work on diagrams and mental models (for example Hegarty's work on mechanical reasoning, which I did not re-confirm). It does not say that any picture helps; poor diagrams force search too. It is foundational for information visualization and for the argument that representation format affects problem-solving cost.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Arrange related information together in space: airspeed, attitude, altitude and heading grouped as the learner will scan them in a real panel.
2. Design each diagram for the specific inference the learner must make ("which way is the nose pointing relative to the horizon?") instead of as a general illustration.
3. Replace lists of numbers with a spatial arrangement when the learner needs to compare (power setting against airspeed).
4. Keep text for rules, sequences and abstract relationships; combine it with a diagram where location matters.
5. Match the app's widget layout to the real cockpit layout, so that the spatial index the learner builds transfers to the sim.

## Ainsworth2006
**Core claims.** Shaaron Ainsworth (2006), "DeFT: A conceptual framework for considering learning with multiple representations," Learning and Instruction 16(3), 183-198 (confirmed), proposes that learning with multiple external representations (MERs) should be analyzed along three fundamental aspects: the design parameters (the number of representations, how information is distributed across them, their form, their sequence, and the support provided for translating between them), the functions that MERs serve, and the cognitive tasks learners must perform to learn from them. The framework is intended to identify factors that affect learning, reconcile inconsistent experimental findings, expose understudied areas and suggest design heuristics (confirmed from the abstract).

**Specifics.** The functions are three families. MERs can be complementary: they provide different information or support different processes (for example a graph for trends and a table for exact values). They can constrain interpretation: one representation, being familiar or inherently limited, restricts how another is interpreted (a concrete example constrains an abstract formula). They can construct deeper understanding: by prompting abstraction, extension of knowledge to a new case, or relation between representations. The cognitive tasks include learning how each representation works (its format and conventions), learning how it relates to the domain, and learning how to translate between representations. A key practical point: MERs impose costs. The learner must understand each representation and relate them, so they help only if the benefit exceeds the costs, and novices facing unfamiliar representations may be overloaded. This explains why some studies show benefits and others not. Design advice includes: use MERs for a purpose, provide translation support (color links, dynamic linking so that a change in one updates the other, co-location), introduce representations one at a time to novices and be careful with the order, and avoid having two representations carry the same information without a function. The paper reviews examples from dynamically linked science and mathematics simulations.

**Criticism and status.** The paper is a framework, so it offers hypotheses more than tests; its categories are broad and empirical support is patchy, domain-specific and not summarized quantitatively here. It has been extended in later reviews and in the Cambridge Handbook chapter on the multiple representations principle (confirmed that a chapter with this title exists). Its value is organizing a field that otherwise gives conflicting results.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Link representations dynamically: moving the throttle changes the engine gauge, the sound and a small graph together, making the relation visible.
2. Give each representation a function (the diagram shows where, the number shows how much, the sim clip shows what it feels like).
3. Support translation with consistent color and labels across the diagram, the text and the sim screenshot.
4. Introduce one representation at a time to beginners, adding the second only once the first is understood.
5. Avoid duplicate representations that do not add function, which cost the learner effort for no gain.
6. For fly mode, align the app's instrument depiction with what the sim shows so the learner does not have to translate.

## deJong2010
**Core claims.** Ton de Jong (2010), "Cognitive load theory, educational research, and instructional design: Some food for thought," Instructional Science 38, 105-134, is the main scholarly critique of cognitive load theory by a respected learning-sciences researcher. It credits CLT with producing useful instructional guidance but argues that the theory has conceptual and methodological problems. I could not re-confirm the page numbers or the DOI this session; the volume and issue are from memory and are not confirmed, so the sources file leaves pages blank.

**Specifics.** As I recall the argument, de Jong raises several issues. The load types (intrinsic, extraneous, germane) are hard to separate in practice, and germane load in particular is defined in a way that makes it nearly any learning-related effort, which risks circularity. Load measures (subjective rating scales, secondary-task performance, physiological indicators) do not distinguish the types and may reflect motivation or other factors. Many demonstrated CLT effects compare conditions that differ in several respects (information content, time on task) so that the explanation by load is not the only possibility. The theory can explain results after the fact, which makes it hard to falsify. Effects were mostly shown with novices and short, well-structured lab tasks, so generalization to complex learning is unclear. De Jong also points out that reducing load is not always desirable, since some difficulty (generation, effort, variability) can help learning, and suggests that CLT should be combined with attention to motivation and metacognition, and with direct measures. He gives a balanced view that CLT's practical recommendations are often sensible even if the theory is imprecise. I do not quote any figures from the paper. The proponents responded in later work (the 2011 Sweller, Ayres and Kalyuga book; the 2019 update) by tightening definitions, treating germane load as a use of resources for intrinsic load, and adding the evolutionary framing.

**Criticism and status.** The debate is not settled. Practitioners generally treat CLT's effects as robust heuristics for novices and short tasks, and treat the theory's grand claims with care. Alternative or complementary frameworks include the cognitive affective theory of learning with media (Moreno), Schnotz's integrated model, and desirable difficulties research (Bjork), covered in other lanes.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Treat every CLT rule as a hypothesis to test with LTF's own learners, not a law.
2. Do not minimize difficulty everywhere: effortful retrieval and sim practice are desirable and belong to other lanes.
3. Use direct performance data (can the learner fly the pattern and land?) alongside any load questionnaire.
4. Pair load-based design with motivation research, because load cannot explain why a learner returns tomorrow.
5. Record in the design knowledge base which decisions rest on CLT, so they can be revisited if the evidence shifts.

## Lane synthesis
The sources in this lane agree on a core picture. Working memory is small and fragile for novel material, and long-term schemas are what let experts cope (Sweller1988, Sweller1998, Baddeley1974). Effort spent on search, matching or ignoring that does not serve the learning goal reduces what is left for understanding: split attention (Chandler1991), redundant or irrelevant material (Harp1998, Rey2012) and confusing layout (Mayer2003). Novices benefit from guidance, worked examples and explicit structure (Kirschner2006), and the benefit fades as expertise grows (Kalyuga2003). Pictures help when they are relevant and organized so that needed inferences are easy (Mayer2001, Larkin1987), animation helps modestly where motion is the content (Tversky2002, Hoffler2007), signals help when they are few and structural (Schneider2018), and multiple representations help only when each has a function and the learner is supported in relating them (Ainsworth2006).

They conflict in four places. First, whether CLT is a well-defined, testable theory (deJong2010) or a robust set of effects with a loose theory behind them. Second, whether "minimal guidance" is a fair description of problem-based and inquiry teaching (Kirschner2006 and its rebuttals). Third, whether emotional design helps learning or mainly liking and motivation (Um2012 against the more cautious Brom2018). Fourth, how large animation benefits are: the estimates fall from the first meta-analysis to the update, and the earlier skepticism of Tversky2002 is partly vindicated.

Ten actionable takeaways for LTF:
1. Put labels on the thing, not in a legend (spatial contiguity).
2. Pair explanations of spatial ideas with narration or short text, but never read on-screen text aloud verbatim.
3. Make every element on a lesson page serve the objective; put asides after the point, labeled.
4. Teach component names before the process that uses them (pre-training).
5. Segment lessons into learner-paced chunks with a visible continue control.
6. Move from worked example to partly completed task to free practice, fading guidance with success.
7. Signal sparingly: spotlight one instrument or step at a time, with consistent color and a non-color backup.
8. Animate real motion with pause, scrub and speed controls, and supply stills as a fallback and reduced-motion alternative.
9. Provide an expert path (skip, hide hints, fewer labels in fly mode) because help for beginners can hurt experts.
10. Treat these as evidence-based heuristics for novices; verify in the app with transfer measures, and use warm, friendly visual design as a modest motivational aid rather than a learning guarantee.
