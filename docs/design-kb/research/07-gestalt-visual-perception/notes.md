# Lane 07 notes: Gestalt and visual perception

## Wertheimer1923
**Core claims.** Wertheimer's 1923 paper (English translation in Ellis 1938 under the title "Laws of organization in perceptual forms") is the origin of the list now called the Gestalt laws. His argument is that we do not experience a mosaic of local sensations that the mind then assembles. We experience organized wholes first, and the way the field splits into units is governed by factors that can be demonstrated and varied. He opens with the point that a field of "just so many dots" is never seen as an arbitrary heap: it is seen as rows, pairs, clusters or figures, and the job of the psychologist is to find out which stimulus properties decide this. The factors he names are proximity (nearer elements go together), similarity (like elements go together), common fate (elements moving together), good continuation (elements that continue a smooth line or curve), closure (contours that enclose a region), and what he calls objective set and habit (a grouping established by the preceding sequence, or by familiar forms). Behind them stands the principle later called Prägnanz: the organization that emerges is the "good" one, meaning simple, regular, symmetric, stable.

**Specifics.** The method is phenomenological demonstration with schematic patterns, not measured performance. A row of equally spaced dots or short lines is seen simply as a uniform row. If alternate gaps are narrowed, the row is seen as pairs, and the viewer can regroup it voluntarily only with effort, which Wertheimer treats as evidence that the tendency is a property of the field. He varies the spacing in small steps to show that grouping strength changes with the ratio of distances, which is the observation Kubovy and colleagues later turned into the "pure distance law" in dot lattices. Similarity is shown with alternating light and dark elements that are equally spaced: the row is read as light pairs and dark pairs, or as columns of like kind. He then sets factors against each other (for example proximity against similarity) and reports which wins, which is the beginning of the question of how cues combine. Good continuation is shown by crossing curves that are seen to pass through each other rather than to bounce apart at the crossing, because the smooth path is preferred. Closure and "good form" are shown by figures where a gap is ignored or where a familiar closed form is preferred to the arbitrary segments that compose it. A key observation is that a pattern can be organized by a factor even when the viewer knows another organization is possible, so knowledge alone does not defeat these tendencies.

**Quotation.** None, because the exact wording of the translation was not confirmed in this session.

**Criticism and status.** The phenomena replicate readily and are in every vision textbook, but the paper gives no rule for combining factors, and the term "law" overstates what are ceteris paribus tendencies. Wagemans and colleagues (2012, part I) point out that for most of a century the principles were illustrated rather than quantified, with Kubovy's proximity work, contour-integration studies (Field, Hayes and Hess 1993) and Palmer's new principles being the main modern advances. Wertheimer's observer reports are subjective, and the effects of attention, task and meaning were not controlled. The claim that organization is purely stimulus-driven has been softened: context, attention and learned familiarity modulate it.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Treat grouping by proximity as the default: the space between a gauge and its caption must be visibly smaller than the space between that pair and the next item.
2. Use similarity only to say "these are the same kind of thing": all challenge-start buttons look alike, all checklist items look alike, and nothing else shares that look.
3. Align the steps of a procedure on one clean line or curve so continuation carries the eye through the sequence.
4. When two cues disagree (a caption sits near one card but is styled like another), the result is ambiguity. Check designs by blurring them and seeing what groups.
5. Remember that viewers apply these tendencies whether or not the designer intended them, so every accidental alignment or repeated shape will be read as a relationship.

## Koffka1935
**Core claims.** Koffka's 720-page textbook is the systematic statement of Gestalt psychology in English (Harcourt, Brace, 1935). Its central claim is that organization, not sensation, is the primitive fact of perception, and that the whole has properties not found in its parts. The slogan "the whole is other than the sum of its parts" is usually credited to Koffka. He distinguishes the geographical environment (the physical world) from the behavioral environment (the world as it appears to the organism), and holds that behavior is determined by the latter. He restates Prägnanz as the tendency of organization to be as good, that is simple, regular and balanced, as the prevailing conditions allow. He gives a long treatment of figure and ground, of the constancies of size, shape, brightness and color, and of how perception relates to memory and learning (the memory trace undergoes the same organizing tendencies as the percept). His theoretical frame is field theory with psychophysical isomorphism: the brain is supposed to contain field processes with the same structure as the percept.

**Specifics.** The treatment of constancy is the part most useful to designers. Koffka argues that a surface looks the same shade under different illumination because what is perceived is the relation between the surface and its surroundings, not the absolute light reaching the eye. He uses the example that a piece of coal in sunlight sends more light to the eye than a sheet of white paper in dim indoor light, yet it still looks black. Brightness and lightness depend on the ratio to the surround and the framework of the whole field. The same relational reasoning is applied to size (apparent size depends on apparent distance) and shape (a tilted circle is still seen as a circle). The treatment of grouping follows Wertheimer and adds the concept that parts have different "belongingness" within a whole depending on the structure. The book also covers the "vectorial" qualities of the visual field: parts of figures seem to push or pull, and balanced structures are more stable. A chapter discusses the memory trace, arguing that a remembered form drifts toward the simpler, more regular one, which prompted later experiments on memory for ambiguous figures.

**Quotation.** None quoted, to avoid misattributing wording.

**Criticism and status.** The isomorphism hypothesis was tested by Köhler and Held with electrical-field experiments and was not confirmed, and the physiological field theory is not accepted. Prägnanz is criticized for being untestable unless "good" is defined independently (Wagemans et al. 2012, part II). The claim that memory traces drift toward good form was challenged by later work showing that the change depends on labeling and context. The relational account of lightness, though, is well supported and carried forward by Gilchrist's anchoring theory. The book is best treated as a historical and conceptual source, not as a source of validated numbers.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Judge a color or gray only in its real context: the same patch looks different on a Day surface and a Night surface, so preview components on the actual backgrounds.
2. Because perception is relational, keep contrast ratios between text and surface stable across themes rather than copying one theme's values into the other.
3. Prefer simple, regular, symmetric forms for the core instrument widgets, since these are resolved fastest and remembered most accurately.
4. Design for the behavioral environment: what the learner perceives while glancing between the sim and the app is what counts, which argues for large, high-contrast, quickly grouped layouts in fly mode.
5. Expect users to remember a simplified, regularized version of a diagram, so give diagrams a clean canonical shape that is a good thing to remember.

## Rubin1915
**Core claims.** Edgar Rubin's Danish doctoral work Synsoplevede Figurer (Copenhagen, 1915; German translation Visuell wahrgenommene Figuren, 1921) introduced figure and ground as a basic unit of visual organization. When a region is seen as a figure it has thing-like character: it appears bounded by its contour, a little nearer, more solid and more salient, and it is more readily described and remembered. The ground is seen as extending continuously behind the figure and is relatively shapeless. The contour between them is "owned" by the figure, and shapes the figure but not the ground. The reversible vase and faces drawing shows that the same contour can be assigned to either side but that only one assignment is held at a time.

**Specifics.** Rubin's descriptive list of differences is the core. First, the figure has form and the ground is relatively formless. Second, the figure appears in front, the ground behind and continuing. Third, the contour is experienced as belonging to the figure, so that the same line looks different (it takes on the shape of the figure) depending on which side is figure. Fourth, the figure is more strongly colored and impressive (surface color in the figure is more "dense" and the ground more "diffuse"). Fifth, the figure suggests meaning and associations more readily than the ground. Later experiments identified stimulus determinants: relatively smaller area, enclosed or surrounded regions, convexity, symmetry, parallel contours, and a vertical or horizontal orientation tend to be seen as figure (Wagemans et al. 2012, part I). Peterson and colleagues showed that familiar shapes such as a recognizable object silhouette on one side of a contour bias figure assignment, and that attention matters. Single-cell recording (von der Heydt and colleagues) found border-ownership cells in visual area V2 that fire differently depending on which side of an edge the figure lies, within a few tens of milliseconds, which gives a neural basis for one-sided contours. Memory is better for the figure region than for the ground region in Rubin's own tests.

**Criticism and status.** The phenomenology is secure. The original claim that figure-ground comes first, before recognition, is no longer accepted in a strict form because meaning, attention and context influence it (Peterson). Nor is the pattern as fully pre-attentive as Rubin supposed. There is no single formula for combining the determinants. The vase figure is a famous demonstration but few designers realize it is a bistable display with approximately equal cues, designed to defeat the normal asymmetry.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Make the content the figure: cards and panels should be convex, enclosed and smaller than the surface behind them, and the surface should be calm and low-contrast.
2. Use figure-ground intentionally for overlays: dimming or blurring the ground pushes a modal forward, and the strength of the dim controls how much the ground is attended.
3. Audit icons and logos for accidental negative-space figures, and use deliberate ones as an identity device (a hidden shape in a mark).
4. Keep the figure-ground relationship stable between Day and Night themes: whichever of surface or object is lighter should stay consistent, so a gauge does not flip from figure to ground.
5. Remember the contour belongs to the figure: border treatments and shadows should clarify which region owns the edge.

## Arnheim1954
**Core claims.** Arnheim (Gestalt psychologist trained in Berlin under Wertheimer and Köhler) wrote Art and Visual Perception (University of California Press, 1954; new revised version, 1974) as a psychology of the creative eye. The thesis is that seeing is itself an active, organizing, thought-like activity, and that composition works through perceptual forces: visual elements have weight, direction and tension, and a composition is perceived as a field of balanced or unbalanced forces. Chapters cover balance, shape, form, growth, space, light, color, movement, dynamics and expression. Arnheim applies Prägnanz to art: viewers find and prefer the simplest structure the stimulus allows, and artists succeed when the structure is clear.

**Specifics.** Balance: every picture has a structural skeleton, including the center, the vertical and horizontal axes and the diagonals, plus a "hidden" frame of the format, and elements placed on this skeleton feel stable. Visual weight rises with size, with brightness (against a dark ground), with saturated warm color, with isolation, with distance from the center (a lever-like effect, so a small element far out can balance a larger element close in), and with interesting or unusual shape. Weight also depends on location: elements high in the field and on the right are claimed to carry more weight, though this left-right claim is Arnheim's inference from artwork and not a settled result. Shape: a square rotated 45 degrees is seen as a diamond, a different figure, which shows that orientation relative to the frame and the vertical changes identity. Arnheim describes the "tension" of near-misses: two shapes that almost align or almost touch look unstable until they clearly align or clearly differ, an observation designers use as "make it the same or make it clearly different". He treats the picture frame as an active element and notes that a figure placed off-center creates dynamic tension while one at the center is calm. There are discussions of visual "completion" (a partly hidden shape is seen as whole) and of simplicity versus complexity: a good design is as simple as the content allows.

**Criticism and status.** The book is an expert's interpretive analysis of artworks, so it generates hypotheses rather than tested effects. Some claims have been tested with mixed outcomes: the left-right weight asymmetry has weak and inconsistent support in the empirical aesthetics literature, and the "lever" balance rule is plausible but is not an established quantitative law. The core ideas of weight, tension, near-miss instability and structural skeleton are widely used in design teaching and are consistent with modern perception research on salience.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Balance by visual weight, not equal area: a dense cluster of instruments can be balanced by a large quiet text block, and the heaviest element should be the primary action or the current lesson.
2. Avoid near-misses: elements that almost align by a few pixels look like errors, so snap to a grid or make the offset deliberate and large.
3. Use off-center placement for energy (hero illustrations) and centered placement for calm (a completed lesson state).
4. Establish a clear structural skeleton behind asymmetric layouts, so the page has identity without chaos.
5. Use tilt and diagonal for tension only where tension is meant: bank angle, warnings, climb.

## Palmer1992
**Core claims.** Stephen Palmer's 1992 paper in Cognitive Psychology (24, 436-447) proposed common region as a new principle of grouping: elements that lie within the same bounded region, such as inside a drawn outline or a shaded area, are perceived as a group. The abstract argues that the effect cannot be reduced to proximity, closure or any other previously known factor, and it also shows that grouping by common region depends on perceived depth relations, so it operates after some depth perception has occurred.

**Specifics.** The experiments used arrays of dots or simple shapes in which a previously established cue, usually proximity or similarity, suggested one grouping, while enclosure by ellipses or rectangles suggested another. Observers judged or were timed on the grouping they saw. Enclosure beat proximity and similarity in the conflict conditions: elements farther apart but inside the same outline were grouped together, and elements of different colors inside one outline were still grouped. Palmer also varied the way the regions were drawn and the depth relations between them, finding that the effect depends on the enclosing region being seen as a surface in front of or behind the elements rather than as an accidental arrangement. Later work by Palmer and Rock (1994) set common region alongside uniform connectedness and element connectedness as the strongest grouping factors known, and later experiments showed that common region also affects perception of motion and attention (items sharing a region are processed as a unit, with costs for attending across boundaries). Common region is the perceptual basis for cards, panels, tabs, table cells and form fieldsets.

**Criticism and status.** The finding is well replicated and cited as a standard grouping principle (Wagemans et al. 2012, part I). Limits: the displays were simple and sparse, so weighting against competing cues in dense interfaces is extrapolation. Boundaries also add visual noise, and too many nested regions flatten the hierarchy (a design observation, not a finding of the paper).

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use a bounded card or tinted panel to group an instrument with its explanation, rather than relying on spacing alone.
2. Limit nesting to about two levels, since each boundary draws attention and competes with the content.
3. A subtle tint or hairline is enough to establish a region, so heavy borders are unnecessary.
4. Check captions: if a caption is nearer to card A but inside card B, the region wins and the caption is read as B's.
5. In fly mode, use regions to separate the checklist, the current step and the instrument reference, so the learner can find each at a glance.

## Palmer1994
**Core claims.** Palmer and Rock (Psychonomic Bulletin and Review, 1994, 1(1), 29-55) argue for rethinking perceptual organization. They propose uniform connectedness (UC): a connected region with uniform visual properties (lightness, color, texture, and so on) is perceived as one unit, and this operates early, producing the entry-level units on which the classical Gestalt grouping principles then work. In the older view, grouping starts from points or edges; in theirs, it starts from regions, and the classical principles (proximity, similarity, common fate) group or parse these regions afterwards. They also describe element connectedness, where elements joined by a line are seen as one unit.

**Specifics.** The paper reviews and reports experiments in which grouping by uniform connectedness was pitted against proximity, similarity, and common fate. A classic arrangement uses a row or lattice of dots in which proximity suggests one grouping, while a line connecting selected pairs of dots, or an outline joining them, suggests another. Participants made speeded judgments and reports of how elements grouped. In these tests connection overrode the other cues, and the effect required no learning. The authors also discuss how UC explains parsing of objects into parts through concavities, how figure and ground relate to regions, and why a region of constant color is segregated from a gradient, as a region of uniform texture is from a different texture. They argue the order is serial: first UC defines regions, then regions are parsed into parts and surfaces, then classical grouping combines them.

**Criticism and status.** The serial claim was challenged by Peterson and Gibson (1994, same journal, 1(4), 509-514), who argued that figure-ground organization, stereo fusion and object recognition show influences that come before or alongside UC, so UC cannot be strictly first. Wagemans et al. (2012, part I) treat connectedness and common region as established principles but describe the ordering as debated. The finding that element connectedness is a very strong cue has been replicated. There is no consensus on the neural basis.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Connect steps in a lesson path, checklist or progress track with a visible line, because connectedness is among the strongest cues of belonging.
2. A diagram that links cause and effect with a drawn line or arrow will be read as related without further labels.
3. Use a continuous background fill for things that belong together, such as a table row highlight or a segmented control.
4. Do not draw connectors you do not mean: ruled lines through a table and default list dividers imply relations or breaks.
5. For charts, a continuous line series reads as one object, whereas separate markers read as individual points.

## Wagemans2012I
**Core claims.** Wagemans, Elder, Kubovy, Palmer, Peterson, Singh and von der Heydt (Psychological Bulletin, 2012, 138(6), 1172-1217) review a century of research on perceptual grouping and figure-ground organization, taking Wertheimer's 1912 paper on apparent motion as the beginning. The review covers classic grouping principles, newer ones (common region, element connectedness, uniform connectedness, synchrony), contour integration, figure-ground determinants, neural mechanisms, and whether modern vision science vindicates Gestalt claims. It concludes that there has been real progress in quantification and neural understanding, but that many questions the Gestalt psychologists posed remain open.

**Specifics.** Grouping: Kubovy and colleagues showed for dot lattices that grouping by proximity follows a "pure distance law" in which the probability of grouping falls approximately exponentially with relative distance, and that the law applies across lattices. Contour integration: Field, Hayes and Hess (1993) found that a path of oriented Gabor elements is detected in noise when neighboring elements are roughly collinear, with detection falling as the orientation change between neighboring elements grows (the paper reports tolerance only up to a limited angle), giving the "association field" idea. Figure-ground: the review lists determinants including surroundedness, relative size (smaller tends to be figure), convexity, symmetry, parallelism, orientation, contrast, and meaning, plus the influence of attention and past experience. Neural: border-ownership cells in V2 and V4 code which side of an edge is figure, with latencies shorter than would be expected if only feedback from higher areas decided it, suggesting local and global interaction. The review also covers time: grouping effects can occur very early but also build up over hundreds of milliseconds, and some depend on attention. It notes that principles such as closure and symmetry have been less well quantified than proximity.

**Criticism and status.** This is a review by leading researchers in a top review journal, so it is the best single entry point to the field. It does not itself claim that the principles can be combined into a general prediction rule, and says so. It documents that "pre-attentive" is too strong for many grouping effects.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use the review's principle list as a grouping checklist in design critique: proximity, similarity, common region, connectedness, continuation, symmetry, common fate.
2. Expect grouping to change with attention and context, so test with real lesson content, not placeholder text.
3. Use convexity, enclosure and smallness to make interactive objects read as figures.
4. Where a line represents data (altitude, airspeed history), keep it smooth and continuous so it is seen as one contour.
5. Treat proximity as quantitative: doubling the spacing between groups is a stronger separator than a small increase, because the effect depends on relative distance.
6. When in doubt, test the layout with a blur or squint check and with a quick glance of one second: whatever groups under those conditions is what a learner under workload, glancing from the simulator, will see first, and any grouping that appears only after careful study is too weak to rely on.
7. Record the grouping decisions (which cue carries which relationship) in the design system, so new screens inherit the same logic instead of reinventing it.

## Wagemans2012II
**Core claims.** The companion article by Wagemans, Feldman, Gepshtein, Kimchi, Pomerantz, van der Helm and van Leeuwen (Psychological Bulletin, 2012, 138(6), 1218-1252) reviews the conceptual and theoretical foundations: holism, the law of Prägnanz, simplicity, and dynamical-systems accounts. Its argument is that central Gestalt ideas have been given operational definitions inside an information-processing framework, that the Prägnanz idea survives as a simplicity or minimum principle in structural information theory and Bayesian models, and that holism is supported by findings such as the configural superiority effect.

**Specifics.** Pomerantz's configural superiority effect: when the same context is added to two simple stimuli, discriminating between them can become faster and more accurate than discriminating the originals. A standard example adds identical lines to two oblique line segments so that they become a triangle and an arrow, yielding emergent features such as closure that are not present in the parts. Kimchi's work on global versus local precedence shows that which level is processed first depends on the size, number and salience of elements, not just a fixed global advantage. Structural information theory (Leeuwenberg, van der Helm) codes patterns as strings of primitives with regularities (iteration, symmetry, alternation) and predicts that the perceived organization has the shortest code, and the review discusses how this relates to the likelihood principle of Helmholtz (the perceived organization is the most probable distal cause). Dynamical-systems models describe organization as an attractor in a field of interacting elements, linking back to Koffka and Köhler. The review also discusses why Gestalt psychology fell from favor (the physiological theory was falsified, the theory was seen as vague) and why it returned.

**Criticism and status.** The simplicity-versus-likelihood debate is unresolved, and the authors present both. Prägnanz remains difficult to define in a way that makes independent predictions. The emergent-feature effects are well replicated.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Design widgets so that the state shows as an emergent feature: in an attitude indicator, the angle between the horizon line and the fixed aircraft symbol is seen directly as a relation, not read as two numbers.
2. Prefer representations where the key variable is a global feature (tilt, alignment, closure, symmetry) that the eye picks up without effort.
3. For diagnostic displays, use a form that is regular when the state is normal and visibly irregular when it is not, for example a row of checklist marks that forms an unbroken line when complete and shows a gap when an item is missed.
4. As a tie-break, favor the layout that has one clearly simplest organization, because viewers will find it.
5. Do not assume a "global first" reading: if local elements are large or salient, viewers will read them first.

## Gibson1979
**Core claims.** James J. Gibson's The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception (Houghton Mifflin, Boston, 1979) holds that perception is the direct pick-up of information in the ambient optic array by an active, moving observer. It rejects the idea of a retinal image that must be interpreted. The environment contains structure and invariants (properties that stay constant as the observer moves), and the visual system is tuned to pick them up. The book introduces the concept of affordances, what the environment offers the animal, for good or ill (Chapter 8), and treats vision as a system including eye, head and body in motion.

**Specifics.** Key information sources: the texture gradient (texture elements get denser and smaller with distance on a ground surface); optic flow (when an observer moves, the optic array flows outward from the point of heading, the focus of expansion, and the rate and pattern of flow specify speed and direction); the horizon ratio (objects of the same height standing on a level ground are cut by the horizon at the same fraction of their height, independent of distance, because the horizon is at the observer's eye level, which specifies size); occlusion and the deletion and accretion of texture at edges; and motion parallax. Gibson developed this from his wartime work on pilots at the US Army Air Force Aviation Psychology program, where he studied how pilots judge landing from the visual information of a runway, so it applies directly to flight: during approach and landing, the texture of the ground flows from the aim point, and the pattern of flow specifies where the aircraft will touch down. He emphasizes that perspective information is not 'depth cues added up': it is higher-order structure that specifies layout. The book also distinguishes the "visual world" (what we see) from the "visual field" (a picture-like experience we can adopt by looking attentively), and treats pictures as surfaces that preserve some invariant information.

**Criticism and status.** The direct-perception claim and the rejection of representation is contested: Marr (1982) and Ullman argued that perception needs computation and inference. Optic-flow information for heading and landing has strong empirical support, but experiments by Wann, Warren and colleagues show that the information is used in combination with other cues. Affordance has become a widely used word in design after Norman, who reworked it as a perceived property of design, a use that Gibsonians (including Gibson's followers) say changes its meaning.

**Use for LTF.**
1. This is the best perceptual grounding for teaching visual flying: pitch from horizon position, speed and sink from texture flow, and aim point from the runway trapezoid. Lessons should explain what the learner will see outside, using these terms.
2. Illustrate in perspective with a horizon line and a texture gradient on the ground, rather than flat diagrams.
3. Use short animations of optic flow (the aim point stays fixed while the surroundings expand) to teach the flare and landing.
4. Use shape, shading and position that signal operability (a raised button looks pressable) while noting that affordances in the interface are conventions.
5. Show the sim's cockpit view and the app side by side, so what the learner learns to see matches what the app shows.

## Fechner1860
**Core claims.** Gustav Theodor Fechner's Elemente der Psychophysik (Breitkopf und Härtel, Leipzig, 1860, two volumes) founded psychophysics as a science of the quantitative relation between stimulus and sensation. Volume 1 covers outer psychophysics (stimulus to sensation) and volume 2 inner psychophysics. Fechner took Ernst Weber's finding (1834) that the smallest detectable change in a stimulus is a constant fraction of the stimulus magnitude (ΔI/I = constant) and assumed that all just-noticeable differences are equal increments of sensation. Integrating gives sensation proportional to the logarithm of stimulus intensity (S = k log I), now called the Weber-Fechner law. He also formalized the methods of limits, constant stimuli and average error, still the standard threshold methods, and went on to found experimental aesthetics (Vorschule der Aesthetik, 1876).

**Specifics.** Weber fractions differ by sense and conditions, with commonly cited values of about 2 percent for visual brightness under good conditions, a few percent for lifted weights (the traditional 1/40 to 1/50 estimate is generally cited), and much larger for taste and smell. Weber's law holds approximately over the middle of the range and fails near threshold. The Fechner equation implies that to produce equal steps of sensation, the stimulus must grow geometrically, which is why decibels, stellar magnitudes and musical octaves use ratio scales. In experimental aesthetics Fechner ran rectangle-preference tests, including the golden section, using methods of selection, construction and use; his results gave a preference for rectangles near the golden proportion but with a wide spread, and later reanalysis and replications (for example the review by Benjafield and later studies) showed that preferences depend strongly on method and individual and that there is no strong evidence for a universal ideal proportion. English translation only covers volume 1 (Adler, 1966).

**Criticism and status.** Stevens (1957) argued that sensation grows as a power function, not a logarithm, and measurement of sensation by JND summation is an unsafe assumption. Weber's law has well-known departures at low intensities and for some senses. The golden-section result is now regarded as weak. The methods, though, remain standard.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Build type, spacing and contrast scales from ratios, not equal increments, since perceived differences are proportional.
2. Make a difference large enough to be seen: steps of a few percent in size or lightness go unnoticed, so adjacent levels of a hierarchy should differ clearly (as an engineering rule of thumb, avoid steps under about 10 to 15 percent in type size, which is a design heuristic, not Fechner's number).
3. Do not justify proportions by the golden section citing Fechner. His own data do not support a single ideal, and the topic belongs to the golden-ratio myth literature.
4. For instrument scales and sliders, perceived equal steps may need logarithmic spacing (for example a range of altitudes from 0 to 10,000 feet MSL).
5. Use the threshold methods (limits, constant stimuli) when testing whether learners can discriminate two states, such as two shades used for alert levels.

## Stevens1957
**Core claims.** S. S. Stevens's "On the psychophysical law" (Psychological Review, 1957, 64(3), 153-181) argues that the relation between stimulus magnitude and perceived magnitude is a power function, ψ = k φ^n, not the logarithm of Fechner. The exponent n is characteristic of each sensory continuum. Stevens distinguishes prothetic continua (those based on addition of excitation, such as loudness and brightness, where the power law applies) from metathetic continua (those based on substitution, such as pitch and position, which are not scaled the same way). He proposes magnitude estimation as the direct method: the observer assigns numbers in proportion to perceived strength, with no assumption that JNDs are equal.

**Specifics.** Exponents commonly tabulated from Stevens's work: loudness (sound pressure of a 3000 Hz tone) approximately 0.67; brightness of a 5-degree target in the dark approximately 0.33 (other conditions give about 0.5 and 1.0 for a point source and for brief flashes in some tabulations); apparent length of a projected line approximately 1.0; apparent area of a projected square approximately 0.7; heaviness of lifted weights approximately 1.45; electric shock through the fingers approximately 3.5. An exponent below 1 means compression (large stimuli are underestimated relative to small ones), above 1 expansion, and 1 linearity. Cross-modality matching (matching loudness to line length, say) gave results consistent with the exponent ratios, which Stevens took as a strong validation. In plain terms the power law is a straight line in log-log coordinates with slope n. The exponent depends on procedure and range, and the values differ somewhat between studies. In information visualization these results were applied by Cleveland and McGill (1984) and others to rank encodings: judgments of position along a common scale are more accurate than length, angle, area and shading, in part because area (about 0.7) and brightness (about 0.33 to 0.5) are compressed and so underestimated.

**Criticism and status.** Context and anchoring effects, the range of the stimuli and the instructions to the observer all change the exponents, and Poulton (1989) catalogued biases in magnitude estimation. Some argue that numbers assigned by observers measure a response rule, not sensation, and the long-standing dispute over whether sensations can be measured on a ratio scale continues. Nevertheless the power law is the standard descriptive account and its main message, that perceived magnitude is a compressive or expansive function of physical magnitude, is secure.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Encode quantities by position along a scale or by length, not area or brightness, since area is underestimated (exponent about 0.7). If areas must be used, scale them by area and add labels.
2. Since brightness is strongly compressive (exponent about 0.33 for a small target in the dark), equal steps in lightness need perceptually uniform spacing, not equal steps in the hex value (see lane 05).
3. Dial and bar gauges that carry exact reading should use pointer angle or bar length against a scale with ticks.
4. Keep intensity changes in alerts moderate: expansive continua such as shock-like strong flashing escalate quickly in perceived strength, so use a few clear levels.
5. When asking learners to judge a quantity from a picture (the angle of bank, the slope of a glidepath), expect systematic over- or underestimation and give a reference line.

## Biederman1987
**Core claims.** Irving Biederman's "Recognition-by-components: A theory of human image understanding" (Psychological Review, 1987, 94(2), 115-147) proposes that the visual system recognizes objects by segmenting the image at regions of deep concavity into simple volumetric parts, called geons (geometric ions), identifying each part from properties of its edges, and matching the arrangement of parts to a stored structural description. About 36 geons are proposed, derived from combinations of a few contrastive edge properties: curvature, collinearity, symmetry, parallelism and cotermination. Recognition is argued to be fast, to work for most viewpoints, and to survive degradation and occlusion as long as the components can be identified.

**Specifics.** The theory rests on nonaccidental properties, image properties that would rarely arise by accident of viewpoint and so reliably signal 3D structure: a straight line in the image signals a straight edge, parallel lines signal parallel edges, symmetric image curves signal symmetric surfaces, and coterminating edges signal a vertex. The geon set is defined by these properties, for example a cylinder differs from a brick by curved versus straight edges. In the experiments, line drawings of common objects were presented briefly and observers named them. Deleting contour segments at the midpoints of edges, which leaves the components recoverable by completion, hurt naming little, whereas deleting the same amount of contour at the vertices, where the edges join and the geon is specified, hurt strongly. Naming also tolerated occlusion where the visible parts were enough to identify the components. The theory predicts that color and texture are secondary and that line drawings are recognized almost as quickly as photographs for basic-level objects, and it predicts that a few components in the right arrangement are enough, so a stylized figure of a few geons is recognized. A hallmark of the account is the importance of contour junctions and the arrangement of parts (an object made of the same components in a different arrangement is a different object).

**Criticism and status.** Viewpoint-dependent accounts (Tarr and Bülthoff; Edelman) show recognition performance varies with viewpoint and that familiar views are favored, which contradicts strong viewpoint invariance. The geon list is a model proposal, and the count of 36 is an estimate. There is also doubt about whether the model can handle subordinate-level recognition (individual faces or species). The supporting evidence on contour deletion and the value of junctions has stood up.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Illustrate the Cessna so that its characteristic parts (high wing, struts, fixed tricycle gear, tail) are recognizable in simple shapes, drawn from a three-quarter view that shows all main parts.
2. Do not obscure the junctions where wing meets fuselage or where struts meet wing with overlap, shadows or strokes, since junctions carry the structure.
3. Line-art style is enough for recognition, so illustrations can be light, consistent and fast to load without losing accuracy.
4. For icons, keep the nonaccidental properties intact: straight stays straight, parallel stays parallel, symmetric stays symmetric.
5. Use the same canonical view for the same part everywhere (the same flap icon, the same view of the yoke) to build recognition through repetition.

## Ramachandran1988
**Core claims.** V. S. Ramachandran's "Perception of shape from shading" (Nature, 1988, 331, 163-166) reports that the visual system interprets shading with built-in assumptions: that the scene is lit by a single light source, that the source is usually above, and that this assumption applies globally across the image. Occluding contours also strongly influence shape perception from shading, and shading-defined shapes can serve as tokens for apparent motion, indicating that the shape is extracted at a stage before motion processing.

**Specifics.** The classic demonstration is an array of disks, each shaded with a gradient from light to dark. Disks that are lighter at the top appear convex, like bumps, and disks that are lighter at the bottom appear concave, like dents. Turning the page upside down reverses all of them at once. In a mixed array in which some disks are lighter at the top and others lighter at the bottom, the two groups segregate immediately, one seen as bumps and the other as dents, and this visual search for one kind among the other is fast, which suggests parallel, pre-attentive extraction of shading direction. Viewers cannot see the whole array as bumps at once when half the shading is reversed, as the single-light-source assumption forces consistency. The paper shows that the interpretation of a patch depends on the surrounding patches (global), and that the occluding edge of a shape can fix the interpretation of the shading inside it. Subsequent work reported the light-from-above prior in other animals and in young observers, and showed it can be modified by experience, with a small bias toward light from the upper left in some populations.

**Criticism and status.** The core phenomena are widely replicated. Adams, Graf and Ernst (2004) showed that the light-from-above prior can be updated by haptic experience. A small leftward bias (Sun and Perona 1998 and others) varies across studies. Shading is also only one of several depth cues, and the effect is weaker with realistic textured surfaces.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Light every raised or recessed element from the same direction, usually above, with the highlight on the top edge and the shadow below, so that buttons look like bumps and inputs look like wells.
2. Hold the light direction constant across the app and between Day and Night, so depth cues never conflict.
3. For pressed or active states, invert the gradient or shadow so the element reads as pushed in.
4. Check any icon with shading, since an icon with the light from below will look inverted.
5. A flat design with no shading is a legitimate choice, but if shadows are used for hierarchy, use a single consistent elevation model.

## Bar2006
**Core claims.** Moshe Bar and Maital Neta's "Humans prefer curved visual objects" (Psychological Science, 2006, 17(8), 645-648) found that people rate real objects with curved contours as more likable than the same objects with sharp-angled contours. The preference held for meaningless abstract patterns and when curved objects were compared with controls that had both curved and sharp elements. The authors proposed that sharp contours carry an implicit signal of threat, which would make the preference an avoidance of angular shapes, and a follow-up fMRI study (Bar and Neta 2007, Neuropsychologia) reported greater amygdala activation for sharp-contoured objects.

**Specifics.** Participants (undergraduate samples) saw pairs of matched everyday objects such as watches, furniture and other items, in a curved and a sharp-angled version, and rated how much they liked them (and in some cases chose between them). A second experiment used abstract, meaningless patterns formed from curved or sharp elements, ruling out familiarity or meaning as the cause. A third compared curved objects to controls containing both curved and sharp features to test whether the sharp contours were disliked or the curves liked. The measure was a preference score, and the effect was reliable across stimulus sets. The authors' interpretation connects with other work showing faster detection of angular features and with the idea that angular contours indicate danger. Bar and Neta's later paper (2007) found activation differences in the amygdala, though the interpretation is open.

**Criticism and status.** The preference replicates in several labs but is moderate and context dependent. Bertamini and colleagues found that curvature preference holds for abstract shapes and many objects but that familiarity, object type and task can change it, and that people sometimes prefer angular designs for objects associated with speed or strength. Alternative accounts hold that curves are simply more visually pleasing or easier to process (fluency), not that angles threaten, and the threat explanation is weakened. The sample sizes were small by present standards, and effect sizes should be treated as modest.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Rounded corners are a defensible default for approachable learning surfaces such as cards, buttons and tags, but the effect is mild, so choose radii as part of identity and not as a rule that overrides other needs.
2. Reserve sharp angular shapes (triangles, chevrons, zigzag edges) for warnings and alerts, where an angular reading carries the right meaning.
3. Define a small radius scale (for example for inputs, cards and large panels) and use it everywhere, because inconsistent radii look careless, which matters more than the exact value.
4. Avoid claiming in design documents that science proves curved is better; cite it as a modest bias.
5. Consider using angular forms purposefully in aviation-themed graphics (wing and runway geometry) to give the brand a recognizable shape language distinct from generic rounded UI.

## Ware2000
**Core claims.** Colin Ware's Information Visualization: Perception for Design (Morgan Kaufmann, first edition 2000; fourth edition 2019) turns vision research into design guidance. His model has stages: parallel extraction of low-level features (orientation, color, size, motion, stereo depth) in the early visual system, pattern perception that finds contours, regions and textures, and serial visual query in working memory. The book argues that good visualizations match this system: use features that pop out for what must be found, use Gestalt structure for relationships, and give detail the luminance contrast that the fine-grained channel needs.

**Specifics.** Preattentive features that support fast search: color, orientation, size, motion, simple shape differences, and spatial grouping, but conjunctions of two features generally do not pop out and require serial search. Ware stresses that the luminance channel carries high spatial detail, while chromatic channels have low spatial resolution, so thin lines and small text need luminance contrast with their background, not only a hue difference. For color coding of categories he recommends a small number of distinct colors (he gives a list of about 12 for nominal coding, with fewer being more reliable). He reviews the Gestalt laws as design rules: proximity, similarity, continuity, symmetry, closure, relative size, and figure-ground, and notes that connectedness and common region can override proximity, citing Palmer. He covers depth cues, with shading and shadows useful in 3D displays, and motion as a very strong attention cue that should be used sparingly. He discusses the visual channels for encoding values (position, length, size, color, texture) and cites the psychophysical rankings. Later editions add material on the neuroscience of pattern perception and on designing for perception.

**Criticism and status.** The book is a synthesis, so individual recommendations rest on studies of varying strength and with lab tasks that differ from real use. Some claims about pre-attentive processing have been refined by later visual-search work (Wolfe). Color count limits depend on the task and the display. It remains a standard applied reference in visualization.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Code each meaning with one salient feature (color or position), and avoid combinations such as shape plus color for the same distinction.
2. Check small text and thin strokes for luminance contrast in both themes, because a hue-only difference vanishes at small sizes.
3. Limit categorical colors in a dashboard or chart to about 5 or 6 and give each a second cue (label or pattern).
4. Use drawn connections for relations, and use motion only for essential state changes, since moving elements capture attention.
5. Put the most important quantity on the most accurate channel (position or length), as dictated by psychophysical ranking.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** Perception organizes before reading. Viewers see groups, figures and depth automatically, and a design either supplies the structure deliberately or leaves viewers to infer it from accidents of spacing and alignment. The grouping cues form a rough hierarchy that the sources support: connectedness and common region are stronger than proximity, and proximity is stronger than similarity in most displays (Palmer 1992; Palmer and Rock 1994; Wagemans et al. 2012; Ware). Figure-ground assignment favors smaller, enclosed, convex, symmetric regions (Rubin; Wagemans). Depth from shading depends on an assumed single light source, usually above (Ramachandran). Perceived magnitudes are relational and nonlinear: Weber, Fechner, Stevens and Koffka agree that differences must be judged as ratios and that area and brightness are compressed.

**Where they conflict or have been revised.** The early claim that organization is pre-attentive and holistic is softened by evidence that attention, meaning, and task shape both grouping and figure-ground (Wagemans; Peterson). Isomorphism and physical field forces failed. Fechner's logarithm and Stevens's power law differ, though both say sensation is compressive. Gibson's direct perception conflicts with computational accounts (Marr), although the optic-flow findings stand. Curvature preference (Bar and Neta) is real but modest, and its threat explanation is doubtful. Fechner's golden-section data do not support an ideal ratio. Biederman's viewpoint invariance was challenged by view-based accounts. Arnheim's left-right weight claims lack firm support.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Group first by region and connection, second by spacing, third by similarity.
2. Make content the figure: small, enclosed, convex shapes on a calm ground.
3. Make gaps between groups clearly larger than gaps within groups, and avoid near-miss alignments.
4. Keep nesting of bounded regions to two levels.
5. Light every raised or recessed element from the same direction (above), in both themes.
6. Build type, spacing and contrast scales as ratios, with steps large enough to see.
7. Encode quantities by position or length, not area or brightness.
8. Use one salient feature per meaning, no conjunction codes, and about 5 or 6 categorical colors at most.
9. Teach visual flying through horizon, texture flow and runway shape, drawn in perspective (Gibson).
10. Use rounded corners as a mild default and an identity choice, reserve angular shapes for warnings, and never justify proportions with the golden section.
