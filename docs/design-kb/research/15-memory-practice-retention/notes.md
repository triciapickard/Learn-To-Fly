## Murre2015
**Core claims.** Hermann Ebbinghaus published Ueber das Gedaechtnis in 1885 (English translation by Ruger and Bussenius, Memory: A Contribution to Experimental Psychology, 1913). Working alone, using himself as the only subject, he measured forgetting with lists of nonsense syllables and the savings method: after a delay he relearned a list and counted how much less time or how many repetitions it took than the first time. The saving is the measure of what remained. Murre and Dros (2015, PLoS ONE) repeated this on one modern participant (Murre) and analyzed the data with current curve-fitting. Their main message is that Ebbinghaus's finding stands: forgetting is steep at first and then slows, and the replication curve closely resembles the original.

**Specifics.** Ebbinghaus's tabulated savings are commonly quoted as roughly 58% at 20 minutes, 44% at 1 hour, 36% at 9 hours, 34% at 1 day, 28% at 2 days, 25% at 6 days and 21% at 31 days. These figures come from textbook tables of the 1885 data and were not re-confirmed this session. Murre and Dros used lists of nonsense syllables (13 per list, learned to one perfect recitation) and relearning after delays spanning about 20 minutes to 31 days, with many lists per delay; the exact number of lists is not confirmed here. They report a curve of similar shape to the original and noted a small rise in savings around the 24 hour delay, which they discuss as a possible effect of sleep or of the time of day at which learning and relearning happened. They also compared mathematical forms. Ebbinghaus himself fitted a logarithmic form; modern writers fit power functions, exponentials, or mixtures, and the debate about the true form (Wixted and Ebbesen 1991 favored the power law) is not settled by one subject.

**Why it matters.** The paper is a bridge: it lets a design document cite the modern replication rather than only a 140-year-old book, and it is open access. It also shows how little is needed to get the effect, which is why the curve is the universal starting point for spaced repetition systems (see Settles2016).

**Criticism and status.** The replication is a single participant, and the experimenter was the subject, as in 1885, which invites expectancy effects. Nonsense syllables remove meaning and prior knowledge, so the curve represents a near worst case. Savings is a more sensitive measure than recall, so "21% savings" does not mean 79% is forgotten in the everyday sense. Meaningful material that is organized and connected to knowledge decays much more slowly, and retrieval practice and spacing change the slope. Popular claims such as "you lose 70% within a day" cannot be traced to the original data and should not be repeated. The existence of a steep early drop and a long tail is not in dispute.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Plan the first review of a lesson within a day, because that is where the steepest drop happens, and later ones further out.
2. Do not put percentages about forgetting in learner-facing copy. Say that memory fades quickly at first and that short reviews rebuild it.
3. Weight review time toward arbitrary items with no meaning hooks (V-speeds, frequencies, memory-item order) and less toward items a learner can derive (why the nose drops when power is reduced).
4. Show forgetting as normal. A "you may have forgotten this" nudge is better than a failure state.
5. If a dashboard shows a decay curve, label it as a model, not a measurement of the learner.

## Bjork2011
**Core claims.** Elizabeth and Robert Bjork argue that conditions which make practice feel fast and error-free often yield poor retention and transfer, whereas conditions that slow apparent progress and cause errors during practice often produce better long-term learning. They call these desirable difficulties, a term Robert Bjork introduced in 1994. The chapter is a short, accessible statement written for a general audience in a volume honoring psychology's real-world contributions. The deeper theory is the new theory of disuse (Bjork and Bjork, 1992): every memory has a storage strength (how well learned, which accumulates and does not decay) and a retrieval strength (current ease of access, which rises with practice and falls with disuse). Items with high storage but low retrieval strength are the ones that benefit most when successfully retrieved.

**Specifics.** The list of desirable difficulties, as given in the chapter and elsewhere: spacing practice rather than massing it; interleaving topics rather than blocking them; varying conditions of practice (different settings, formats, examples); testing rather than restudying; generating answers or solutions rather than reading them; and, in motor and some verbal tasks, giving feedback less frequently. The central distinction is between performance (what you can show now) and learning (a durable change that supports retention and transfer). A learner who tests well right after practice may not know the material a week later, and vice versa. The chapter points out that learners and teachers use the immediate state as a guide, so they favor easier conditions. A difficulty is "desirable" only when the learner can overcome it with effort and has the prior knowledge; a task that is too hard simply fails. The Bjorks also draw practical advice for students: space sessions, quiz yourself, mix up topics, vary study places, and be suspicious of the feeling that something is easy.

**Criticism and status.** The framework is a synthesis, and critics note that it can be applied after the fact: any outcome can be called a desirable difficulty if it helps and an ordinary difficulty if it does not. The strongest members of the list (testing, spacing, interleaving, generation) are supported by large literatures; others in the original frame (disfluent fonts, see Taylor2020) failed to replicate. Some researchers prefer cognitive load accounts (lane 14) and note that for complete novices worked examples beat unguided struggle. The two views fit together if difficulty is understood as effort on the right processing (retrieval and discrimination), not effort on irrelevant details.

**Related work.** Slamecka and Graf (1978, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory) showed the generation effect: words a person generates from a rule are remembered better than words read, which is the experimental root of the claim that producing answers beats reading them. Bahrick's long-term studies of Spanish learned in school (1984, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General) found that knowledge that survived the first years tended to persist for decades (his permastore), and that the number of spaced practice sessions mattered more than the amount of initial training. Details of both were not re-confirmed this session. For a flying product they support asking learners to produce answers and to practice across months rather than days.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Judge the product by retention a week later, not by lesson completion rates or in-session accuracy.
2. Put friction in the right place: recall prompts, mixed scenario practice and delayed reviews. Remove friction from navigation, reading and setup.
3. Fade hints and scaffolds in challenges as the learner succeeds.
4. Vary practice conditions (different airports, weather, aircraft states) in challenges to build transfer.
5. Explain once, early, why effortful practice feels worse, so learners do not read struggle as failure.
6. Avoid a design that makes every step feel smooth and rewarding; a smooth experience can mean no learning.

## Roediger2006
**Core claims.** Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke showed that taking a test on studied material produces better long-term retention than spending the same time restudying it. The paper argues that testing is not only a way to measure memory but a learning event, and that it works with educationally realistic prose passages as well as lists of words. It is one of the most cited papers in the field and gave the effect its name, test-enhanced learning.

**Specifics.** In the first experiment, students read short prose passages (on topics such as the sun and sea otters, about 250 words each) and then either studied the passage again or took an immediate free-recall test with no feedback. Three conditions were compared: study-study-study-study (SSSS), study-study-study-test (SSST) and study-test-test-test (STTT). Final free-recall tests came 5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week later. The pattern is the key finding: on the 5 minute test, repeated study was best, as most people would expect; on the 2 day and 1 week tests, the groups that had tested themselves recalled more than the group that restudied, with the STTT group best. The exact percentages could not be confirmed this session and are not given. A second experiment varied the number of tests and the study conditions and again found that repeated testing led to better delayed recall. Participants were also asked to predict how well they would remember; those in the restudy condition were more confident, even though they performed worse on the delayed test, an early illustration that learners misjudge which methods work.

**Criticism and status.** The testing effect is among the most replicated findings in educational psychology. Rowland (2014) meta-analyzed dozens of studies and found a reliable benefit, larger with feedback and with recall rather than recognition tests. Boundary conditions: benefit shrinks if the first test is too easy to be effortful, retrieval success matters (failed retrieval without feedback helps little), and restudy can win on very short delays as the 5 minute result shows. Early studies used college students and verbal material; later work extends to children, older adults, and classroom settings, and to some problem solving and procedural content, though evidence for motor skills is thinner. The "retrieval practice" label should not be taken to mean only quizzing; it includes any attempt to bring information out of memory.

**Design note.** The effect is not limited to being right: even unsuccessful retrieval attempts followed by feedback appear to help, which suggests asking before teaching (pretesting) is not wasted time. That claim comes from later work and is not confirmed here. The practical rule is that the attempt should come before the answer and that feedback should follow if the attempt fails.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Put a free-recall prompt at the end of each lesson section before showing the answer (for example, "List the four forces").
2. Let learners attempt to recite checklists from memory before the checklist appears.
3. Do not show correct answers before the learner commits to one.
4. Prefer short, frequent low-stakes retrieval over one long end-of-module test.
5. Tell learners that tests are practice, not a grade.
6. Use recall rather than recognition for the facts that matter most (V-speeds, memory items).

## Karpicke2011
**Core claims.** Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt, in Science, asked whether retrieval practice beats elaborative studying, a technique widely recommended in education and generally regarded as a strong, meaning-based method. Students read a science text and then either practiced retrieving what they had learned or built concept maps while looking at the text. A week later the retrieval group did better on both verbatim questions and inference questions, including a test that asked students to build a concept map. The paper is cited as evidence that retrieval is not just a route to rote memory but supports meaningful learning.

**Specifics.** The first experiment used 80 undergraduates (as commonly reported) who read a science passage about a topic such as sea otters, then were assigned to one of four conditions: read once; repeated study (reread several times); elaborative concept mapping; or retrieval practice (free recall of the passage, followed by a second study period and a second recall). After a week, a short-answer test comprised verbatim items taken directly from the text and inference items requiring integration of facts. Retrieval practice produced the best performance on both kinds of item. The commonly quoted figure is that retrieval-practice students scored about 50% higher than concept-mapping students on the final test, but that number was not re-confirmed and should be cited loosely. A second experiment (about 120 students) used a different design in which the final test included a concept-map format, and again retrieval practice won. Students were also asked to predict their performance; they expected concept mapping and repeated study to work better than retrieval, and the predictions of the retrieval group were less optimistic, showing that learners favored strategies that produced the strongest feelings of fluency. The authors interpret the retrieval benefit as a result of reconstructing and consolidating knowledge in a way that organizes it for later access.

**Criticism and status.** The result has been replicated in many labs and extended to schools (for example the Roediger, Agarwal, McDaniel and colleagues classroom studies in a middle school). Critics note that concept mapping in the study was done with the text available and for a fixed time, whereas trained, self-directed mapping might do better; that the final test relied on what was practiced (a text with one form of test); and that outcomes may differ with complex material that needs organizing before retrieval. Transfer to far-different contexts is less clear. The effect size of retrieval over elaboration is smaller than over rereading. Retrieval practice works best when the learner succeeds often enough; repeated failure without feedback is demoralizing and not helpful.

**Why it is useful to a designer.** The paper gives a plain argument against a common product pattern: activities that look rich and meaningful (maps, summaries, annotated notes) are not automatically better for retention than a simple act of recall. It also shows the value of testing a plausible method against retrieval, rather than against doing nothing, since many techniques beat nothing but lose to retrieval. When LTF adds a new feature that claims to help learning, the right comparison is retrieval practice of the same duration.

**Use for LTF.**
1. A lesson could end with a "blank page" recall: write or select what you remember, then compare with the lesson.
2. Do not count a concept map, summary or highlight tool as a retention feature.
3. Follow factual recall with inference questions ("What happens to your stall speed if you add weight?") to support transfer.
4. Show a learner their own predicted versus actual results over time to teach calibration.
5. Keep retrieval success rates reasonable by offering cues and a second attempt before revealing answers.

## Cepeda2006
**Core claims.** Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer (2006) review and quantitatively synthesize the distributed practice literature on verbal recall. Confirmed by search this session: the synthesis covers 839 assessments of distributed practice drawn from 317 experiments in 184 articles. The main conclusions: spacing learning sessions apart beats massing them together in the great majority of studies; and the length of the best gap between sessions depends on how long the learner must retain the material, with longer retention intervals favoring longer gaps.

**Specifics.** Terminology matters. The inter-study interval (ISI) is the gap between learning episodes. The retention interval (RI) is the gap between the last study episode and the final test. A distributed-practice experiment varies ISI. The review used meta-analytic methods on both recall and recognition tests and compared groups by ISI and RI. Findings: distributed practice produced an advantage in most cases; for retention intervals of seconds or minutes, short gaps were best; as the RI grew, longer gaps became better; a gap that was too short (massed) gave a clear disadvantage at long RIs. Most experiments used word lists, paired associates, facts and similar verbal material and short RIs of minutes to days. Only a minority of data points had RIs beyond a month, which is a limitation the authors stress: the literature could not say how large a gap to use for retention measured in months, a question that motivated their 2008 study (Cepeda2008). They also discuss mechanisms: study-phase retrieval (the second study event triggers retrieval of the first, which functions like a test), deficient processing in massed practice (less attention to a just-seen item), and contextual variability (spacing allows more varied encoding contexts).

**Criticism and status.** The spacing effect is among the oldest and strongest findings in memory research (Ebbinghaus noticed it in 1885 and Jost's law followed). Meta-analytic estimates since 2006, including Donovan and Radosevich (1999) for skills and later reviews, concur. Caveats: the 2006 database is verbal and short-term; effects for complex problem solving, motor skills and real course content are supported but not as heavily tested; and the best gap varies with materials. Spacing does not mean the learner will like it: massed practice feels more productive because performance during the session is higher.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Make "come back tomorrow" a normal and welcomed part of a module rather than a hindrance to progression.
2. Revisit earlier lessons inside later ones, not only in a separate review area.
3. Make the scheduler depend on how soon the learner needs the knowledge (first solo date, checkride).
4. Avoid offering a long single-sitting "cram to pass" mode as the only route; if it exists, say it will fade.
5. Separate analytics for massed versus spaced attempts to check the effect in LTF's own data.

## Cepeda2008
**Core claims.** Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted and Pashler (2008) ran a large web-based experiment to find how the best gap between study sessions changes with the retention interval. They found a temporal ridgeline: for every retention interval there is an optimal gap, the optimal gap rises with the retention interval but the ratio of gap to retention interval falls, and performance declines more sharply when the gap is too short than when it is too long.

**Specifics.** 1,354 participants (commonly reported figure) learned a set of obscure facts, then reviewed them after a gap that ranged from 0 to 105 days, and were tested after a retention interval ranging from 7 to 350 days. The result was a surface of recall as a function of both gap and retention interval. When the test was a week away, the best gap was about a day, roughly 15 to 40% of the retention interval; when the test was a year away, the best gap was around 3 weeks, about 5 to 10% of the interval (these ratios are approximate, as presented in secondary summaries and not re-checked against the paper). Compared with no gap, using the best gap improved final recall by a large relative amount (roughly 60% in some conditions according to summaries; not confirmed). The authors recommend that if the goal is to remember for a long time, the interval between study episodes should be longer than short-term retention studies suggest, and that guessing too long is safer than guessing too short because the penalty is asymmetric.

**Criticism and status.** One review session only; real study involves many sessions, where expanding versus uniform schedules is still debated (Karpicke and Roediger 2007 found that expanding intervals were not better than equal intervals for long-term retention). The materials were trivia facts, not skills, and the sample was a self-selected online group. Follow-up work (Cepeda, Coburn, Rohrer, Wixted, Mozer and Pashler 2009) fitted models and gave scheduling advice. Because the rule is expressed as a percentage, applying it to a learner who does not know their test date is not possible; adaptive systems use performance instead.

**Use for LTF.**
1. A default schedule for a learner with a checkride in 2 months: first review about a day after learning, then at expanding gaps up to a week or two.
2. Err toward longer gaps if the system must choose.
3. Treat spacing as a design constraint for course pacing: a 60-day course should not put all aircraft-systems content in week one.
4. Present due reviews as short sessions of a few minutes so frequent spacing is feasible.
5. Do not present the ratio as a law in documentation; present it as a starting point to be refined from learner data.

## Dunlosky2013
**Core claims.** Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham reviewed ten learning techniques that students commonly use or that educators recommend, and rated each for utility in education. Confirmed by search: practice testing and distributed practice received high utility ratings; summarization, highlighting, the keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text learning and rereading were rated low; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice were rated moderate. The monograph appeared in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 14(1), pages 4 to 58.

**Specifics.** The ten techniques: elaborative interrogation (generating explanations of why a stated fact is true), self-explanation (explaining how a problem step or new information relates to what you already know), summarization, highlighting and underlining, the keyword mnemonic (using a keyword image to link a foreign word and its meaning), imagery use for text learning, rereading, practice testing, distributed practice and interleaved practice. For each, the authors give a general description, an explanation of why it should work, then evaluate evidence on four dimensions: learning conditions, student characteristics (age, ability, prior knowledge), materials, and criterion tasks (recall, comprehension, application, and durability over delays). They also evaluate evidence in education contexts and note issues for implementation, such as training cost and time. The high-utility verdicts rest on evidence across all four dimensions: practice testing works for learners from elementary to adult, for varied materials, and with delays of days to weeks, and its benefit is not restricted to recall; distributed practice is strong across ages and materials with benefit over long delays. The low ratings mean either limited generality (keyword mnemonics help for foreign vocabulary but not for much else) or little evidence of benefit (highlighting and rereading were popular but showed little durable gain). The review also notes the fact that students often choose rereading and highlighting because of the fluency they produce.

**Criticism and status.** The ratings reflect evidence in 2013. Interleaving is rated moderate mostly because of few classroom studies; later work (Rohrer and colleagues) would arguably raise it. Self-explanation and elaborative interrogation are rated moderate because gains depend on learners' prior knowledge and on the quality of explanations. Some critics argue that the review favors techniques with large lab literatures and that real learning combines techniques. The paper tests individual study techniques, not curriculum design, and it deals with learners studying texts and facts more than with skill training like flying.

**Use for LTF.**
1. The two top techniques, retrieval and spacing, are the default scaffolding of the product.
2. Skip features that mainly support highlighting, copying and rereading of lesson text, or treat them as low-value extras.
3. Add self-explanation prompts at decision points ("Why do we check the fuel selector first?").
4. Teach study technique in a short onboarding card, since learners tend to choose weak methods.
5. Use the "criterion task" idea in design: define what the learner must be able to do later (in the sim) and test that, not recall of text alone.

## Rohrer2007
**Core claims.** Doug Rohrer and Kelli Taylor tested whether shuffling practice problems from several topics (interleaving or mixing) helps learning more than practicing the same problem type repeatedly in a block. In a mathematics task, students who practiced mixed problems did worse during practice but far better on a test a week later. The paper is the classic classroom-relevant demonstration of the interleaving effect and a standard citation for the claim that blocked practice gives an illusion of mastery.

**Specifics.** Participants were college students (small groups, about 18 per condition as commonly reported) learning to calculate the volume of four solids (wedge, spheroid, spherical cone and half cone), each with its own formula. They received a tutorial for each solid and then 16 practice problems, four of each type, either blocked (all four problems of one solid together) or mixed (random order). In practice, blocked students scored markedly higher, approximately 89% against 60% for the mixed group. On a test a week later, the pattern reversed: roughly 63% for mixed against 20% for blocked, as commonly cited. Those figures were not re-confirmed this session, though the direction and the large size of the reversal are well established. The authors argue that in a block, students can reuse the same procedure without deciding which procedure fits, whereas mixing forces them to identify the problem type each time, which is also what a test requires. A related experiment, Kornell and Bjork (2008, Psychological Science), showed that learners induced artists' painting styles better from interleaved than blocked examples, and that most learners believed blocking was better.

**Criticism and status.** Replications and extensions in classrooms (Rohrer, Dedrick and Burgess 2014; Rohrer, Dedrick and Stershic 2015, seventh-grade math; a large 2020 classroom study) found benefits, with larger effects on delayed tests. A meta-analysis by Brunmair and Richter (2019, Psychological Bulletin) reported an overall advantage for interleaving but wide variation by material, with the clearest benefit for visual categories such as paintings and a weaker or absent benefit for some other materials, mathematics included (details not confirmed this session; check before quoting). Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated it moderate. Limits: very novice learners may need blocked practice first; if categories are very different, mixing adds nothing; and the effect combines spacing with discrimination, so it is hard to tell which part helps. Learners rate interleaved sessions as less effective.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Initial lessons introduce one maneuver at a time (blocked), then challenge sets mix them (climb, descend, turn) without announcing which is next.
2. Troubleshooting drills should shuffle cues (rough engine, low oil pressure, alternator light) so learners practice choosing a procedure.
3. Warn learners that mixed practice will feel harder and give lower in-session scores; do not penalize scores during mix sets.
4. Include "which procedure applies?" prompts, not just "perform procedure X".
5. Track delayed performance rather than session accuracy to evaluate mixes.

## Pashler2008
**Core claims.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork were commissioned by the Association for Psychological Science to evaluate whether educational practice should be tailored to individual learning styles. Confirmed by search: the paper is in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 9(3), 105 to 119, and concludes there is no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-style assessments into general educational practice. The authors say that only one kind of evidence would support the practical claim, a crossover interaction, and that virtually no studies show it.

**Specifics.** The learning styles idea has several forms: preferences for modes (visual, auditory, kinesthetic), cognitive styles (holist versus serialist, verbalizer versus visualizer) and personality measures. The paper defines the key practical claim, the meshing hypothesis: that an individual learns better when instruction matches their style than when it does not. To test it, a study must (1) classify learners into groups by a style measure, (2) randomly assign each learner to one of at least two instructional methods, (3) give all learners the same test, and (4) find that the method best for one style group is not best for the other, a crossover interaction. Merely showing that groups differ in preference, or that one method wins overall, does not support meshing. The authors found few studies with proper design, and the ones that satisfied the criteria contradicted the hypothesis. They also say it is plausible that people differ in ability and prior knowledge, which may matter, and that content should shape the modality (a lesson on geography uses maps). They also describe the opportunity cost: time and money on style inventories could go to methods with evidence, and they say that studying would be worthwhile if a proper test showed benefits.

**Criticism and status.** Subsequent work has not reversed the conclusion: Rogowsky, Calhoun and Tallal (2015, Journal of Educational Psychology) found no relation between style and performance by modality, and Newton (2015) found that the belief remains widespread among educators (a majority of surveyed teachers), and Kirschner and van Merrienboer (2013) place learning styles among urban legends. Critics point out that preferences affect motivation and choice, which may have indirect effects, and that "styles" is used loosely; the review is about matching. Evidence about expertise reversal (guidance helps novices but hurts experts) is a real aptitude-treatment interaction but is based on prior knowledge, not style.

**Use for LTF.**
1. No style quizzes, "visual learner" badges or style-based tracks.
2. Choose a representation by what the content is: spatial relations and instrument scans as diagrams and animations, radio phrasing as audio plus text, control feel as sim practice.
3. Offer text, captions and audio for accessibility and convenience, framed as options, not types.
4. Adapt on prior knowledge and measured performance (placement checks, skip options), which have evidence.
5. Do not reuse "learning style" language in marketing or onboarding.

## Kluger1996
**Core claims.** Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi (1996, Psychological Bulletin) challenged the assumption that feedback reliably improves performance. They reviewed a century of feedback research, meta-analyzed it, and proposed Feedback Intervention Theory (FIT). Their headline result: the average effect of a feedback intervention (FI) on performance was positive but moderate, and in more than one third of the cases feedback made performance worse. They argue the effect depends on where the feedback directs the learner's attention.

**Specifics.** The meta-analysis, as commonly reported, covered 607 effect sizes from 131 studies and more than 23,000 observations, with an average effect of about d = 0.41. The counts are well known but were not re-confirmed this session. A feedback intervention is defined as an action by an external agent to give information about aspects of task performance. FIT's central claims are: behavior is regulated by comparing feedback with goals; attention is limited, so feedback shifts it among three levels of a hierarchy, the task-detail level (how to do the thing), the task-motivation level (the goal and how it is going) and the meta-task level (the self, such as ability and worth). Feedback that moves attention toward the self and away from the task tends to reduce performance, as in praise or criticism of the person, threats to self-esteem and normative comparisons. Feedback is more effective when it contains cues for learning and when it directs attention to task details that can be changed. Moderators: complex tasks were more vulnerable to negative effects; feedback that gave information on correct solutions helped more than feedback that only reported outcomes; praise had mixed or neutral effects; and feedback that lowered the learner's self-efficacy hurt. Feedback with goal-setting and with specific information increased gains.

**Criticism and status.** The paper is highly cited and shaped later work, including Hattie and Timperley (2007), who used a three-question model (where am I going, how am I going, where to next) and four levels (task, process, self-regulation, self), and Shute (2008) who summarized formative feedback guidelines. Critics note that many included studies were workplace or laboratory studies with college students, that meta-analytic moderators rest on small subsets of studies, and that FIT is only partially tested. The negative cases are not explained by a single factor. For design, the main reliable messages are: feedback with a task focus helps; self-focused feedback is risky; and an actionable step matters.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Write feedback about the aircraft and the procedure ("You were 12 knots fast over the threshold"), not about the learner ("You are bad at landings").
2. Pair every observation with one next step.
3. Avoid score-only feedback; show what moved the score.
4. Do not use public leaderboards or normative comparison in feedback for beginners; compare to a target and to their own past.
5. Be careful with praise copy ("Awesome!"); use specific confirmation ("Rotated at 55 KIAS as briefed").
6. Test feedback variants in the product, since sign effects vary.

## Butler2008
**Core claims.** Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger studied multiple-choice tests as a learning tool, a practical question because MC tests are cheap and widespread. They found that an MC test produces a testing effect but also that wrong options (lures) can be absorbed and later recalled as if true. Giving feedback after the test increased correct answers on a later test and reduced lure intrusions. They also compared immediate and delayed feedback.

**Specifics.** Participants read short prose passages on topics from art history, science and similar, took a four-alternative multiple-choice test, and a week later took a cued-recall final test, in which they produced answers to short questions. Conditions: no test (just reading), MC test with no feedback, MC test with immediate feedback after each question, and MC test with feedback delayed until after the whole test (often one day later in some variants). Findings: taking an MC test (even without feedback) improved final recall of tested facts over not being tested; lures chosen on the MC test increased intrusions of wrong answers on the final test when no feedback was given; feedback increased correct recall and reduced these intrusions; and delayed feedback gave a benefit on the final test relative to immediate feedback in these experiments. The authors link the delay benefit to spacing: a delayed feedback event is a second, spaced exposure. They also highlight that feedback matters most on items answered incorrectly, and that correct answers given with low confidence benefit as well. The take-home point for item writing: always give the correct answer after the item, and consider using plausible wrong choices only when feedback follows.

**Criticism and status.** The delayed-feedback advantage is modest and inconsistent across the literature; reviews of feedback timing (Kulik and Kulik 1988, and Shute 2008) report that immediate feedback often wins in applied classroom or computer tutor settings, particularly for procedural skills and when learners are novices, while delayed feedback wins in some lab studies with verbal material. Hence the timing question is unsettled and task dependent. The research used college students and one-time sessions. Whether the lure effect translates to ordinary quiz use with immediate corrective explanation is likely small.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Never leave a wrong answer without feedback: show the correct answer and the reason.
2. Explain why each plausible distractor is wrong (for aviation, pairs that are often confused such as indicated and calibrated airspeed).
3. Offer a summary review at the end of a quiz with items missed, which doubles as spaced re-exposure.
4. Use immediate, concise corrective feedback in sim challenges, where the learner is performing a procedure and timing matters; use delayed review for knowledge items.
5. Ask for confidence before revealing the answer so low-confidence correct answers get an explanation too.

## Bloom1984
**Core claims.** Benjamin Bloom (1984, Educational Researcher) summarized dissertation studies at the University of Chicago comparing three forms of instruction: conventional class, mastery learning in a group, and one-to-one tutoring with mastery methods. The average tutored student performed about two standard deviations above the average conventional student, placing them above about 98% of the conventional class; mastery learning in groups gave about one standard deviation, above about 84%. The "2 sigma problem" is to find group methods as effective as tutoring that can be afforded.

**Specifics.** Mastery learning (Bloom 1968, building on Carroll's 1963 model of school learning) divides a course into small units, teaches, gives a formative test, then provides corrective instruction and a second test for students who have not reached a mastery criterion (typically 80 to 90% correct) before they move on. The idea is that aptitude predicts the time needed to learn rather than whether learning is possible. In the paper, Bloom reports studies by students (Anania and Burke) on topics such as cartography, probability and others, with samples in the dozens per condition over multiple weeks (approximate; individual sample sizes not confirmed here). He lists variables that might close the gap in group settings: improved cues, participation and reinforcement, corrective feedback, and homework or parental support, each with an effect estimate from other studies, and suggests that combining two or three may approach 2 sigma.

**Criticism and status.** VanLehn (2011, Educational Psychologist) reviewed controlled comparisons and found that human tutoring has an effect size of roughly 0.79 and step-based intelligent tutoring systems roughly 0.76, with answer-based systems lower, around 0.3; he concluded Bloom's 2 sigma was an overestimate, partly because Bloom's studies tested students on the taught content shortly after instruction and had small samples. Others argue mastery learning's gains depend on test alignment, can slow fast learners, and focus on narrow objectives. A meta-analysis of mastery learning (Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns 1990) found moderate effects, about 0.5 standard deviations. Nevertheless, the finding that step-level feedback, correction and mastery gating help is robust.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Gate progression on demonstrated mastery (for example three on-speed approaches) with corrective help and a retry.
2. Offer placement checks so experienced learners can skip, since mastery gates frustrate those who already know the material.
3. Give feedback at the step level inside widgets and challenges, not only at the end.
4. Show the mastery criterion clearly (what counts as done) rather than a hidden score.
5. Do not cite "2 sigma" as an expected effect; cite VanLehn's more modest range if a number is needed.

## Haladyna2002
**Core claims.** Thomas Haladyna, Steven Downing and Michael Rodriguez (2002, Applied Measurement in Education) assembled a taxonomy of multiple-choice (MC) item-writing guidelines, checked them against textbooks and against empirical studies, and rated how well each is supported. The paper is the standard reference on how to write MC questions for classroom assessment, and it is cited by many testing programs.

**Specifics.** The authors compiled 31 guidelines, drawn from 27 textbooks and a body of research studies (counts as commonly reported, not re-confirmed). Guidelines fall into content concerns, formatting and style concerns, writing the stem, and writing the choices. Content: base each item on a single important learning objective; avoid trivial content, trick items and opinion-based items; keep vocabulary simple; avoid overly specific or overly general content. Stem: state the central idea clearly in the stem and put as much of the wording as possible in the stem rather than repeating it in each choice; avoid negatives such as "not" or "except" (if used, emphasize them); word the stem as a question or complete sentence. Choices: use as many plausible distractors as can be written, but note that research (including Rodriguez's 2005 meta-analysis of option counts) suggests three options (one key and two distractors) are usually enough because additional distractors rarely function; make all options plausible and homogeneous in content, length and grammar; place options in logical or numerical order; vary the position of the correct answer; avoid "all of the above"; avoid "none of the above" in most cases; avoid absolute terms such as always and never; avoid clues linking stem and key such as grammar and repeated words; base distractors on common student errors. The authors classify guideline support as strong when both textbooks and studies agree, and weaker when the advice rests on convention.

**Criticism and status.** The list is an expert-consensus synthesis; many guidelines have little experimental backing. The best evidence concerns three options being sufficient, avoiding "all of the above", and negatively worded stems making items harder without improving measurement. MC items test recognition, which tends to give weaker retention effects than recall tasks (Roediger2006 and the retrieval literature), and well-written MC tests can still reward test-wiseness. Good distractors need pilot data, such as which wrong answers real learners choose. The guidelines were written for classroom tests, not for formative quizzes inside a learning product, where feedback and explanation carry more weight than psychometric properties.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Build an item-writing checklist from these guidelines into the content review (alongside the aviation fact review).
2. Write distractors from real beginner errors (mixing up altitude and airspeed, wrong V-speed, misreading a QNH).
3. Prefer three options unless there are more real misconceptions; avoid "all of the above".
4. Randomize correct position in code and keep numeric options in ascending order.
5. Keep options similar in length and grammar so there are no cues.
6. Where the skill is recall, convert MC items to fill-in or short-answer for the high-value facts, then use MC for discrimination.
7. Log which distractors learners pick and revise those that no one chooses.

## Taylor2020
**Core claims.** Taylor, Sanson, Burnell, Wade and Garry tested whether Sans Forgetica, a typeface designed at RMIT University to be deliberately hard to read and promoted as aiding memory, actually helps people remember. It does not. The paper's title, "Disfluent difficulties are not desirable difficulties", makes the point that visual disfluency is not one of the desirable difficulties in the Bjork sense. The result is a cautionary tale about taking a theory (desirable difficulties) and applying it to a surface feature.

**Specifics.** Participants studied material in Sans Forgetica or in a standard font (such as Arial) and were later tested for memory, using word lists or text and with different test formats. Across experiments there was no memory benefit for the disfluent font and in some measures the standard font did better. The authors position their work against the original promotion of the font, which rested on limited evidence at the time. The theoretical background is the disfluency effect reported by Diemand-Yauman, Oppenheimer and Vaughan (2011, Cognition): in a lab study and in a high school classroom study, material in hard-to-read fonts was remembered better, presumably because the difficulty prompted deeper processing. The specific classroom percentages commonly quoted for that paper were not confirmed this session, so they are not quoted here. Rummer, Schweppe and Schwede (2016, Memory) failed to replicate the disfluency effect for a hard font. A meta-analysis of the disfluency literature has been published (authors and details not confirmed this session; check before quoting) and is generally described as finding no reliable memory benefit from perceptual disfluency.

**Criticism and status.** The negative result for font manipulation is supported by several independent experiments. Limits: Taylor et al. tested one font in short laboratory tasks; it is possible there are special conditions where mild disfluency changes processing, but nothing reliable has been identified. The result does not argue against desirable difficulties: those that work operate on how deeply the meaning is processed and retrieved (testing, spacing, interleaving, generation), not on the legibility of the letters. A related point from the reading research lane: poor legibility slows reading and increases errors, which costs attention that could go to learning.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Keep body text highly legible; do not use unusual, thin or degraded typefaces on the theory that struggle helps memory.
2. Put difficulty into the task (recall, mixing, delay), not the visual presentation.
3. Question vendor or article claims of the form "make it harder to read and people remember more".
4. Reserve distinctive typography for headings and identity, not for the passages learners must retain.
5. If an experiment on type is run, use learner data over delayed tests rather than preference.

## Settles2016
**Core claims.** Burr Settles and Brendan Meeder of Duolingo (ACL 2016) present half-life regression (HLR), a machine-learned model of how quickly a learner forgets each item, used to schedule practice. It joins the psychology of the forgetting curve and the spacing effect with logs from a real language-learning product. The model predicts the probability that a learner recalls a word at a given time and an estimate of how long the memory lasts, so the app can choose what to review.

**Specifics.** The model assumes recall probability falls exponentially with the lag since last practice, p = 2^(-lag/h), where h is the half-life, the lag after which recall probability falls to one half. The half-life is not fixed; it is predicted by a regression, h = 2^(theta times x), where x is a feature vector for the learner and word: the number of times the learner has seen the word, the number of correct and incorrect attempts, and lexeme tags (identifying the word and its grammatical features), and theta are learned weights. The loss function trains the model to fit both recall probability and half-life. The data come from Duolingo practice logs of a very large number of learner-word interactions (the paper reports on the order of millions; the exact figure was not confirmed here), with an accompanying public dataset. HLR was compared with baselines including the Leitner system (a box system in which a correct answer promotes an item to a box with a longer interval), Pimsleur's graduated-interval recall idea, and logistic regression. The paper reports HLR predicted recall substantially better than these baselines on the logs and reports positive results in live experiments for engagement; the specific error reduction and percentage figures were not confirmed this session and are not quoted. The authors also note that the model is interpretable: weights show that more correct answers lengthen half-life and that difficulty varies by word.

**Criticism and status.** The work is from the vendor, so engagement claims are not independent. It covers vocabulary items, a task close to the verbal literature, not procedures or motor skill. The model predicts memory of items, which is not understanding or transfer. For small products, simple schedules such as Leitner boxes or SM-2 give most of the benefit with little infrastructure. The release of data and code has made it a common baseline for later research on knowledge tracing and scheduling.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Start with a per-item record: item id, last reviewed time, correct and incorrect counts, and response time.
2. Implement a simple box scheduler first, then consider an HLR-like model when enough data exists.
3. Treat memory-item style content (V-speeds, emergency steps, radio phrases, checklist order) as schedulable items.
4. Show learners how many items are due, in short sessions, rather than a streak count.
5. Log by item and learner so schedule parameters can be tuned against delayed recall.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** The sources converge on a small number of well-replicated principles. Retrieving information from memory strengthens it more than rereading (Roediger2006, Karpicke2011, Dunlosky2013). Spreading practice over time beats massing it, and the best gap grows with how long the knowledge must last (Cepeda2006, Cepeda2008). Mixing problem types improves delayed performance although it lowers practice scores (Rohrer2007, Bjork2011). Learners misjudge this: fluency and ease feel like learning but are poor guides, so people choose weaker methods. Feedback matters, but its direction and content decide whether it helps (Kluger1996, Butler2008). Two popular ideas lack support: matching teaching to learning styles (Pashler2008) and degraded fonts as a memory aid (Taylor2020). Mastery learning with corrective feedback works, though less dramatically than "2 sigma" claimed (Bloom1984).

**Where they conflict or are unsettled.** Feedback timing: lab studies on verbal items favor delay, while applied studies and skill tasks favor immediacy. Expanding versus equal spacing is unresolved. The best gap ratio is approximate and depends on materials. Interleaving helps in many materials but not all and needs a base of initial blocked learning. Almost all evidence uses verbal recall by students, so flight procedures and motor skills are extrapolations. Vendor evidence (Settles2016) is not independent.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Make retrieval the default interaction: ask before telling.
2. Schedule a first review about a day later and expand gaps; when unsure, go longer.
3. Anchor schedules to the learner's target date.
4. Gate on mastery with corrective retries, but allow placement skips.
5. After initial blocked learning, mix scenarios and procedures; tell learners it will feel harder.
6. Write task-focused feedback with one next step; avoid judging the learner or praise without information.
7. Give the correct answer and the reason for every wrong option; use item-writing rules and distractors from real errors.
8. Ask for confidence before answers, to expose illusions of knowing.
9. Do not build learning-style features or degraded typography; put effort into task design.
10. Log item-level correctness, timing and dates from day one, and measure delayed retention, not session completion.
