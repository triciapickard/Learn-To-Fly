# Lane 21: Brand identity and distinctiveness (research notes)

## sharp2010how

Sharp, B. (2010). *How Brands Grow: What Marketers Don't Know*. Oxford University Press.

### Core claims

Sharp argues that much conventional marketing wisdom (deep loyalty, sharp differentiation, tight targeting) is contradicted by purchase data, and that brands grow mainly by gaining more buyers, including light and occasional ones, rather than by deepening the loyalty of existing buyers. Two ideas matter most for identity work. First, a brand's chance of being bought depends on mental availability (the probability that a buyer notices, recognizes or thinks of the brand in a buying situation) and physical availability (how easy the brand is to find and buy). Second, because most buyers see little real difference between competing brands, the practical job of branding is distinctiveness (being easy to recognize and attribute) more than differentiation (being meaningfully different). Distinctive brand assets such as colors, logos, shapes and sounds are the memory cues that deliver this.

### Specifics

The following points come from secondary summaries and reviews I fetched, not from the book text, because the book itself was not accessible this session.

- Double jeopardy: smaller brands have far fewer buyers, and those buyers are also slightly less loyal, so large brands look more loyal mainly because they are large. A course-notes summary of the book (winzarh.github.io/Breakthrough_Business_Intelligence/How_Brands_Grow_Part_1.html) lists this as a law alongside "big brands have more buyers" and "loyalty differences exist but are small".
- Growth comes from light buyers because purchase frequency in a category is highly skewed: most buyers purchase rarely, and the same notes describe the negative binomial distribution used to model this. The implication drawn is that focusing only on heavy buyers cannot scale a brand.
- A review at boringlyeffective.com lists the book's growth rules as: reach all category buyers, be easy to buy, get noticed, build memory structures linked to category entry points, create distinctive brand assets, stay consistent yet fresh, and avoid giving buyers reasons to defect. The same review states that the book's chapter on assets treats meaningful differentiation as secondary to distinctiveness.
- The notes summary says the book argues that marketers often refresh a brand out of boredom while buyers barely notice, so consistent names, visuals and advertising cues build and protect memory structures (the golden arches and the Coca-Cola bottle shape appear as examples).

### Replication and criticism

The empirical core (double jeopardy, the narrow range of loyalty differences, the Dirichlet-type purchase model) rests on decades of replication across categories and countries by the Ehrenberg-Bass tradition; see the Ehrenberg, Goodhardt and Barwise (1990) review in this lane for the original evidence and its documented exceptions (niche and change-of-pace brands). The more contested parts are the managerial extrapolations. The boringlyeffective.com review, which is a practitioner review and not peer reviewed, argues that "differentiation is a myth" goes further than the survey data support, that the Dirichlet fit weakens in high-involvement, premium, subscription, B2B and new-category settings, and that "loyalty is a myth" is better read as "between-brand loyalty differences are small". Trade coverage shows an ongoing dispute between Sharp and Les Binet and Peter Field over the 60:40 brand-to-activation rule and the suitability of the IPA databank for causal claims, and Mark Ritson's objection is reported to be to "sophisticated mass marketing", arguing some targeting is needed to position a brand; I saw both only second-hand. Treat it as strong generalization for repeat-purchase markets and opinion beyond them.

### Implications for LTF

LTF is a small, new brand in a category (learning to fly in a simulator) where an incumbent reference set exists: the sim itself, YouTube instructors, official manuals. The distinctiveness argument suggests LTF should spend its identity effort on a small number of consistent, recognizable cues rather than on novel visual treatments for each lesson. Concretely: one wordmark and mark used identically on lesson pages, fly mode, progress cards and social previews, and one accent color and type voice repeated across light and dark themes. Because most visitors will not perceive a meaningful functional difference between "yet another flying course" and LTF, the ability to be recognized in a crowded search result or video thumbnail is a growth lever. The emphasis on category entry points also suggests labeling lessons around situations a beginner actually has (first time in the sim, landing, radio calls). The caution is that the evidence base is consumer-goods and service purchase panels, not learning products, so the claim that distinctiveness beats differentiation should be tested on LTF's own sign-up and return data rather than assumed.

## romaniuk2018building

Romaniuk, J. (2018). *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*. Oxford University Press.

### Core claims

Romaniuk gives practitioners a measurement-led method for deciding which non-name elements of a brand (logos, colors, shapes, characters, sounds, taglines, packaging) actually work as distinctive brand assets. The central claim is that an asset is only distinctive if buyers in the category link it to the brand, and that this is an empirical question to be measured, not a matter of design intention or agency belief. The book sits inside the Ehrenberg-Bass view that brands grow by being easy to notice and recall, so assets function as retrieval cues that stand in for the brand name when the name is not visible, as in a quick scan of a shelf, a feed or a thumbnail.

### Specifics

Facts below come from the Oxford University Press Australia and New Zealand catalogue record and the search-engine extracts of it, and from trade coverage that quotes the author.

- Publication: OUP Australia and New Zealand, 16 March 2018; Open Library work OL22541773W, ISBN 0190311506. The author is a research professor and associate director at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, University of South Australia.
- The table of contents, as indexed, includes chapters on measuring asset strength, fame metrics, uniqueness metrics and "the Grid".
- Two criteria define a distinctive asset: fame (the proportion of category buyers who link the asset to the brand) and uniqueness (the extent to which the asset evokes only that brand and not competitors). Fame and uniqueness are plotted on a two-by-two Grid. Secondary sources label the quadrants differently (for example "use or lose", "investment potential", "avoid solo use", "ignore or test"), so exact labels should be checked in the book before quoting.
- A source described as being a quote from Romaniuk in trade press defines assets as the "non-brand name elements that trigger brand recall in the minds of category buyers". I could confirm only that this appears in search extracts attributed to her; I did not confirm the original page.
- Thresholds are reported inconsistently. One practitioner post states a 50 percent figure for each axis; another claims higher cutoffs. I could not tie either to a primary Ehrenberg-Bass document, so no threshold is adopted here.
- Measurement caution reported second-hand: prompted questions can inflate fame scores, so tests should use unprompted or lightly prompted methods.
- A 2023 Journal of Brand Management study, as indexed in search results, tested 44 assets in the UK automotive category and found logo to be the most uniquely ownable asset type; I did not read it.

### Replication and criticism

The Grid is a measurement framework, not a causal experiment, and its reported thresholds are conventions. Independent academic replication of the specific fame-and-uniqueness cutoffs is thin in what I could find; most application is by the Institute's own researchers and by market research firms selling asset-testing tools, so the commercial incentive to use it is high. Some critics in the trade press argue that focusing on assets can come at the expense of the brand name and message; a Mumbrella360 headline carried on marketingscience.info quotes an Ehrenberg-Bass professor saying you are "poorly branding" if you focus on assets over your brand (I saw only the headline, not the talk). The method also measures category buyers' recall, which means the results are category- and market-specific, and a startup with low awareness will score low on fame almost by definition.

### Implications for LTF

For LTF the useful part is the discipline: before investing in a mascot, a cockpit-glyph motif or a signature illustration style, ask whether it could eventually be both famous and unique, and measure it with unprompted tests (for example a small panel shown the asset without the name and asked what it is). Candidate LTF assets are a wordmark treatment, an instrument-derived symbol, an accent color, a distinctive progress-ring motion, and the voice of the briefings. Because LTF will start with near-zero fame, the guidance on low-fame, high-uniqueness assets applies: choose cues that are ownable (not another blue-and-white aviation cliché such as a generic airplane silhouette, which many flight sites share) and then repeat them without variation until fame grows. The risk to avoid is churning the visual identity every quarter. The fly-mode screen, where the interface should recede, should carry only the minimum brand cues.

## romaniuk2007evidence

Romaniuk, J., Sharp, B., & Ehrenberg, A. (2007). Evidence concerning the importance of perceived brand differentiation. *Australasian Marketing Journal, 15*(2), 42-54.

### Core claims

The paper is the main peer-reviewed statement of the case that distinctiveness, not perceived differentiation, should be at the center of brand strategy. The abstract (Crossref record for DOI 10.1016/s1441-3582(07)70042-3) says the research challenges "the central importance of differentiation to brand strategy" and offers an empirically grounded theoretical argument that differentiation plays a more limited role in brand competition than the orthodox literature assumes. The authors then present data, spanning many categories and two countries, showing a low level of perceived differentiation across competing brands, and yet customers still buy those brands. They conclude by placing distinctiveness, meaning a brand building unique associations that make it more easily identifiable, at the center of brand strategy, and call for research on "being distinctive and getting noticed".

### Specifics

- Evidence type: survey-based cross-sectional data on perceived brand attributes and differences, across many product and service categories and two countries; the abstract does not state the number of categories, the sample sizes or the country names, and I did not obtain the full text, so none are given here.
- Concept contrast: differentiation is a perceived and valued difference in what a brand offers; distinctiveness is the property of being identifiable and different in its cues, whether or not the difference is valued. A brand can have a unique logo (distinctive) without that logo implying a functional advantage (differentiated).
- The argument's logic: if buyers in a category see few differences between brands and still buy a range of them, then buying cannot depend mainly on a perceived advantage; salience and ease of recognition matter more.
- The reference list is substantial (61 references per the Crossref record), which suggests the paper reviews the differentiation literature as well as presenting data. A Bournemouth University manuscript indexed in the search results describes the paper as one of several large-scale investigations showing that most category buyers do not perceive differences between brands, and notes that this research stream has led some to question the importance of differentiation.

### Replication and criticism

The low-perceived-differentiation finding comes from the same research tradition that promotes the conclusion, which is a concern for independence; the stronger claim is that buying is not driven by perceived differences. Critics argue that survey measures of perceived differences may miss the differences that matter at purchase, that mainstream-category results do not transfer to premium or high-involvement categories, and that the position can be read as licensing sameness. A practitioner review (boringlyeffective.com) states that the book-length version of the argument overstates "differentiation is a myth". In its own framing the paper recommends distinctiveness as a replacement for differentiation, not an excuse to be similar to competitors. No large preregistered replication of this specific study was found in my searches.

### Implications for LTF

The paper helps settle a recurring design argument for LTF: should the identity say "we are better than other flight courses" (a differentiation claim that beginners will not verify) or "this is recognizably LTF" (distinctiveness)? The evidence favors investing in recognizable cues and consistent presentation, while keeping real product quality (accurate lessons, good challenges) as the thing that retains learners. For copy, avoid superlatives that cannot be demonstrated and instead let consistent structure (the same lesson skeleton, the same checklist presentation, the same debrief format) become the recognizable thing. It also supports resisting "why do all aviation apps look the same" anxiety only up to a point: the paper does not recommend looking like competitors, it recommends being easily told apart. Where LTF's interface necessarily converges with cockpit conventions (instrument layouts, color meanings for warnings), distinctiveness should come from non-functional layers (type, illustration, motion, voice), not from changing conventions that carry safety meaning.

## romaniuk2003measuring

Romaniuk, J., & Sharp, B. (2003). Measuring brand perceptions: Testing quantity and quality. *Journal of Targeting, Measurement and Analysis for Marketing, 11*(3), 218-229.

### Core claims

The paper tests a common assumption of positioning practice: that particular brand perceptions (a single "trigger" attribute, or a chosen cluster of attributes) make customers more loyal, so brands should pick a position and push it. In a subscription market, the authors found little evidence for either. What predicted loyalty was the quantity of perceptions: the more image attributes a respondent associated with a brand, the more loyal that respondent was. The authors read this as a salience or "share of mind" effect, and conclude that distinctiveness is useful for making sure marketing is noticed and correctly attributed, while the source of that distinctiveness is a less important decision. This is an early empirical root of the later Ehrenberg-Bass emphasis on mental availability and distinctive assets.

### Specifics

I read the full text of the author's accepted article (Springer-hosted PDF for DOI 10.1057/palgrave.jt.5740079).

- Setting: a subscription market in which customers normally use one provider (the paper gives home loans and electricity supply as examples), so switching is "defection". Business users with influence over supplier choice were interviewed by telephone. Three competing brands covering over 95 percent of the market had sample sizes of 4,000, 900 and 350.
- Measures: attributes (pricing, service, expertise, relationship quality, all positively worded) were chosen by the lead brand's agency and research department; respondents indicated which brands they associated with each attribute. Loyalty was an 11-point probabilistic measure of switching derived from the Juster scale, inverted so that higher meant more likely to stay.
- Hypothesis 1 (single attributes): across the three brands, associating the brand with an attribute went with higher loyalty, but only slightly; the mean difference was 0.6 of a scale point (the text equates this to a 6 percent difference in defection probability), with attribute means ranging between 0.4 and 1.0. No attribute acted as a trigger.
- Hypothesis 2 (multi-attribute positions): eight positions of up to four attributes were tested (service, expertise, information or solution, relationship, pricing, plus three mixed combinations). Differences in loyalty ranged from 0.7 to 2.1 points, mostly around 1.5, with no brand showing an ideal position.
- Hypothesis 3 (salience): there was a consistent linear relationship between the number of times a brand was mentioned across the attributes and loyalty. The baseline loyalty was about six out of ten, and each additional mention raised loyalty by about 0.1 point; the paper notes a slight brand-size effect that was relatively minor.
- Recommendation: monitor all attributes rather than a few "key" ones; build the "bank" of perceptions over the long term; in the short term pick whichever attribute gives the best creative execution, and brand communications prominently so people know who is advertising.

### Replication and criticism

This is a single study in one subscription market with business customers, and the authors themselves say it studied only one aspect of behavior (retention, not acquisition) and recommend replication across other markets, especially fast-moving consumer goods. They also concede that the attribute count could be a crude attitude measure, so salience and attitude accounts are not fully separated. Later work extends the salience view (Romaniuk and Sharp, 2004, Marketing Theory, conceptualizes brand salience as propensity to be noticed or come to mind in buying situations), but the claim that "position does not matter" is contested by positioning practitioners and has not, as far as I found, been established in the same strength for products that are chosen once rather than repeatedly.

### Implications for LTF

Two practical points follow for a learning product. First, do not over-invest in choosing the single "right" brand promise ("the fastest way to solo", "the safest way to learn"); the data suggest that breadth of associations (a beginner thinking of LTF for pre-flight checks, for landing practice, for radio calls, for understanding instruments) matters more than any one claim. This supports building the identity around a consistent presence across many lesson types. Second, prominent and distinct branding of any message matters because competitors can run near-identical claims. In the product, every lesson header, challenge briefing and progress summary should make the LTF identity unmistakable at a glance, because a learner may arrive from a search result or a shared link without the home page context. The caveat is that the evidence concerns subscription customers and retention, so treat it as a hypothesis to test on LTF's own return-visit data.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record for DOI 10.1057/palgrave.jt.5740079 (title, authors, 2003, volume 11, issue 3, pages 218-229); full text read from link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1057/palgrave.jt.5740079.pdf (abstract, hypotheses, method, results, discussion, limitations).

## ehrenberg1990double

Ehrenberg, A. S. C., Goodhardt, G. J., & Barwise, T. P. (1990). Double jeopardy revisited. *Journal of Marketing, 54*(3), 82-91.

### Core claims

The paper documents and explains "double jeopardy" (DJ): in any given period a small brand typically has far fewer buyers than a larger brand, and in addition its buyers tend to buy it less often. The authors treat this as a widespread empirical regularity, review the evidence for it, set out theories that account for it, describe known exceptions and deviations, and draw practical implications (Crossref abstract for DOI 10.1177/002224299005400307). The pattern was first described in a different field by William McPhee in 1963, as reported in a secondary source I fetched, and the 1990 paper brought it into mainstream marketing as a law-like regularity of buying behavior.

### Specifics

I could read only the abstract and secondary accounts, not the article's tables.

- The regularity: market share differences between brands are mostly differences in the number of buyers (penetration); loyalty measures such as how often buyers buy the brand vary only modestly and in a way that follows from brand size. So low-share brands are penalized twice.
- The abstract says the paper covers "the theories that account for its occurrence". A practitioner review of Sharp's book (boringlyeffective.com) names the NBD-Dirichlet model as the statistical model used in this tradition to describe and predict share, penetration and purchase frequency, so that is the likely theoretical frame, but I did not confirm it from the 1990 text.
- Exceptions: a secondary source states that the two main deviations are niche brands (unusually low penetration but higher loyalty than expected) and change-of-pace brands (unusually high penetration but low repeat purchase, as with seasonal products), and that these are identified by checking deviations from the expected relationship. I did not confirm the data behind this in the paper itself.
- Later reviews state that DJ has been generalized to many markets including consumer packaged goods, durables and services; I saw this only in search extracts, so the breadth of generalization should be checked before being used as a design claim.
- Implication in the abstract's own words is only "practical implications"; the Sharp book and Ehrenberg-Bass publications later developed the managerial reading that growth requires more buyers, not only more loyal buyers.

### Replication and criticism

DJ is among the better-replicated regularities in marketing, being repeatedly shown with panel data across product types and countries, and the existence of the identified exceptions (niche and change-of-pace brands) is part of the original account, not an after-the-fact patch. Nonetheless, the managerial extrapolations are disputed. A later study of deviations for small and medium share brands, found only as a search extract, reports that on average more than half of the brands show marked and persistent deviations, which would qualify how universally "DJ" can be applied to a specific brand. Critics also point out that DJ is a pattern of aggregate buying, not a causal mechanism; a brand cannot simply be "made bigger" to be more loyal. For learning products, which are not repeat-purchase packaged goods, the pattern may not carry over directly.

### Implications for LTF

The design lesson is about how to read LTF's own metrics. A small brand with few learners will also show lower repeat visits and lower completion per learner; this does not by itself mean the lessons are poor or the identity is weak. LTF should therefore avoid over-reading loyalty-style numbers (streak lengths, return frequency) as measures of product quality early on, and compare them against category expectations where they exist. It also argues for putting resources into reach and easy recognition (consistent identity, shareable lesson pages and progress cards with the same branding) before building elaborate retention mechanics. In the product, avoid presenting a "your loyalty" framing to learners; the dashboard should report progress against learning goals, not against brand attachment.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record for DOI 10.1177/002224299005400307 (title, authors, 1990, volume 54, issue 3, pages 82-91, abstract, 47 references); WebSearch extracts naming McPhee (1963) and the niche and change-of-pace exceptions; article tables not read.

## ehrenberg2002brand

Ehrenberg, A., Barnard, N., Kennedy, R., & Bloom, H. (2002). Brand advertising as creative publicity. *Journal of Advertising Research, 42*(4).

### Core claims

The paper argues that most brand advertising works as publicity (making the brand salient and familiar) and rarely persuades. The abstract on the WARC page (warc.com/content/article/jar/brand-advertising-as-creative-publicity/en-gb/77314) says "Advertising seldom seems to persuade" and that "Advertising in a competitive market needs to maintain the brand's broad salience", meaning "being a brand the consumer buys or considers buying". It adds that this turns on brand awareness "but together with memory associations, familiarity, and brand assurance", and that publicity can also help develop salience. The practical consequence stated by the authors is that this publicity view should change both the briefs given to agencies (the abstract's example is that cut-through matters more than having a persuasive selling proposition) and how results are evaluated.

### Specifics

- Evidence type: an argumentative review paper from the Ehrenberg tradition, drawing on prior empirical work (including the same group's "nudging" research, a 1997 paper in the same journal listed in Crossref with the title "Advertising: Strongly persuasive or nudging?"), not a new experiment. I saw only the abstract, so I cannot report datasets or numbers.
- Key terms in the abstract: salience (being a brand the consumer buys or considers buying), brand awareness, memory associations, familiarity, brand assurance, publicity, cut-through, persuasive selling proposition.
- The concluding question in the abstract is notable: since few advertisements seem to actively seek to persuade, how much do the advertisements themselves have to change, rather than how we think and talk about them?
- Bibliographic caution: the Crossref record gives the title, authors, year (2002), journal, volume 42 and issue 4, but its page field ("1-13") disagrees with the DOI string (jar-42-4-7-18) and with a WARC citation listing that appeared in search results ("7-18"); pages are therefore left blank in sources.json.
- Context: the abstract's concept of broad salience is the forerunner of the later Sharp and Romaniuk term mental availability; the link is my inference from the vocabulary, not a statement in the abstract.

### Replication and criticism

The "advertising seldom persuades" position is contested. Advocates of the persuasion or emotional-response view, and effectiveness researchers using campaign award databases, argue that advertising can change preferences and long-term brand effects, so the strong version of the publicity claim is an opinion supported by selective evidence. The paper is also not a randomized test of publicity against persuasion. Within the Ehrenberg tradition the claim is consistent with other findings (low perceived differentiation, small loyalty differences), but that internal agreement is not independent replication. Treat this as a well-argued expert position with a research-tradition behind it, not as settled science.

### Implications for LTF

For a product whose ads, social previews and share cards are tiny, the publicity view argues for making every outward-facing piece instantly attributable and memorable rather than argumentative. Concretely, LTF's share cards, lesson thumbnails and email headers should be recognizable as LTF before any text is read: consistent frame, mark, type and color. Inside the product, the same principle applies to onboarding: rather than persuading a beginner with claims about teaching quality, show a short, branded, successful first experience (a first lesson, a first quiz result) that builds familiarity and assurance. The warning is for copywriting: avoid long persuasive pages to win new learners and prefer short, repeated, distinctive messages. Because the evidence is about advertising, not about the product experience, it should not be used to justify skipping real product quality or honest instruction.

Confirmed this session: Crossref records for DOI 10.2501/jar-42-4-7-18 (title, authors, 2002, volume 42, issue 4; page field inconsistent) and for the 1997 nudging paper; abstract text read on warc.com/content/article/jar/brand-advertising-as-creative-publicity/en-gb/77314; full text not read.

## aaker1997dimensions

Aaker, J. L. (1997). Dimensions of brand personality. *Journal of Marketing Research, 34*(3), 347-356.

### Core claims

Aaker defines brand personality as "the set of human characteristics associated with a brand" and argues it can be described by five dimensions, found by factor analysis of how people rate well-known brands on human traits: Sincerity, Excitement, Competence, Sophistication and Ruggedness. She develops a 42-item Brand Personality Scale and claims it is reliable, valid and generalizable across product categories. The paper also argues that brand personality serves a symbolic or self-expressive function, in contrast to product attributes, which serve a utilitarian function, and that consumers imbue brands with human traits (animism).

### Specifics

I read the full text of the article (a JSTOR-derived copy hosted at gwern.net).

- Item pool: 309 candidate traits were assembled from psychology personality scales, marketers' scales and original qualitative work, then reduced to 114 traits.
- Study 1 sample: 631 respondents (about 55 percent of 1,200 questionnaires mailed to a national panel, with a sample intended to represent the US population) rated a subset of 37 brands on the 114 traits using a Likert scale of how well each trait describes the brand. The brands included symbolic, utilitarian and mixed categories, with Levi's jeans included in every subset as a control.
- Analysis: principal components analysis of the 114 by 114 correlation matrix of traits across the 37 brands. A five-factor solution explained 92 percent of the variance in brand personality, and was chosen on scree, interpretability and stability across subsamples (for example male versus female, younger versus older).
- Dimensions and typical brands named in the paper: Sincerity (Hallmark cards), Excitement (MTV), Competence (The Wall Street Journal), Sophistication (Guess jeans), Ruggedness (Nike tennis shoes). The final scale has 15 facets (four each for Sincerity and Excitement, three for Competence, two each for Sophistication and Ruggedness) with three traits per facet, giving 45 traits, then 42 after the test-retest step dropped three.
- Reliability: test-retest correlations for the five dimensions were between .74 and .77; Cronbach's alphas were .93 (Sincerity), .95 (Excitement), .93 (Competence), .91 (Sophistication) and .90 (Ruggedness).
- Confirmation: a second independent sample of 180 respondents rated 20 brands in 10 categories on the 42 traits. A confirmatory factor analysis with correlated factors gave CFI .98, GFI .91, AGFI .86 and RMSR .07.
- Comparison with the human Big Five: Sincerity, Excitement and Competence resemble Agreeableness, Extroversion and Conscientiousness, whereas Sophistication and Ruggedness do not map to any of the five human dimensions. Aaker suggests these two tap aspirations people desire but do not necessarily have.

### Replication and criticism

The scale became the dominant instrument in the field, but it has been criticized repeatedly. Azoulay and Kapferer (2003, Journal of Brand Management, DOI 10.1057/palgrave.bm.2540162) argue that such scales merge several dimensions of brand identity, personality being only one, so that they do not strictly measure personality (I saw this in the abstract extract via search, not the full paper). Secondary reviews report that the five factors do not replicate cleanly across cultures, with only partial overlap in later work, and that Aaker's item pool includes demographic traits such as gender, age and class, blurring brand personality with user imagery. Romaniuk and Ehrenberg (2012, also in this lane) go further and question whether consumers attribute personality traits to brands at all. The data are also US consumers' ratings of famous brands from the 1990s. Evidence strength: a careful psychometric study, but ratings of well-known brands say little about unknown ones.

### Implications for LTF

For LTF the useful reading is that personality is a tool for choosing the voice and visual character of the product, not a proven driver of purchase. If LTF wants to be seen as Competence (the paper's highest-correlating traits are reliable, responsible, dependable and efficient) with a trace of Sincerity (domestic, honest, genuine, cheerful), then copy, error messages, checklist wording and color should consistently support that: calm tone, no hype, no gamified bravado. Ruggedness cues (tough, strong, outdoorsy) and Excitement cues (daring, spirited, imaginative) may feel at odds with a safety-minded beginner audience, though they could be applied sparingly for challenge success states. The scale can be repurposed as a lightweight audit: have five to ten beginners rate LTF screens on a handful of these traits to see if the perceived character matches the intended one. Do not treat the five-factor structure as a guarantee that the audience will perceive LTF in these terms, and do not use the scale to predict conversion.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record for DOI 10.2307/3151897 (title, author, 1997, volume 34, issue 3, page 347); full text read via gwern.net/doc/economics/1997-aaker.pdf (abstract, definition, samples, statistics, discussion); WebSearch extracts for the 2003 critique and cross-cultural replication.

## romaniuk2012brands

Romaniuk, J., & Ehrenberg, A. (2012). Do brands lack personality? *Marketing Theory, 12*(3), 333-339.

### Core claims

This seven-page commentary asks two questions about the brand personality concept: do consumers see brands as having human traits (the "brand as human" idea), and is there a consistent consumer view of a brand that gives it a distinct personality that separates it from competitors (the "brand personality as metaphor" idea)? Based on their own empirical evidence, the authors answer no to both. The Crossref abstract states that respondents rarely attribute personality-based traits to a brand (the examples given are "Charming" and "Helpful") and that there is little evidence of distinct brand personalities for competing brands. They conclude that the value of brand personality to consumers choosing brands, and to marketers designing brand strategy, is questionable.

### Specifics

- Type of evidence: a commentary replying to another paper ("Brand Relationship and Personality Theory: Metaphor or Consumer Perceptual Reality?"), using the authors' own prior data on brand perceptions; the abstract gives no sample sizes or numbers, and I did not obtain the full text, so none are reported.
- The two tests proposed are well suited to a design context: (1) incidence, that is, how often respondents attach human-trait descriptors to brands when given a free choice; and (2) distinctiveness, that is, whether the traits attached differ between competing brands.
- The finding of little brand-to-brand differentiation in personality echoes the same research group's earlier work on perceived brand differentiation (Romaniuk, Sharp and Ehrenberg 2007, in this lane) and on image attributes, where perceptions of competing brands in a category tended to be similar.
- The commentary has only five references per the Crossref record, so it is an argument piece, not a literature review.

### Replication and criticism

The paper is a short expert commentary, so its evidence strength is moderate at best, and it comes from the group that also champions distinctiveness over differentiation, so it is not an independent test. The opposing view is represented by Aaker (1997) and by the brand relationship literature (Fournier 1998, also in this lane), where consumers readily attribute personality-like traits and relationship roles to brands in depth interviews; the difference may partly be one of method (forced, scaled ratings versus free responses, interviews versus pick-any surveys). Azoulay and Kapferer (2003) criticize Aaker from a different angle: that the scales do not measure personality in a strict sense, which is compatible with the claim that personality is not what drives brand perception. There is no consensus that has overturned either side.

### Implications for LTF

This commentary is a useful brake on over-investing in brand personality work. For a small product, a detailed personality brief ("LTF is a friendly, confident, adventurous instructor") may not be perceived by learners at all, and what they will notice instead is whether the product is clear, calm and easily recognized. A practical approach for LTF: write a simple tone-of-voice guide with a few concrete rules (second person, no jargon without explanation, no exclamation marks in safety-related text) instead of a personality essay, and then test whether new learners can describe the product in their own words after a first session. If most descriptions use plain functional terms (clear, easy, step by step) rather than human traits, that is consistent with this paper's finding and suggests that recognizability and clarity should take precedence over persona work. For the mascot or character question, the paper offers no support for adding one purely to inject personality.

## keller1993conceptualizing

Keller, K. L. (1993). Conceptualizing, measuring, and managing customer-based brand equity. *Journal of Marketing, 57*(1), 1-22.

### Core claims

Keller defines customer-based brand equity as "the differential effect of brand knowledge on consumer response to the marketing of the brand". A brand has positive equity when consumers react more favorably to an element of the marketing mix than they would to the same element attributed to a fictitiously named or unnamed version of the product. Brand knowledge is the key: it has two components, brand awareness (recall and recognition) and brand image (the set of associations held in memory). Equity arises when the consumer is aware of the brand and holds some favorable, strong and unique associations. The paper's memory framework is an associative network model in which nodes are linked with different strengths.

### Specifics

I read the full text (a copy hosted by Duke University).

- Brand awareness: brand recognition is the ability to confirm prior exposure when the brand is given as a cue; brand recall is the ability to generate the brand from memory when given the category or other cue. Their relative importance depends on whether choices are made at the point of purchase (recognition) or before (recall).
- Types of associations, ordered by increasing scope: attributes, benefits and attitudes. Attributes are product-related (the physical composition or service requirements) or non-product-related. Keller lists four non-product-related attributes: price information, packaging or product appearance information, user imagery (who uses it) and usage imagery (where and in what situations it is used). Benefits are functional, experiential or symbolic. Brand attitudes are overall evaluations, typically modeled as a function of beliefs about attributes and benefits.
- Three dimensions of associations that matter: favorability, strength and uniqueness. Strength depends on the amount and quality of processing at encoding; uniqueness is whether the association is shared with competitors.
- Brand identities: the paper defines a brand by Kotler's formulation as a name, term, sign, symbol or design, and calls individual components "brand identities" (name, logo, symbol and so on). It emphasizes the brand name but states that similar criteria apply to the logo or symbol.
- Choice criteria for a name: simple, familiar and distinctive. Ease of comprehension, pronunciation and spelling help encoding; mnemonic factors and vivid words help learning; a familiar word helps recall while a more unusual word can help recognition (the paper cites past work that high-frequency words are easier to recall while low-frequency words can be easier to recognize). A "flicker perception" test (how quickly a name can be perceived when exposed briefly) is mentioned as used by researchers.
- Identities should be chosen to be mutually reinforcing, but the paper states that although careful identity choice can contribute significantly, "the primary input comes from supporting marketing activities".

### Replication and criticism

The framework is conceptual and has been used as the starting point for many empirical studies, rather than being a hypothesis that has been replicated or refuted. Critiques focus on measurement: how to quantify favorability, strength and uniqueness, and on its reliance on consumers' stored associations, which the Ehrenberg-Bass tradition reads as too attitude-oriented (see Romaniuk and Sharp, 2003, in this lane, who find that the number of associations rather than their kind relates to loyalty). Keller himself notes in the paper that remarkably little empirical research had at that point examined brand name and logo considerations, and calls for more research on how names, logos, symbols and slogans contribute to equity. The paper is a canonical academic framework, strong on structure, but not empirical evidence.

### Implications for LTF

The association taxonomy is directly usable as a checklist for LTF's identity. Product-related attributes (accurate lessons, sim-verified challenges), non-product attributes (price, appearance, who learns here, when), and symbolic benefits (feeling like a pilot) each need a deliberate statement and a place in the product. The uniqueness dimension is the useful warning: if LTF's associations (aviation, learning, blue sky) are all shared with other flight apps, there will be no differential effect. The name guidance (simple, familiar, distinctive, easy to spell and say) applies to lesson, mode and feature names as well as to the product name: "Fly mode" is simple and familiar; avoid coined feature names that a beginner must learn first. The recall versus recognition distinction matters for how LTF is found: learners who search from memory need a recallable name and phrase, while those scanning a feed need recognizable visual cues.

## henderson1998guidelines

Henderson, P. W., & Cote, J. A. (1998). Guidelines for selecting or modifying logos. *Journal of Marketing, 62*(2), 14-30.

### Core claims

The authors turn logo choice from taste into a small set of measurable design properties and then tie those properties to what a company wants a logo to achieve. Using 195 logos calibrated on 13 design characteristics, they identify three communication objectives that a logo can serve (high recognition, low investment, and high image) and state which design profile suits each (Crossref abstract for DOI 10.1177/002224299806200202). The paper is the origin of the three design dimensions, elaborateness, naturalness and harmony, that later logo research keeps using.

### Specifics

- Abstract-level guidance: high-recognition logos (accurate recognition created by high investment) should be very natural, very harmonious and moderately elaborate. Low-investment logos (which produce a false sense of knowing and positive affect) should be less natural and very harmonious. High-image logos (a professional look and a strong positive image) must be moderately elaborate and natural.
- Dimension definitions, as paraphrased in a later paper by van der Lans and colleagues (Marketing Science 2009, also in this lane): elaborateness refers to a design's richness and ability to capture the essence of an object; natural designs depict commonly experienced objects; harmony refers to the congruency of the patterns and parts of a design. The same paper explains that these dimensions are made of lower-level design elements (the 2009 paper lists complexity, activeness, depth, representativeness, organicity, roundness, symmetry and balance) rated by designers, plus objective elements (repetition, proportion, parallelism).
- Responses measured in this tradition: positive affect, shared meaning (consensus about the first meaning a logo brings to mind), subjective familiarity (feeling of having seen the logo before even with no prior exposure), and true and false recognition. The 2009 paper credits the 1998 study with showing that subjective familiarity can enhance brand choice.
- Method as described by later authors: stimulus-level aesthetics research in which separate groups of designers and consumers rate each logo, and the ratings are averaged for each logo (the 2009 paper points out that this averaging masks individual heterogeneity).
- A student thesis indexed in the search results summarizes the finding that medium elaborateness drew more positive attitudinal responses, and that logos that use recognizable icons such as animals, objects, characters or places are more easily recognized and learned; I treat these as secondary summaries.

### Replication and criticism

The three-dimension structure has been reproduced and extended. The 2009 study across ten countries found the dimensions to be similar across countries, and found responses to naturalness and harmony to be especially consistent with the 1998 and 2003 findings, with the largest differences for elaborate designs (a more negative reaction to elaborate designs in a Russian cluster). Later logo experiments, for example Luffarelli, Mukesh and Mahmood (2019, in this lane), still use the 1998 method of showing logos and collecting averaged ratings. Limitations: I could not confirm the 1998 sample (the 2009 extension used business undergraduates for recognition); logo evaluation in a lab differs from brand experience in the world; the abstract-level guidelines are not experimentally verified causal rules, since logos differ on many uncontrolled properties; and the defined "optimal" ranges are correlational patterns across 195 existing logos. I did not find a replication specifically on app icons or interface marks.

### Implications for LTF

LTF needs at least two kinds of marks. A product mark that should be learned quickly (the high-recognition profile: natural, harmonious, moderately elaborate) and small utility marks such as lesson-type icons, which need to be instantly readable at small sizes. The guidance suggests that a depiction of something familiar (a yoke, a wing, an instrument face) will be learned faster than an abstract shape, but a purely literal airplane silhouette would fall into a shared-category look, so moderate elaboration with a distinctive treatment is the target. "Very harmonious" translates to consistent geometry: shared corner radii, stroke weights and proportions across the mark, the instrument widgets and the lesson icons. Harmony also feeds false recognition (the sense of already knowing a logo), which is a risk for LTF: a very harmonious but generic mark may be mistaken for other aviation brands. The low-investment profile (less natural, very harmonious) matters for a new product with little marketing budget, since it can produce a feeling of familiarity; LTF should check with test participants that familiarity does not come from resemblance to a competitor's mark.

## vanderlans2009cross

van der Lans, R., Cote, J. A., Cole, C. A., Leong, S. M., Smidts, A., Henderson, P. W., Bluemelhuber, C., Bottomley, P. A., Doyle, J. R., Fedorikhin, A., Moorthy, J., Ramaseshan, B., & Schmitt, B. H. (2009). Cross-national logo evaluation analysis: An individual-level approach. *Marketing Science, 28*(5), 968-985.

### Core claims

The paper tests whether the logo design dimensions identified by Henderson and Cote (elaborateness, naturalness and harmony) are universal and whether people respond to them in similar ways across cultures. Using data from ten countries (Argentina, Australia, China, Germany, Great Britain, India, the Netherlands, Russia, Singapore and the United States), the authors find that the dimensions are similar across countries and that responses are "relatively consistent", with minor differences across three cross-national clusters (the West, Asia and Russia). The managerial conclusion in the abstract is that firms can implement a global logo strategy, but can also optimize logos for specific countries.

### Specifics

I read the full text (hosted by Columbia Business School).

- Stimuli and raters: consumer and designer ratings of 195 logos, the same set size as Henderson and Cote (1998). Designers rated subjective design elements (complexity, activeness, depth, representativeness, organicity, roundness, symmetry, balance), which form the three dimensions; objective elements were repetition, proportion (the ratio of width to height) and parallelism (number of parallel lines).
- Responses: positive affect, shared meaning, subjective familiarity, true recognition and false recognition. For recognition, in each country five groups of about 30 business undergraduates viewed a subset of 39 logos for two seconds each, did a distracter task for about 10 minutes, and then viewed a booklet of 78 logos (39 targets, 39 distracters), indicating which they had seen. True recognition was the percentage correctly recognizing a target, and false recognition the percentage claiming to recognize a distracter.
- Statistical approach: a Bayesian finite-mixture structural equation model with a concomitant variable allowing cluster probabilities to vary by country, to avoid averaging away individual-level differences.
- Summary of results: natural designs universally increase positive affect, shared meaning, subjective familiarity and true recognition and decrease false recognition. Designs high in harmony universally increase positive affect, subjective familiarity and false recognition without affecting shared meaning or true recognition. The largest cluster differences were for elaborate designs: mostly they increased affect, familiarity and recognition (true and false) while decreasing shared meaning, but the Russian cluster responded differently (a much smaller effect on affect and a negative effect on familiarity). The authors offer a tentative explanation based on uncertainty avoidance (Arrindell et al.), which they present as a possible explanation, not a tested one.
- The paper notes that the design dimensions explain only a modest share of true and false recognition.

### Replication and criticism

This is the most direct extension of the 1998 logo work and is partly a replication across populations, reaching broadly similar conclusions. Its limits are stated by its own design: student samples for the recognition test, a fixed logo set of 195 existing designs, and stimulus-level ratings by designers. The finding of "universality" concerns the structure of perceived design and the sign of the responses, not the size of the effects, so it supports the dimensions as a vocabulary and not as a precise predictor. The role of culture in the elaborate-design effect rests on a post hoc explanation and a small number of country clusters, so the result for Russia should be treated cautiously. No independent replication focused on mobile-app marks was found.

### Implications for LTF

For a product aimed at English-speaking beginners worldwide, the main takeaway is reassuring: a natural, harmonious mark should work with similar effects across the countries studied, so LTF need not maintain different logo versions for different regions. The practical risks sit with elaborate or highly decorative treatments, where responses vary most. If LTF later localizes, the identity (mark, color, geometry) should be held constant and variation limited to language and examples. The distinction between true and false recognition is a useful test design for LTF: show target and distracter marks (including competitor marks) in a quick-exposure test and count how often people claim to recognize a mark they have not seen; a high false-recognition rate warns of resemblance to others. The paper's two-second exposure is also a reasonable proxy for the glance a learner gives a tab icon or a share-card thumbnail.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record and abstract for DOI 10.1287/mksc.1080.0462; full text read from business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/4563/cross_national_logo.pdf.

## luffarelli2019let

Luffarelli, J., Mukesh, M., & Mahmood, A. (2019). Let the logo do the talking: The influence of logo descriptiveness on brand equity. *Journal of Marketing Research, 56*(5), 862-878.

### Core claims

Logo descriptiveness is the extent to which a logo's textual or visual elements indicate the type of product the brand sells (for example Costa Coffee's coffee beans and the word "coffee", versus Starbucks, which does not). Across six studies (four experiments, one large survey and one secondary-data study, plus four further experiments in web appendices) the authors show that more descriptive logos improve brand evaluations, purchase intentions and brand performance. The mechanism is processing ease: descriptive logos are easier to process, which elicits stronger impressions of authenticity (authentic, trustworthy, credible), which consumers value. Two moderators matter: the effect is much weaker for familiar brands and reverses for brands whose product type has negative associations.

### Specifics

I read the accepted manuscript (Sussex Research Online copy).

- Study 1: 180 online participants (Mechanical Turk) saw one of two fabricated logo pairs (basketball equipment, running shoes), each with a more and a less descriptive version matched in a pretest (n = 80) on symmetry, complexity, likability, familiarity and dynamism. Descriptive logos produced stronger authenticity impressions via ease of processing.
- Study 3: 174 real logos from UK crowdfunding sites (relatively unknown startups) were rated by 1,327 people for purchase intentions and authenticity, and by a separate 1,303 people for descriptiveness, with numerous design characteristics controlled. Authenticity mediated the effect on purchase intentions, and logo type (wordmark, icon-only, mixed) did not moderate it.
- Study 4: the benefit of the more descriptive logo shrank when participants were familiar with the brand versus unfamiliar, though the indirect effect through authenticity stayed positive (90 percent intervals above zero in both groups).
- Study 5: 240 Prolific participants; a descriptive logo with oil drops helped an olive oil producer but hurt a palm oil producer (pretest: olive oil rated more positively than palm oil, 6.15 versus 3.95 on a nine-point scale).
- Study 6: among 479 consumer-facing (B2C) brands in the 2013 KLD Stats data, Compustat financials were available for 423; logo descriptiveness was positively associated with gross profit (beta = .16, p = .005), and with EBITDA and net income at marginal significance (p = .071 and p = .053).
- Practitioner implication: among the 597 brands studied in Studies 3 and 6, 41 percent of logos were descriptive and 59 percent were not.
- The authors find no evidence that logo descriptiveness interacts with brand personality, and no substantial difference between goods and service brands.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence strength: a multi-study paper with experiments, a survey and archival data, which is stronger than typical logo studies, but it is observational for the profit finding (association, with controls), uses online panel participants and mostly fictional or little-known brands, and was published in a single article by one team, so independent replication is still awaited in what I found. The reversal for negatively valenced products shows boundary conditions, and the attenuation for familiar brands means the effect matters mainly early in a brand's life. My own observation is that the result sits in tension with the minimalist logo trend, and a design-press commentary (AIGA Eye on Design, listed in search results but not read) engages with this; I report only that it exists. The finding should not be read as universal advice to add literal pictures: the authors' own data show wordmark, icon and mixed forms all work.

### Implications for LTF

LTF is exactly the case where this paper applies most: an unfamiliar brand in a category where learners want credibility. A mark or lockup that tells people at a glance that this is about flying (a wing, yoke, instrument or runway element, or the word "fly" in the lockup) should be learned faster and read as more trustworthy than a purely abstract shape. The reversal result is a caution for any imagery that evokes negative associations of flying (a crash, a stall, a red warning) in the brand mark or in thumbnails for beginner lessons. For the in-product icon set, descriptiveness argues for literal, labeled icons (a landing-gear glyph for the gear lesson) over abstract tokens. As LTF becomes familiar, the benefit should decline, leaving room to simplify the mark later without penalty.

## orth2008holistic

Orth, U. R., & Malkewitz, K. (2008). Holistic package design and consumer brand impressions. *Journal of Marketing, 72*(3), 64-81.

### Core claims

Instead of studying single package elements (color, shape, typeface) one at a time, the authors treat a package as a whole and ask which kinds of overall design exist and what brand impressions each creates. Seven studies identify key types of package designs and the factors that differentiate them, and relate these holistic designs to consumer impressions of the brand (Crossref abstract for DOI 10.1509/jmkg.72.3.064). They simplify package selection into five holistic types: massive, contrasting, natural, delicate and nondescript. The paper matches types to the personality dimensions of Aaker (1997): sincere brands should have natural package designs, exciting brands contrasting designs, competent brands delicate designs, sophisticated brands natural or delicate designs, and rugged brands contrasting or massive designs.

### Specifics

- The abstract gives the type labels, the mapping to personality impressions and the number of studies (seven). It also says the authors discuss trade-offs among the impressions that holistic types create and illustrate findings with many real packages.
- Later authors place the method in the same tradition as Henderson and Cote's logo work, following the earlier approach of showing stimuli to respondents and collecting ratings (Luffarelli et al. 2019 list Henderson and Cote 1998, Henderson, Giese and Cote 2004 and Orth and Malkewitz 2008 as an "established method"). A 2009 cross-national logo paper (van der Lans et al.) describes the Orth and Malkewitz work as applying the elaborateness, naturalness and harmony dimensions to wine bottle design.
- A 2024 review of packaging research (Ghorbani and Westermann, Journal of Product and Brand Management) characterizes the study as a holistic, sender-perspective investigation: it examines how brands can manipulate package designs to convey intended value propositions, as opposed to how consumers themselves evaluate packaging.
- The Crossref record lists 71 references.
- I could not confirm the product categories in the seven studies from the abstract alone; indexed keywords in search results suggest wine and perfume, but I report this only as a lead.

### Replication and criticism

The five-type structure is cited as a foundation for later packaging work, and its link between design types and Aaker-style personality is plausible and consistent with logo research. Criticisms to bear in mind: it is a sender-perspective design (brands choose the design to produce a target impression), the tested categories are limited, the personality mapping inherits all the weaknesses of the Aaker scale (see Romaniuk and Ehrenberg 2012 and Azoulay and Kapferer 2003 in this lane), and the 2024 review argues that consumers use more criteria than a designer-defined typology, twelve in its qualitative work, including practical and symbolic ones. The mapping is based on ratings by consumers who saw static images, not on actual purchasing behavior. I found no preregistered replication.

### Implications for LTF

The paper shows that the overall gestalt of a design, more than individual elements, carries the impression. For LTF, this argues for judging the interface and cards as whole compositions in review (a lesson page, a briefing card), not only checking colors and fonts individually. If the intended impression is competent and sincere, the mapping points toward natural and delicate designs, meaning calm, refined, restrained compositions over contrasting, heavy or high-energy ones. Dashboards and fly mode, where the instrument widgets sit next to the sim, should avoid massive or contrasting treatments that would compete with the sim and signal bravado. Treat the map as a hypothesis for LTF's visual language and verify it by showing learners screens and asking what impression they get. Keep in mind that the personality framework underlying it has contested validity.
