# Lane 29: Instructional design models and evidence

Working notes for the Learn to Fly Design Knowledge Base. Each section covers one source, in the order the sources were verified and read.

## alfieri2011does

Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich and Tenenbaum (2011), "Does discovery-based instruction enhance learning?", Journal of Educational Psychology 103(1), 1-18.

### Core claims

The paper is the first systematic meta-analysis of the discovery-learning literature, written to settle a long argument between advocates of constructivist, discovery-style teaching and critics who favor explicit instruction. It runs two meta-analyses on a pool of 164 studies. The first compares unassisted discovery (learners get no guidance and no feedback) with explicit instruction. The second compares enhanced or assisted discovery (learners still construct the knowledge but get scaffolding, feedback, worked examples or prompts to explain) with other forms of instruction. The authors' conclusion, in the abstract, is that unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding and elicited explanations do.

### Specifics

Method. Studies were found mainly through PsycINFO, ERIC and Dissertation Abstracts International, plus citation chasing. The first analysis required a direct test of an explicit-instruction condition against a condition with no guidance or feedback during the learning task. The second required a condition with guidance plus a comparison condition. Studies with only qualitative data or unusable statistics were excluded. Effects were coded as Cohen's d with random-effects models.

Headline numbers (all from the paper's own text, fetched this session). Unassisted discovery: 580 comparisons from 108 studies. The abstract reports d = -0.38 with 95% CI [-.44, -.31] at the comparison level; the study-level analysis gave d = -0.38 with CI [-.50, -.25]. Negative means explicit instruction won. Enhanced discovery: 360 comparisons from 56 studies, d = 0.30, 95% CI [.23, .36] at the comparison level and [.15, .44] at the study level. The authors call both effects small but meaningful.

Moderators for the unassisted comparison. Against unassisted discovery, worked examples did best among the explicit conditions (d = -0.63), then feedback (d = -0.46), direct teaching (-0.29) and explanations provided (-0.28). Domain mattered: the explicit advantage was small in math (-0.16), larger in science (-0.39) and problem solving (-0.48), and largest in verbal and social skills (-0.95). Age mattered: all age groups favored explicit teaching, and the gap was larger for adolescents (-0.53) than for adults (-0.26). Publication tier mattered too: first-tier journals gave a larger explicit advantage (-0.67) than lower-tier journals (-0.24), though the reported fail-safe numbers were in the thousands of unpublished studies.

Moderators for the enhanced comparison. Enhanced discovery beat comparison conditions in every domain coded as favorable, with the largest effects in physical and motor skills (1.05) and computer skills (0.64) and the smallest in science (0.11). Adults gained more than children. Unpublished studies and dissertations leaned against enhanced discovery (-0.54), another publication-pattern wrinkle.

The authors define discovery learning as any situation where the learner is not given the target information and must find it with only the provided materials.

### Replication and criticism

This is a meta-analysis, so it sits near the top of the evidence hierarchy in this lane, but it has limits the authors acknowledge. The included studies are mostly short laboratory experiments with small samples, so the results extend only cautiously to months-long curricula. Heterogeneity is large, so the averages hide a lot of variation. The category "enhanced discovery" is broad, so "it works" really means "some structured help beats lesser help." Critics from the problem-based and inquiry camp (see the Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn response to Kirschner and colleagues) argue that their methods were never unassisted in the first place, which the meta-analysis in effect concedes by splitting the field into assisted and unassisted halves. I did not read later syntheses this session and cite none.

### Implications for LTF

For a beginner flying course, the practical reading is "explore, but with rails." An unscaffolded sandbox lesson ("go fly and see what the elevator does") is the condition the meta-analysis found weakest. LTF challenge briefings should therefore give a worked demonstration or a worked example first (what the instruments should show at each step), then let the learner practice, then give specific feedback. Debriefs should include elicited explanations ("why did the nose rise when you added power?"), a condition that did well here. Feedback after a challenge is itself an effective "scaffold" here, which supports building explicit post-flight debriefs rather than a bare score. Early lessons should lean explicit and later challenges can fade guidance. For fly mode used next to the sim, keep the guidance short and procedural, since the learner is already carrying a heavy load from the sim itself.

## kirschner2006why

Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006), "Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work: an analysis of the failure of constructivist, discovery, problem-based, experiential, and inquiry-based teaching", Educational Psychologist 41(2), 75-86. Read together with the authors' 2007 reply to commentaries (Educational Psychologist 42(2), 115-121).

### Core claims

This is a position paper, not an experiment. It argues that fifty years of empirical comparisons show that minimally guided instruction (the authors group constructivist, discovery, problem-based, experiential and inquiry-based teaching under that heading) is less effective and less efficient than instruction that gives learners strong, explicit guidance. The argument rests on human cognitive architecture: working memory is severely limited when handling novel material, long-term memory is effectively unlimited, and learning means building organized knowledge (schemas) in long-term memory. Searching for a solution burdens working memory without necessarily building schemas, so novices who must discover a procedure learn less than novices who are shown it. The abstract also states a boundary condition: the advantage of guidance recedes only when learners have enough prior knowledge to supply "internal" guidance (the expertise reversal effect, from the cognitive load literature).

### Specifics

Definition of guidance. The 2007 reply restates the 2006 definition: direct instructional guidance means providing information that fully explains the concepts and procedures students must learn, together with learning-strategy support that fits human cognitive architecture. That is not the same as a lecture; the authors count worked examples, modeling and case studies as direct guidance.

The key empirical pillar is the worked-example effect: one group solves problems, another receives the same problems with solutions, and later test performance (including transfer problems) favors the group that studied solutions, because only one variable differs between groups. The reply insists on this "one variable at a time" standard and uses it to dismiss many pro-inquiry studies. Their objections: comparing a new curriculum with an older one confounds curriculum with method, and a study in which discussing a relevant problem beat discussing an irrelevant one (the osmosis experiments) does not test whether discussion beats being given the explanation.

Other specific arguments in the reply: collaboration itself costs working memory, so collaborative problem-based learning can add extraneous load; the historical movement from "pure" discovery to "guided discovery" and from unscaffolded to scaffolded inquiry is read as a retreat forced by evidence; and teaching general problem-solving or inquiry skills has no demonstrated payoff because no teachable, general strategy has been isolated, so domain-specific knowledge should be taught directly. The reply also says that the "ultimate scaffold" is to supply the whole solution and process information, either before a task or just in time during it.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence strength: a literature review and theoretical argument by three authors committed to cognitive load theory, supported by many controlled lab studies but not itself a meta-analysis. Crossref lists more than four thousand citing works. In the same journal, Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn (2007) argued that problem-based and inquiry learning are not minimally guided because they use heavy scaffolding; Schmidt, Loyens, van Gog and Paas (2007) argued that problem-based learning is compatible with cognitive architecture; Kuhn (2007) argued that teaching science should emphasize inquiry skills over content. The authors' reply grants that modern problem-based and inquiry learning are "very structured" and calls this a welcome shift, but says guided discovery with scaffolding is hard to tell apart from direct guidance. Critics' recurring point is that the paper lumps together methods that differ greatly. The Alfieri meta-analysis (also in this lane) supported the narrower claim that unassisted discovery lost to explicit instruction. The paper's own boundary matters: the guidance advantage shrinks as expertise grows.

### Implications for LTF

LTF's learners are true novices in a domain with an enormous amount of new vocabulary, so they sit on the "guidance wins" side of the boundary. Lessons should present worked examples before asking for performance: show a labeled walkthrough of a traffic-pattern leg with what the instruments read, then ask the learner to predict, then to fly it. Challenge briefings should state the procedure, not invite the learner to reinvent it. Avoid long unguided "explore the sim" prompts. Later modules should fade support (shorter briefings, checklists). Fly mode should offer just-in-time procedural cues rather than open-ended prompts, since the learner's working memory is already occupied by the sim. Treat the paper as a strong design default for novice phases, not as settling the whole debate.

Confirmed this session: Crossref records for DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1 (2006) and 10.1080/00461520701263426 (2007 reply); web search summaries of the abstract and the Hmelo-Silver response (cogtech.usc.edu record, DOI 10.1080/00461520701263368); full text of the 2007 reply read as PDF (ntnu.no wiki attachment). The full 2006 text was not opened, so statements about its content are limited to the abstract and the reply's restatements.

## shute2008focus

Shute (2008), "Focus on formative feedback", Review of Educational Research 78(1), 153-189.

### Core claims

A long narrative review of the feedback literature of the preceding half century, ending with practical guidelines. Feedback is widely believed to be helpful, but the article stresses that the evidence is inconsistent: about one third of the studies in two landmark meta-analyses (Bangert-Drowns and colleagues 1991; Kluger and DeNisi 1996) showed negative effects of feedback on learning. Shute defines formative feedback as information communicated to the learner that is intended to modify thinking or behavior to improve learning, and says researchers agree it should be nonevaluative, supportive, timely and specific. Feedback that harms learning is, by definition here, not formative.

### Specifics

Types. The review separates verification (simple right or wrong judgment) from elaboration (the informational part: explanation of why, hints, worked examples, error analysis). Table 1 of the paper arrays types from least to most complex, and its elaborated variants include topic, response-specific, hints and cues, bugs and misconceptions, and informative tutoring feedback.

Table 2, things to do: focus feedback on the task, not the learner; give elaborated feedback (the what, how and why); present it in manageable units to avoid overload; be specific and clear; keep it as simple as possible but no simpler; reduce uncertainty between performance and goals; give unbiased, objective feedback; promote a learning goal orientation; and give feedback after learners have attempted a solution rather than before. Table 3, things to avoid: normative comparisons; overall grades (summarizing Wiliam, students given grades only showed no gains, comments only showed large gains, and grades plus comments showed no gains); discouraging or self-esteem-threatening messages; praise, except sparingly; oral delivery; and interrupting a learner who is actively engaged.

Timing (Table 4). Immediate feedback fixes errors in real time and is efficient for procedural skills; delayed feedback has been associated with better transfer. For difficult tasks use immediate feedback, at least initially; for relatively simple ones, delay to avoid intrusion. A computer-based instruction meta-analysis cited in the review found a mean weighted effect of 0.80 for feedback versus none on immediate outcomes (22 studies) but 0.35 on delayed outcomes (9 studies). A motor-task experiment found that spacing feedback over more trials worsened practice performance but improved retention. The review concludes the effect of timing depends on task and learner, with low-achieving learners favored by immediate feedback and high-achieving learners by delayed.

Kluger and DeNisi's feedback intervention theory is summarized: feedback that directs attention to the task helps, whereas feedback that directs attention to the self (praise, ego threat) tends to hurt. Response certitude: learners who are sure they are right read feedback less, so give richer feedback to those who are confidently wrong (a finding not consistently replicated, as the review itself notes with a failed replication).

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: narrative review that reports results of meta-analyses and experiments but does not itself pool effects. The review concedes "many conflicting findings and no consistent pattern of results" is a fair description of the field and that several of its guidelines come from single or small studies. The review itself notes that field studies tend to favor immediate feedback while many laboratory studies favor delay, so the timing question is unsettled.

### Implications for LTF

Challenge debriefs and quiz responses are LTF's feedback surfaces. Use elaborated, task-focused wording ("the nose pitched up because power was added before trim"), not scores or praise. Show feedback after the learner commits an answer, never before. In a novice's first attempts at a difficult challenge, give immediate cues; as the learner improves, switch to a post-flight debrief. Break debriefs into short chunks rather than one wall of text. Do not rank learners against each other on dashboards; avoid a bare percentage as the only feedback, since grade-only feedback produced no gains. In fly mode do not interrupt a learner mid-maneuver with popups; queue feedback for the pause.

Confirmed this session: Crossref records for DOI 10.3102/0034654307313795 and the 2007 ETS report version; full text of the article read as PDF (myweb.fsu.edu/vshute), including abstract, Tables 1 to 4 and the timing and learner-level sections quoted above.

## keller1987development

Keller (1987), "Development and use of the ARCS model of instructional design", Journal of Instructional Development 10(3), 2-10.

### Core claims

Keller built the ARCS model to answer two questions: can the many theories of human motivation be synthesized into a simple schema a practitioner can use, and can motivating instruction be designed systematically rather than by intuition or "native talent"? His answer is a four-category model (Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction), a catalog of strategies under each, and a motivational design process that plugs into ordinary instructional design models. The paper argues that motivation is not a fixed trait of learners that designers must accept; it is something instruction can raise or kill, so a designer should treat a motivation problem as a design problem to diagnose and solve. The paper also reports two small teacher field tests, which Keller says only support usefulness.

### Specifics

Foundation. ARCS rests on expectancy-value theory: people engage in an activity if they see it as linked to meeting their needs (value) and expect to succeed (expectancy). Keller's earlier four categories were renamed to generate the acronym. The strategy catalog was built by collecting strategy statements from research, handbooks and practitioner interviews, then sorting them into the four categories; four people classified them and an intraclass correlation estimated reliability at .78.

The four conditions. Attention: get it, then sustain it, balancing boredom against overstimulation. Strategy families in Table 1 include incongruity, concreteness (visual representations, examples of every important concept), variability, humor, inquiry and participation (games, role plays, simulations). Relevance: families include experience (state how the instruction builds on existing skills), present worth, future usefulness, need matching, modeling and choice, and relevance can come from the way something is taught, not only from the content. Confidence: state learning goals and evaluation criteria, organize material by increasing difficulty so that the challenge is "conquerable", help learners set realistic goals, attribute success to effort when that is true, and let learners learn new skills under low risk then practice well-learned tasks under realistic conditions. Satisfaction: let learners use a new skill in a realistic setting as soon as possible, give informative feedback when it is immediately useful, avoid threats, and avoid external evaluation when the learner can evaluate his or her own work.

Process. Define (classify the problem, analyze audience motivation, write motivational objectives, which like instructional objectives state behavior, condition and criterion), Design (brainstorm strategies, then select using five guidelines: do not take much instructional time, do not detract from objectives, fit time and money limits, be acceptable to the audience, be compatible with the delivery system), Develop, and Evaluate using direct measures such as persistence, effort, emotion and attitude, not achievement scores alone. Keller insists motivation is a means to an end: if learners arrive motivated, do not add strategies (his example is a banquet project that consumed half a language course).

Field tests. Eighteen middle-school teachers took a four-month workshop; all found the model comprehensible and useful, and sixteen (89%) said it helped. A second workshop of sixteen teachers ran six days; of twelve respondents, ten (83%) found it comprehensible and useful and nine (75%) felt it helped, with weaker results where teachers picked problems about individual students rather than instruction.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: a design model with a conceptual synthesis and two uncontrolled attitudinal field tests. Keller himself lists the limits: the author ran both workshops, the outcomes were questionnaires, and no learning outcomes were measured. The model is described as heuristic, not algorithmic, requiring judgment. I did not read later controlled studies this session and make no claims about effect sizes. A fair criticism is that ARCS is a taxonomy of strategies and does not prove that any listed strategy works for a given audience.

### Implications for LTF

ARCS gives LTF a checklist for lesson and challenge pages. Attention: a short concrete visual or a "what would you expect to see?" prompt before a concept. Relevance: say in one line why this skill matters for the next flight, and tie it to the learner's sim experience. Confidence: state the criteria for a challenge up front, order challenges by difficulty so each is a conquerable step, and attribute improvement to practice. Satisfaction: let the learner use each new skill in the sim soon, and give informative feedback. Keller's own caution matters: do not over-decorate. Motivational devices must not slow the lesson or distract from objectives, which argues against heavy gamification in fly mode. Dashboards should help learners judge their own progress, since avoiding external evaluation is an ARCS strategy.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1007/bf02905780 (title, author, 1987, Journal of Instructional Development 10(3), 2-10); full text of the article read as PDF (ocw.tudelft.nl), including abstract, Tables 1 to 5 and the two field tests.

## wood1976role

Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), "The role of tutoring in problem solving", Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 17(2), 89-100.

### Core claims

The paper introduces "scaffolding" into the education vocabulary. Its subject is tutoring: how an expert helps a less expert person. Scaffolding, as the authors define it, is the adult controlling those elements of the task that are initially beyond the learner's capacity, so the learner can concentrate on and complete only the elements within his or her range of competence. They go further than assisted completion: they argue the process can lead to the learner developing task competence of their own. Finally they offer a six-part account of what a tutor does. The paper describes itself as an exercise in problem-finding, an observational description of a tutoring process, not a hypothesis test.

### Specifics

Sample and task. Thirty children, ten each at ages 3, 4 and 5, equally divided by sex, individually tutored in sessions of 20 minutes to an hour. They were recruited by advertisement from families living near Cambridge, Massachusetts, predominantly middle-class or lower middle-class. The task was to build a pyramid from 21 interlocking wooden blocks spread out in a jumble. A single tutor (the third author) followed a general plan: she gave a short free play period, then intervened according to the child's actions, with pre-set rules about how much help to give. Two independent scorers agreed on 94% of a pool of acts.

Findings. Unassisted success rose with age: the ratio of incorrect to correct pair-constructions moved from 9:0 to 2:8 to 1:2 across the three ages, and over 75% of the 15 construction acts needed for a pyramid were unassisted for 5-year-olds, against 50% and 10% for the 4s and 3s. None of the 3-year-olds could put four blocks together correctly. In the paper's Table 2, "showing" succeeded more often than "telling" at every age (3-year-olds 40% versus 18%, 4-year-olds 63% versus 40%, 5-year-olds 80% versus 57%). Imitation was selective: children imitated only acts they could already do fairly well. Comprehension of a solution preceded production of it.

The six functions. (1) Recruitment: enlist the learner's interest in and adherence to the task requirements. (2) Reduction in degrees of freedom: simplify the task by reducing the number of constituent acts, so the learner can recognize whether a step has fitted, while the tutor fills in the rest. (3) Direction maintenance: keep the learner pursuing the objective, partly by zest and sympathy, and by making it worthwhile to risk the next step. (4) Marking critical features: accentuate relevant features and interpret discrepancies between what the child produced and a correct production. (5) Frustration control: problem solving should be less stressful with a tutor than without, with the main risk being too much dependency on the tutor. (6) Demonstration: modeling an idealized version of the act, which may complete or explicate a solution the learner partly executed.

### Replication and criticism

The evidence is a small, descriptive, single-tutor observational study, and the authors say so. It was never a controlled test, and the sample is narrow in age, geography and background. "Scaffolding" later became a general instructional term, often fused with Vygotsky's zone of proximal development, which this paper does not itself invoke. One secondary essay (Hendrick, Substack) argues that the concept has been stretched into a mechanical rule such as "more help after failure, less after success" and that later uses overemphasize the cognitive functions over the motivational ones; I treat that as opinion, not a finding. Alfieri and colleagues (2011), another source in this lane, give meta-analytic support for scaffolded discovery.

### Implications for LTF

The six functions double as a design checklist for a lesson or challenge. Recruitment: a short, concrete reason to care. Reduction in degrees of freedom: start a challenge with most variables fixed (power set, trim set) and release them gradually. Direction maintenance: keep a visible goal on screen in fly mode. Marking critical features: highlight the instrument or cue that matters, and show the gap between the learner's value and the target. Frustration control: normal-sized failure messages and an easy retry. Demonstration: an idealized demonstration, preferably before the learner attempts a step, since "showing" beat "telling" in this study. Also note that comprehension precedes production: have learners recognize correct indications (a recognition quiz on what a stabilized approach looks like) before asking them to produce them. Fade the cues so the learner is not left dependent.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x (title, authors, 1976, volume 17, issue 2, pages 89-100); full text read as PDF (copy at sachafund.wordpress.com): sample, task, scoring agreement, Tables 1 to 3 and the six functions; Hendrick essay fetched for the critique only.

## mayer2004should

Mayer (2004), "Should there be a three-strikes rule against pure discovery learning? The case for guided methods of instruction", American Psychologist 59(1), 14-19.

### Core claims

Mayer's thesis is that there is enough research evidence to make any reasonable person skeptical of pure discovery as a preferred method of instruction, whether it is practiced under cognitive or social constructivism. He separates two things that are often fused. Constructivism as a theory of learning (learners actively build knowledge) is not at issue. What he attacks is the "constructivist teaching fallacy," his term for the inference that because learning is active, teaching must make learners behaviorally active (hands-on work, discussion, free exploration). He wants the activity to be cognitive (selecting, organizing and integrating knowledge), not merely behavioral. His closing recommendation: guided discovery, curricular focus and cognitive activity, not unstructured exploration and activity as an end in itself.

### Specifics

Method. This is a narrative historical review of three research literatures, each with a "strike." He defines pure discovery (the student gets problems with little or no guidance), guided discovery (the student gets the problem plus hints, direction, coaching, feedback or modeling) and expository methods (the student gets the problem with the answer).

Strike one, discovery of problem-solving rules (1950s and 1960s). Craig (1956) had students find a rule such as "odd initial sound"; a guided group, told what to attend to, learned more efficiently and transferred as well as the pure discovery group. Kittel (1957) compared pure discovery, guided discovery and expository groups: pure discovery did worst and guided discovery best on immediate retention, delayed retention and transfer. Gagné and Brown (1961), with series-sum problems, found that guided discovery took the most learning time but produced the best transfer. Shulman and Keislar's 1966 summary found guided discovery generally more effective than pure discovery.

Strike two, conservation strategies (1970s). Studies by Gelman, Wallach and Sprott, Beilin and Brainerd showed children learning conservation concepts through guided practice, concrete demonstrations of reversibility, or even simple right-or-wrong feedback; Mayer notes that Brainerd observed little research had directly compared self-discovery with parallel tutoring, and effects in Inhelder, Sinclair and Bovet's self-discovery studies were small by comparison.

Strike three, LOGO programming (1980s). Papert's call for hands-on discovery produced studies: Kurland and Pea's students with more than 50 hours of pure-discovery LOGO could write simple programs but held many incorrect conceptions; Pea and Kurland found hands-on LOGO exploration no better on planning tests than no programming; Fay and Mayer (1994) found a guided group, given modeling of design concepts, hints and feedback, wrote more elegant programs and planned better. Reviewers concluded that mediated instruction is a prerequisite for LOGO's transfer.

Mechanism. Active learning needs two cognitive processes: selecting relevant incoming information and integrating it with prior knowledge. Pure discovery can fail the first, because learners may never come into contact with the to-be-learned principle. Expository methods can fail the second if they discourage sense-making. Guided discovery meets both. The warning line he draws is that "constructivism = hands-on activity" is a formula for educational disaster, but not that constructivism is wrong.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: a selective narrative review by an author who is a leading voice in multimedia learning, drawing on small experiments from the 1950s to the 1980s using artificial tasks (he acknowledges the "old-fashioned horse-race design"). It is not a systematic review, and a selection effect is possible. It was followed by a meta-analysis (Alfieri and colleagues, 2011, in this lane) that reached the same broad verdict with a fully described search: unassisted discovery lost to explicit instruction, and enhanced discovery beat other instruction. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argued a related case from cognitive load theory, and Hmelo-Silver and colleagues replied that problem-based and inquiry learning are scaffolded. Mayer himself concedes guided discovery is not a single method: the hard part is knowing how much and what kind of guidance to supply, and he allows that in some cases direct instruction works while in others "some mixture of guidance and exploration" is needed. The paper's Figure 1 is a useful simple frame: it separates cognitive activity from behavioral activity.

### Implications for LTF

This source gives LTF a clean test for any interactive widget or challenge: is the activity cognitive (the learner must select, organize or integrate) or only behavioral (clicking, dragging, flying around)? A drag-the-slider widget earns its place only if the learner must predict, explain or connect what changes. In challenges, avoid the pure-discovery pattern ("fly and notice"); use guided discovery: set a goal, point attention at the relevant cue (for example, the airspeed trend), give hints, then feedback. Because guided discovery took longer yet transferred best in the Gagné and Brown study, do not judge lessons only by completion time. Do not build group-discussion features expecting learning gains; guidance still matters. In the sim, free practice is fine after guided practice, as a consolidation step.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1037/0003-066x.59.1.14; full text read as PDF (csun.edu copy), including abstract, the three strikes, the definitions and the concluding section.
