# Lane 29: Instructional design models and evidence

Working notes for the Learn to Fly Design Knowledge Base. Each section covers one source, in the order the sources were verified and read.

## alfieri2011does

Alfieri, Brooks, Aldrich and Tenenbaum (2011), "Does discovery-based instruction enhance learning?", Journal of Educational Psychology 103(1), 1-18.

### Core claims

The paper is the first systematic meta-analysis of the discovery-learning literature, written to settle a long argument between advocates of constructivist, discovery-style teaching and critics who favor explicit instruction. It runs two meta-analyses on a pool of 164 studies. The first compares unassisted discovery (learners get no guidance and no feedback) with explicit instruction. The second compares enhanced or assisted discovery (learners still construct the knowledge but get scaffolding, feedback, worked examples or prompts to explain) with other forms of instruction. The authors' conclusion, in the abstract, is that unassisted discovery does not benefit learners, whereas feedback, worked examples, scaffolding and elicited explanations do.

### Specifics

Method. Studies were found mainly through PsycINFO, ERIC and Dissertation Abstracts International, plus citation chasing. The first analysis required a direct test of an explicit-instruction condition against a condition with no guidance or feedback during the learning task. The second required a condition with guidance plus a comparison condition. Studies with only qualitative data or unusable statistics were excluded. Effects were coded as Cohen's d with random-effects models.

Headline numbers (all from the paper's own text, fetched this session). Unassisted discovery: 580 comparisons from 108 studies. The abstract reports d = -0.38 with 95% CI [-.44, -.31] at the comparison level; the study-level analysis gave d = -0.38 with CI [-.50, -.25]. Negative means explicit instruction won. Enhanced discovery: 360 comparisons from 56 studies, d = 0.30, 95% CI [.23, .36] at the comparison level and [.15, .44] at the study level. The authors call both effects small but meaningful.

Moderators for the unassisted comparison. Against unassisted discovery, worked examples did best among the explicit conditions (d = -0.63), then feedback (d = -0.46), direct teaching (-0.29) and explanations provided (-0.28). Domain mattered: the explicit advantage was small in math (-0.16), larger in science (-0.39) and problem solving (-0.48), and largest in verbal and social skills (-0.95). Age mattered: all age groups favored explicit teaching, and the gap was larger for adolescents (-0.53) than for adults (-0.26). Publication tier mattered too: first-tier journals gave a larger explicit advantage (-0.67) than lower-tier journals (-0.24), though the reported fail-safe numbers were in the thousands of unpublished studies.

Moderators for the enhanced comparison. Enhanced discovery beat comparison conditions in every domain coded as favorable, with the largest effects in physical and motor skills (1.05) and computer skills (0.64) and the smallest in science (0.11). Adults gained more than children. Unpublished studies and dissertations leaned against enhanced discovery (-0.54), another publication-pattern wrinkle.

The authors define discovery learning as any situation where the learner is not given the target information and must find it with only the provided materials.

### Replication and criticism

This is a meta-analysis, so it sits near the top of the evidence hierarchy in this lane, but it has limits the authors acknowledge. The included studies are mostly short laboratory experiments with small samples, so the results extend only cautiously to months-long curricula. Heterogeneity is large, so the averages hide a lot of variation. The category "enhanced discovery" is broad, so "it works" really means "some structured help beats lesser help." Critics from the problem-based and inquiry camp (see the Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn response to Kirschner and colleagues) argue that their methods were never unassisted in the first place, which the meta-analysis in effect concedes by splitting the field into assisted and unassisted halves. I did not read later syntheses this session and cite none.

### Implications for LTF

For a beginner flying course, the practical reading is "explore, but with rails." An unscaffolded sandbox lesson ("go fly and see what the elevator does") is the condition the meta-analysis found weakest. LTF challenge briefings should therefore give a worked demonstration or a worked example first (what the instruments should show at each step), then let the learner practice, then give specific feedback. Debriefs should include elicited explanations ("why did the nose rise when you added power?"), a condition that did well here. Feedback after a challenge is itself an effective "scaffold" here, which supports building explicit post-flight debriefs rather than a bare score. Early lessons should lean explicit and later challenges can fade guidance. For fly mode used next to the sim, keep the guidance short and procedural, since the learner is already carrying a heavy load from the sim itself.

## kirschner2006why

Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006), "Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work: an analysis of the failure of constructivist, discovery, problem-based, experiential, and inquiry-based teaching", Educational Psychologist 41(2), 75-86. Read together with the authors' 2007 reply to commentaries (Educational Psychologist 42(2), 115-121).

### Core claims

This is a position paper, not an experiment. It argues that fifty years of empirical comparisons show that minimally guided instruction (the authors group constructivist, discovery, problem-based, experiential and inquiry-based teaching under that heading) is less effective and less efficient than instruction that gives learners strong, explicit guidance. The argument rests on human cognitive architecture: working memory is severely limited when handling novel material, long-term memory is effectively unlimited, and learning means building organized knowledge (schemas) in long-term memory. Searching for a solution burdens working memory without necessarily building schemas, so novices who must discover a procedure learn less than novices who are shown it. The abstract also states a boundary condition: the advantage of guidance recedes only when learners have enough prior knowledge to supply "internal" guidance (the expertise reversal effect, from the cognitive load literature).

### Specifics

Definition of guidance. The 2007 reply restates the 2006 definition: direct instructional guidance means providing information that fully explains the concepts and procedures students must learn, together with learning-strategy support that fits human cognitive architecture. That is not the same as a lecture; the authors count worked examples, modeling and case studies as direct guidance.

The key empirical pillar is the worked-example effect: one group solves problems, another receives the same problems with solutions, and later test performance (including transfer problems) favors the group that studied solutions, because only one variable differs between groups. The reply insists on this "one variable at a time" standard and uses it to dismiss many pro-inquiry studies. Their objections: comparing a new curriculum with an older one confounds curriculum with method, and a study in which discussing a relevant problem beat discussing an irrelevant one (the osmosis experiments) does not test whether discussion beats being given the explanation.

Other specific arguments in the reply: collaboration itself costs working memory, so collaborative problem-based learning can add extraneous load; the historical movement from "pure" discovery to "guided discovery" and from unscaffolded to scaffolded inquiry is read as a retreat forced by evidence; and teaching general problem-solving or inquiry skills has no demonstrated payoff because no teachable, general strategy has been isolated, so domain-specific knowledge should be taught directly. The reply also says that the "ultimate scaffold" is to supply the whole solution and process information, either before a task or just in time during it.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence strength: a literature review and theoretical argument by three authors committed to cognitive load theory, supported by many controlled lab studies but not itself a meta-analysis. Crossref lists more than four thousand citing works. In the same journal, Hmelo-Silver, Duncan and Chinn (2007) argued that problem-based and inquiry learning are not minimally guided because they use heavy scaffolding; Schmidt, Loyens, van Gog and Paas (2007) argued that problem-based learning is compatible with cognitive architecture; Kuhn (2007) argued that teaching science should emphasize inquiry skills over content. The authors' reply grants that modern problem-based and inquiry learning are "very structured" and calls this a welcome shift, but says guided discovery with scaffolding is hard to tell apart from direct guidance. Critics' recurring point is that the paper lumps together methods that differ greatly. The Alfieri meta-analysis (also in this lane) supported the narrower claim that unassisted discovery lost to explicit instruction. The paper's own boundary matters: the guidance advantage shrinks as expertise grows.

### Implications for LTF

LTF's learners are true novices in a domain with an enormous amount of new vocabulary, so they sit on the "guidance wins" side of the boundary. Lessons should present worked examples before asking for performance: show a labeled walkthrough of a traffic-pattern leg with what the instruments read, then ask the learner to predict, then to fly it. Challenge briefings should state the procedure, not invite the learner to reinvent it. Avoid long unguided "explore the sim" prompts. Later modules should fade support (shorter briefings, checklists). Fly mode should offer just-in-time procedural cues rather than open-ended prompts, since the learner's working memory is already occupied by the sim. Treat the paper as a strong design default for novice phases, not as settling the whole debate.

Confirmed this session: Crossref records for DOI 10.1207/s15326985ep4102_1 (2006) and 10.1080/00461520701263426 (2007 reply); web search summaries of the abstract and the Hmelo-Silver response (cogtech.usc.edu record, DOI 10.1080/00461520701263368); full text of the 2007 reply read as PDF (ntnu.no wiki attachment). The full 2006 text was not opened, so statements about its content are limited to the abstract and the reply's restatements.

## shute2008focus

Shute (2008), "Focus on formative feedback", Review of Educational Research 78(1), 153-189.

### Core claims

A long narrative review of the feedback literature of the preceding half century, ending with practical guidelines. Feedback is widely believed to be helpful, but the article stresses that the evidence is inconsistent: about one third of the studies in two landmark meta-analyses (Bangert-Drowns and colleagues 1991; Kluger and DeNisi 1996) showed negative effects of feedback on learning. Shute defines formative feedback as information communicated to the learner that is intended to modify thinking or behavior to improve learning, and says researchers agree it should be nonevaluative, supportive, timely and specific. Feedback that harms learning is, by definition here, not formative.

### Specifics

Types. The review separates verification (simple right or wrong judgment) from elaboration (the informational part: explanation of why, hints, worked examples, error analysis). Table 1 of the paper arrays types from least to most complex, and its elaborated variants include topic, response-specific, hints and cues, bugs and misconceptions, and informative tutoring feedback.

Table 2, things to do: focus feedback on the task, not the learner; give elaborated feedback (the what, how and why); present it in manageable units to avoid overload; be specific and clear; keep it as simple as possible but no simpler; reduce uncertainty between performance and goals; give unbiased, objective feedback; promote a learning goal orientation; and give feedback after learners have attempted a solution rather than before. Table 3, things to avoid: normative comparisons; overall grades (summarizing Wiliam, students given grades only showed no gains, comments only showed large gains, and grades plus comments showed no gains); discouraging or self-esteem-threatening messages; praise, except sparingly; oral delivery; and interrupting a learner who is actively engaged.

Timing (Table 4). Immediate feedback fixes errors in real time and is efficient for procedural skills; delayed feedback has been associated with better transfer. For difficult tasks use immediate feedback, at least initially; for relatively simple ones, delay to avoid intrusion. A computer-based instruction meta-analysis cited in the review found a mean weighted effect of 0.80 for feedback versus none on immediate outcomes (22 studies) but 0.35 on delayed outcomes (9 studies). A motor-task experiment found that spacing feedback over more trials worsened practice performance but improved retention. The review concludes the effect of timing depends on task and learner, with low-achieving learners favored by immediate feedback and high-achieving learners by delayed.

Kluger and DeNisi's feedback intervention theory is summarized: feedback that directs attention to the task helps, whereas feedback that directs attention to the self (praise, ego threat) tends to hurt. Response certitude: learners who are sure they are right read feedback less, so give richer feedback to those who are confidently wrong (a finding not consistently replicated, as the review itself notes with a failed replication).

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: narrative review that reports results of meta-analyses and experiments but does not itself pool effects. The review concedes "many conflicting findings and no consistent pattern of results" is a fair description of the field and that several of its guidelines come from single or small studies. The review itself notes that field studies tend to favor immediate feedback while many laboratory studies favor delay, so the timing question is unsettled.

### Implications for LTF

Challenge debriefs and quiz responses are LTF's feedback surfaces. Use elaborated, task-focused wording ("the nose pitched up because power was added before trim"), not scores or praise. Show feedback after the learner commits an answer, never before. In a novice's first attempts at a difficult challenge, give immediate cues; as the learner improves, switch to a post-flight debrief. Break debriefs into short chunks rather than one wall of text. Do not rank learners against each other on dashboards; avoid a bare percentage as the only feedback, since grade-only feedback produced no gains. In fly mode do not interrupt a learner mid-maneuver with popups; queue feedback for the pause.

Confirmed this session: Crossref records for DOI 10.3102/0034654307313795 and the 2007 ETS report version; full text of the article read as PDF (myweb.fsu.edu/vshute), including abstract, Tables 1 to 4 and the timing and learner-level sections quoted above.

## keller1987development

Keller (1987), "Development and use of the ARCS model of instructional design", Journal of Instructional Development 10(3), 2-10.

### Core claims

Keller built the ARCS model to answer two questions: can the many theories of human motivation be synthesized into a simple schema a practitioner can use, and can motivating instruction be designed systematically rather than by intuition or "native talent"? His answer is a four-category model (Attention, Relevance, Confidence, Satisfaction), a catalog of strategies under each, and a motivational design process that plugs into ordinary instructional design models. The paper argues that motivation is not a fixed trait of learners that designers must accept; it is something instruction can raise or kill, so a designer should treat a motivation problem as a design problem to diagnose and solve. The paper also reports two small teacher field tests, which Keller says only support usefulness.

### Specifics

Foundation. ARCS rests on expectancy-value theory: people engage in an activity if they see it as linked to meeting their needs (value) and expect to succeed (expectancy). Keller's earlier four categories were renamed to generate the acronym. The strategy catalog was built by collecting strategy statements from research, handbooks and practitioner interviews, then sorting them into the four categories; four people classified them and an intraclass correlation estimated reliability at .78.

The four conditions. Attention: get it, then sustain it, balancing boredom against overstimulation. Strategy families in Table 1 include incongruity, concreteness (visual representations, examples of every important concept), variability, humor, inquiry and participation (games, role plays, simulations). Relevance: families include experience (state how the instruction builds on existing skills), present worth, future usefulness, need matching, modeling and choice, and relevance can come from the way something is taught, not only from the content. Confidence: state learning goals and evaluation criteria, organize material by increasing difficulty so that the challenge is "conquerable", help learners set realistic goals, attribute success to effort when that is true, and let learners learn new skills under low risk then practice well-learned tasks under realistic conditions. Satisfaction: let learners use a new skill in a realistic setting as soon as possible, give informative feedback when it is immediately useful, avoid threats, and avoid external evaluation when the learner can evaluate his or her own work.

Process. Define (classify the problem, analyze audience motivation, write motivational objectives, which like instructional objectives state behavior, condition and criterion), Design (brainstorm strategies, then select using five guidelines: do not take much instructional time, do not detract from objectives, fit time and money limits, be acceptable to the audience, be compatible with the delivery system), Develop, and Evaluate using direct measures such as persistence, effort, emotion and attitude, not achievement scores alone. Keller insists motivation is a means to an end: if learners arrive motivated, do not add strategies (his example is a banquet project that consumed half a language course).

Field tests. Eighteen middle-school teachers took a four-month workshop; all found the model comprehensible and useful, and sixteen (89%) said it helped. A second workshop of sixteen teachers ran six days; of twelve respondents, ten (83%) found it comprehensible and useful and nine (75%) felt it helped, with weaker results where teachers picked problems about individual students rather than instruction.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: a design model with a conceptual synthesis and two uncontrolled attitudinal field tests. Keller himself lists the limits: the author ran both workshops, the outcomes were questionnaires, and no learning outcomes were measured. The model is described as heuristic, not algorithmic, requiring judgment. I did not read later controlled studies this session and make no claims about effect sizes. A fair criticism is that ARCS is a taxonomy of strategies and does not prove that any listed strategy works for a given audience.

### Implications for LTF

ARCS gives LTF a checklist for lesson and challenge pages. Attention: a short concrete visual or a "what would you expect to see?" prompt before a concept. Relevance: say in one line why this skill matters for the next flight, and tie it to the learner's sim experience. Confidence: state the criteria for a challenge up front, order challenges by difficulty so each is a conquerable step, and attribute improvement to practice. Satisfaction: let the learner use each new skill in the sim soon, and give informative feedback. Keller's own caution matters: do not over-decorate. Motivational devices must not slow the lesson or distract from objectives, which argues against heavy gamification in fly mode. Dashboards should help learners judge their own progress, since avoiding external evaluation is an ARCS strategy.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1007/bf02905780 (title, author, 1987, Journal of Instructional Development 10(3), 2-10); full text of the article read as PDF (ocw.tudelft.nl), including abstract, Tables 1 to 5 and the two field tests.

## wood1976role

Wood, Bruner and Ross (1976), "The role of tutoring in problem solving", Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry 17(2), 89-100.

### Core claims

The paper introduces "scaffolding" into the education vocabulary. Its subject is tutoring: how an expert helps a less expert person. Scaffolding, as the authors define it, is the adult controlling those elements of the task that are initially beyond the learner's capacity, so the learner can concentrate on and complete only the elements within his or her range of competence. They go further than assisted completion: they argue the process can lead to the learner developing task competence of their own. Finally they offer a six-part account of what a tutor does. The paper describes itself as an exercise in problem-finding, an observational description of a tutoring process, not a hypothesis test.

### Specifics

Sample and task. Thirty children, ten each at ages 3, 4 and 5, equally divided by sex, individually tutored in sessions of 20 minutes to an hour. They were recruited by advertisement from families living near Cambridge, Massachusetts, predominantly middle-class or lower middle-class. The task was to build a pyramid from 21 interlocking wooden blocks spread out in a jumble. A single tutor (the third author) followed a general plan: she gave a short free play period, then intervened according to the child's actions, with pre-set rules about how much help to give. Two independent scorers agreed on 94% of a pool of acts.

Findings. Unassisted success rose with age: the ratio of incorrect to correct pair-constructions moved from 9:0 to 2:8 to 1:2 across the three ages, and over 75% of the 15 construction acts needed for a pyramid were unassisted for 5-year-olds, against 50% and 10% for the 4s and 3s. None of the 3-year-olds could put four blocks together correctly. In the paper's Table 2, "showing" succeeded more often than "telling" at every age (3-year-olds 40% versus 18%, 4-year-olds 63% versus 40%, 5-year-olds 80% versus 57%). Imitation was selective: children imitated only acts they could already do fairly well. Comprehension of a solution preceded production of it.

The six functions. (1) Recruitment: enlist the learner's interest in and adherence to the task requirements. (2) Reduction in degrees of freedom: simplify the task by reducing the number of constituent acts, so the learner can recognize whether a step has fitted, while the tutor fills in the rest. (3) Direction maintenance: keep the learner pursuing the objective, partly by zest and sympathy, and by making it worthwhile to risk the next step. (4) Marking critical features: accentuate relevant features and interpret discrepancies between what the child produced and a correct production. (5) Frustration control: problem solving should be less stressful with a tutor than without, with the main risk being too much dependency on the tutor. (6) Demonstration: modeling an idealized version of the act, which may complete or explicate a solution the learner partly executed.

### Replication and criticism

The evidence is a small, descriptive, single-tutor observational study, and the authors say so. It was never a controlled test, and the sample is narrow in age, geography and background. "Scaffolding" later became a general instructional term, often fused with Vygotsky's zone of proximal development, which this paper does not itself invoke. One secondary essay (Hendrick, Substack) argues that the concept has been stretched into a mechanical rule such as "more help after failure, less after success" and that later uses overemphasize the cognitive functions over the motivational ones; I treat that as opinion, not a finding. Alfieri and colleagues (2011), another source in this lane, give meta-analytic support for scaffolded discovery.

### Implications for LTF

The six functions double as a design checklist for a lesson or challenge. Recruitment: a short, concrete reason to care. Reduction in degrees of freedom: start a challenge with most variables fixed (power set, trim set) and release them gradually. Direction maintenance: keep a visible goal on screen in fly mode. Marking critical features: highlight the instrument or cue that matters, and show the gap between the learner's value and the target. Frustration control: normal-sized failure messages and an easy retry. Demonstration: an idealized demonstration, preferably before the learner attempts a step, since "showing" beat "telling" in this study. Also note that comprehension precedes production: have learners recognize correct indications (a recognition quiz on what a stabilized approach looks like) before asking them to produce them. Fade the cues so the learner is not left dependent.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1111/j.1469-7610.1976.tb00381.x (title, authors, 1976, volume 17, issue 2, pages 89-100); full text read as PDF (copy at sachafund.wordpress.com): sample, task, scoring agreement, Tables 1 to 3 and the six functions; Hendrick essay fetched for the critique only.

## mayer2004should

Mayer (2004), "Should there be a three-strikes rule against pure discovery learning? The case for guided methods of instruction", American Psychologist 59(1), 14-19.

### Core claims

Mayer's thesis is that there is enough research evidence to make any reasonable person skeptical of pure discovery as a preferred method of instruction, whether it is practiced under cognitive or social constructivism. He separates two things that are often fused. Constructivism as a theory of learning (learners actively build knowledge) is not at issue. What he attacks is the "constructivist teaching fallacy," his term for the inference that because learning is active, teaching must make learners behaviorally active (hands-on work, discussion, free exploration). He wants the activity to be cognitive (selecting, organizing and integrating knowledge), not merely behavioral. His closing recommendation: guided discovery, curricular focus and cognitive activity, not unstructured exploration and activity as an end in itself.

### Specifics

Method. This is a narrative historical review of three research literatures, each with a "strike." He defines pure discovery (the student gets problems with little or no guidance), guided discovery (the student gets the problem plus hints, direction, coaching, feedback or modeling) and expository methods (the student gets the problem with the answer).

Strike one, discovery of problem-solving rules (1950s and 1960s). Craig (1956) had students find a rule such as "odd initial sound"; a guided group, told what to attend to, learned more efficiently and transferred as well as the pure discovery group. Kittel (1957) compared pure discovery, guided discovery and expository groups: pure discovery did worst and guided discovery best on immediate retention, delayed retention and transfer. Gagné and Brown (1961), with series-sum problems, found that guided discovery took the most learning time but produced the best transfer. Shulman and Keisler's 1966 summary (spelling as in Mayer's text) found guided discovery generally more effective than pure discovery.

Strike two, conservation strategies (1970s). Studies by Gelman, Wallach and Sprott, Beilin and Brainerd showed children learning conservation concepts through guided practice, concrete demonstrations of reversibility, or even simple right-or-wrong feedback; Mayer notes that effects in Inhelder, Sinclair and Bovet's self-discovery studies were small by comparison.

Strike three, LOGO programming (1980s). Papert's call for hands-on discovery produced studies: Kurland and Pea's students with more than 50 hours of pure-discovery LOGO could write simple programs but held many incorrect conceptions; Pea and Kurland found hands-on LOGO exploration no better on planning tests than no programming; Fay and Mayer (1994) found a guided group, given modeling of design concepts, hints and feedback, wrote more elegant programs and planned better.

Mechanism. Active learning needs two cognitive processes: selecting relevant incoming information and integrating it with prior knowledge. Pure discovery can fail the first, because learners may never come into contact with the to-be-learned principle. Expository methods can fail the second if they discourage sense-making. Guided discovery meets both. The warning line he draws is that "constructivism = hands-on activity" is a formula for educational disaster, but not that constructivism is wrong.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: a selective narrative review by an author who is a leading voice in multimedia learning, drawing on small experiments from the 1950s to the 1980s using artificial tasks (he acknowledges the "old-fashioned horse-race design"). It is not a systematic review, and a selection effect is possible. It was followed by a meta-analysis (Alfieri and colleagues, 2011, in this lane) that reached the same broad verdict with a fully described search: unassisted discovery lost to explicit instruction, and enhanced discovery beat other instruction. Kirschner, Sweller and Clark (2006) argued a related case from cognitive load theory, and Hmelo-Silver and colleagues replied that problem-based and inquiry learning are scaffolded. Mayer himself concedes that the hard part of guided discovery is knowing how much and what kind of guidance to supply.

### Implications for LTF

This source gives LTF a clean test for any interactive widget or challenge: is the activity cognitive (the learner must select, organize or integrate) or only behavioral (clicking, dragging, flying around)? A drag-the-slider widget earns its place only if the learner must predict, explain or connect what changes. In challenges, avoid the pure-discovery pattern ("fly and notice"); use guided discovery: set a goal, point attention at the relevant cue (for example, the airspeed trend), give hints, then feedback. Because guided discovery took longer yet transferred best in the Gagné and Brown study, do not judge lessons only by completion time. Do not build group-discussion features expecting learning gains; guidance still matters. In the sim, free practice is fine after guided practice, as a consolidation step.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1037/0003-066x.59.1.14; full text read as PDF (csun.edu copy), including abstract, the three strikes, the definitions and the concluding section.

## merrill2002first

Merrill (2002), "First principles of instruction", Educational Technology Research and Development 50(3), 43-59. Supplemented by Merrill (2002), "A pebble-in-the-pond model for instructional design", Performance Improvement 41(7), 39-44.

### Core claims

Merrill reviewed instructional design theories to find prescriptive principles on which they agree despite their different vocabularies and philosophies. The ETR&D paper is a preliminary report of that search. Its claim is that five principles recur: learning is promoted when learners (a) engage in solving real-world problems, (b) have existing knowledge activated as a foundation for new knowledge, (c) see new knowledge demonstrated, (d) apply new knowledge, and (e) integrate new knowledge into their own world. He shows how seven representative models, among them STAR Legacy (Vanderbilt), 4-MAT, Jonassen's constructivist learning environments and Schank's learning by doing, include them. The conclusion is that they use a wide variety of terms but include fundamentally similar principles.

### Specifics

The abstract above is from the Springer page for the paper. The companion article in Performance Improvement, which I read in full, states the definitions more fully. A principle is a relationship that is always true under appropriate conditions. A first principle of instruction is a prescriptive design principle on which various instructional design theories are in essential agreement. Three properties are listed: learning is promoted in direct proportion to a program's implementation of first principles; they work in any delivery system; and they are prescriptive, not descriptions of how learners learn.

The principles are grouped into a four-phase cycle centered on a problem: activation of prior experience, demonstration of skills, application of skills and integration into real-world activities. The companion article turns each principle into check questions. Problem-centered: is the content presented in the context of real-world problems, are learners engaged at the task as well as the operation level, is there a progression of problems? Demonstration: does it show rather than merely tell, and is learner guidance employed? Application: do learners get a varied sequence of problems with feedback and "gradually diminished coaching"? Integration: are there techniques to transfer knowledge to everyday life, and chances to publicly demonstrate, reflect and create new uses?

Pebble-in-the-Pond is Merrill's design process for implementing them, described by Merrill as a version of 4C/ID (see the van Merriënboer source). Ripples: specify a whole task (the pebble), set a progression of such tasks of increasing complexity, identify the component knowledge and skills each needs, choose the instructional strategy, and design the interface; then produce materials. A strategy combines four interaction modes with the knowledge components: tell, ask, show and do. Its worked example is an Excel course that presents a whole problem, teaches needed components, shows the problem worked, then asks learners to complete later problems with less and less guidance, until final authentic tasks serve as assessment. Merrill criticizes early-specified abstract objectives, saying the content (whole tasks) should be specified first.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: expert synthesis of theories, not an experiment. The principles come with the claim that agreement across theories signals what matters; that is an argument from consensus, not a test, and the theories examined share many authors and intellectual roots. The companion article says that these principles, when implemented, promote more effective and efficient instruction than popular forms that fail to implement them, citing a study (Boyle and Merrill) that I did not read. A later book by Merrill (2013) elaborates the framework; I did not read it and make no claims about it. Notably, the Sweller, Kirschner and Clark reply (read in this lane) cites Merrill (2002) as an example of recent models that stress guidance, which is a point of agreement between camps: demonstration and diminishing coaching, not open exploration.

### Implications for LTF

Merrill gives LTF a lesson skeleton and a review checklist. Each lesson starts from a whole, realistic task (fly a standard circuit, handle a go-around), not from a topic. Open with activation (what do you already notice from the sim or from driving?), then demonstration before application: show what the correct instrument indications look like, then let the learner try with feedback, then fade coaching across successive challenges. The integration step maps well onto in-sim challenges that ask learners to apply the skill in a new scenario (different runway or wind) and explain what they did. The "progression of whole tasks, with later tasks as the assessment" idea suggests the final challenge in a module be the real assessment, with no step-by-step guidance.

Confirmed this session: Crossref record DOI 10.1007/bf02505024; Springer page for the ETR&D article (abstract with the five principles and seven models, full text paywalled and not read); full text of the Performance Improvement companion read as PDF (itgs.ict.usc.edu); the 2007 reply (Educational Psychologist) for the mention of Merrill (2002).

## bloom1984sigma

Bloom (1984), "The 2 sigma problem: the search for methods of group instruction as effective as one-to-one tutoring", Educational Researcher 13(6), 4-16. Read with von Hippel (2024), "Two-sigma tutoring: separating science fiction from science fact", Education Next, as the critical account.

### Core claims

Bloom reports that students taught one-to-one by a tutor, with formative tests and corrective work, scored about two standard deviations ("2 sigma") above students in a conventional class of about 30. Students in a mastery-learning class of about 30, using the same teacher, formative tests and corrective procedures, scored about one sigma above. He argues that most students have the potential to reach this high level of learning, that one-to-one tutoring is too costly for most societies to provide at scale, and that the "2 sigma problem" is to find group methods, practical for average teachers, that reach tutoring-level achievement. His proposed route is to combine mastery learning (about one sigma) with other alterable variables, such as improved prerequisites or teaching processes, and he reports one combination at about 1.6 sigma.

### Specifics

Design. The essay opens with two University of Chicago doctoral dissertations (Anania, 1982, 1983; Burke, 1984). Students were randomly assigned to conventional, mastery or tutoring conditions, with similar aptitude, prior achievement and attitudes. The teaching time was the same except for corrective work. Replications used four samples at grades four, five and eight and two subjects, probability and cartography, each limited to 11 periods over a three-week block. Using the control class standard deviation, the average tutored student was about two standard deviations above the control mean (above 98% of control students) and the average mastery student about one (above 84%). About 90% of tutored students and 70% of mastery students reached the level attained by only the top 20% of conventional students. Time on task was 65% (conventional), 75% (mastery) and over 90% (tutoring). Correlations between aptitude and final achievement fell from about +.60 under conventional teaching to +.25 under tutoring; Bloom flags that restricted range partly explains the low values.

Table I, adapted from Walberg (1984), lists effect sizes for "alterable variables": tutorial instruction 2.00; reinforcement; feedback-corrective (mastery learning) 1.00; cues and explanations; student classroom participation; time on task; cooperative learning .80; graded homework; homework; higher-order questions and others lower; socioeconomic status shown for contrast. Bloom writes that no combination of mastery learning with a second variable had exceeded 2 sigma.

### Replication and criticism

Strong criticism. Von Hippel (2024) points out that the 2 sigma figure rests essentially on the two dissertations; that Bloom's hand-drawn Figure 1 was illustrative and not fitted to data; and that Bloom's table differs from the Walberg source he credits, where tutoring was estimated at 0.40 and not 2.00 (Bloom repeated 0.40 but labeled it "peer and cross-age remedial tutoring"). Earlier and later meta-analyses of tutoring find far smaller effects: Cohen, Kulik and Kulik (1982) found about 0.33 standard deviations across 65 studies, with only one reporting two sigma; Nickow, Oreopoulos and Quan (2020) found an average of 0.37 across randomized studies, with none of 96 producing two sigma. Kraft (2020) is cited as saying the claim anchored unrealistic expectations of effect sizes. Von Hippel's own reading is that the experiment was not just tutoring (it was a package of mastery-style instruction and corrective procedures) and that it has not been replicated. Evidence strength for the 2 sigma figure is therefore weak: two dissertations, short interventions, tests aligned to the taught material. The qualitative message, that frequent formative testing with correction helps, is better supported (see the Kulik source).

### Implications for LTF

Never promise "tutor-level" gains or cite 2 sigma in LTF marketing or lesson copy. Treat it as a motivational ideal, and use the realistic range (about 0.3 to 0.4 for tutoring, about 0.5 for mastery programs in the Kulik meta-analysis) when planning expectations. The design lessons that survive scrutiny are practical: LTF can replicate the feedback-corrective loop (check understanding, diagnose, reteach, parallel retest) in software, where a human tutor cannot scale. Challenge debriefs should work as the "corrective" step and a parallel challenge as the retest. Aptitude-achievement correlations shrinking under mastery are an argument for letting learners repeat until they meet the standard instead of fixed-length lessons. Because sim time is the scarce resource, a short targeted correction after each flight attempt is likely to pay off more than extra content.

## kulik1990effectiveness

Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns (1990), "Effectiveness of mastery learning programs: a meta-analysis", Review of Educational Research 60(2), 265-299.

### Core claims

A meta-analysis of 108 controlled evaluations of mastery learning programs in colleges, high schools and the upper elementary grades finds positive effects on examination performance. The abstract adds that the effects seem stronger for weaker students in a class, vary with the mastery procedures used, the experimental design and the course content, that mastery programs improve attitudes toward course content and instruction, may increase time on instructional tasks, and that self-paced mastery programs often reduce completion rates in college classes. The review covers two families: Bloom's Learning for Mastery (teacher-paced, group-based, formative tests with corrective instruction) and Keller's Personalized System of Instruction (PSI; self-paced, written materials, unit quizzes with restudy until mastery).

### Specifics

Inclusion rules required a comparison with a conventionally taught group, with a mastery criterion of at least 70% correct on quizzes, and excluded studies with unequal exposure to the criterion test. Of the 108 studies, 72 were PSI and 36 were Bloom's approach. Positive effects appeared in 96 studies, and 67 of those reported statistically significant gains, while none of the studies with negative results reported significant differences. The average effect size across 103 studies was 0.52 (standard error 0.033), meaning the average mastery student performed at about the 70th percentile of a non-mastery class. Eleven studies with follow-up examinations averaged 0.71 about eight weeks after instruction. In 13 studies reporting aptitude levels, the average effect was higher for less able students (0.61) than for more able ones (0.40), but the difference was not significant.

Moderators. Group-based programs produced larger effects than self-paced ones; programs with high mastery standards beat lower standards; and locally developed tests showed larger effects than standardized ones. In PSI studies the average effect on standardized tests was 0.33 versus 0.52 on local tests; the few Bloom-style studies using standardized tests showed no significant effect. Effects were larger when control groups got less quiz feedback than the mastery group, and smaller when the comparison group had the same quizzes and feedback. The social sciences gained more than math and natural science. Time on task rose, and self-paced college courses had lower completion rates.

### Replication and criticism

A meta-analysis, and a sizable one, but heterogeneous: study quality varied, many PSI studies were nonrandom, and the test-alignment issue is serious. The authors themselves note that the large local-test effects are not offset by demonstrably poorer standardized-test performance, but also that the Bloom-style studies showed little or no effect on standardized tests, so the benefit may reflect closer alignment with taught material. Roughly a quarter of effect-size variance was explained by the five study features in their regression. This review finds effects of about half a standard deviation, a long way below the 1 sigma Bloom claimed for mastery learning in 1984 and the 2 sigma for tutoring.

### Implications for LTF

This is the most defensible evidence base for LTF's progression design. Gate progress on a demonstrated criterion and set it high: the review found high mastery standards worked better. Give learners unlimited retries with a correction between attempts. Because self-paced mastery lowered completion in college courses, LTF should add structure (suggested next steps, visible pacing, reminders) so that self-paced does not become stalled. Expect smaller gains on transfer to unfamiliar situations than on in-course tests, so in-sim challenges should include novel scenarios. Dashboard progress should present mastery per skill, not a single percent complete.

## hattie2009visible

Hattie (2009), "Visible learning: a synthesis of over 800 meta-analyses relating to achievement", Routledge. Read with the criticisms of Bergeron (2017, McGill Journal of Education 52(1), 237-246) and Wecker, Vogel and Hetmanek (Zeitschrift fur Erziehungswissenschaft 20(1), 21-40).

### Core claims

Hattie set out to build a single comparable ranking of what influences achievement in school-aged students. The publisher describes the book as the result of 15 years of research that synthesizes more than 800 meta-analyses and covers the influence of students, the home, the school, curricula, teachers and teaching strategies. Its central argument, as the publisher puts it, is that what works best for students also works best for teachers: setting challenging learning intentions, being clear about what success looks like, and using strategies that build conceptual understanding. The book presents a model of "visible teaching and visible learning" and a benchmark effect size for comparing interventions. Wikipedia's account says the book draws on 815 meta-analyses and uses Cohen's d.

### Specifics

Method. Effect sizes from each included meta-analysis are expressed as Cohen's d and averaged within a named "influence" (for example, feedback or class size), producing a league table. A benchmark of d = 0.40, the "hinge point," is used to divide the zone of desired effects from smaller ones. In the first edition, the secondary sources I fetched list values such as feedback 0.73, formative evaluation of programs 0.90, self-reported grades 1.44 and Piagetian programs 1.28. I could not confirm a count of influences, study totals or the 0.40 definition from a primary page this session, so treat those details as the way critics describe the method, not as confirmed facts from the book.

Hattie answers critics in a 2023 chapter, "Meta-analysis and criticisms of Visible Learning"; I confirmed it exists but did not read it.

### Replication and criticism

This is a synthesis of meta-analyses, a "meta-meta-analysis," and it is the most criticized source in this lane. The criticisms are specific.

Calculation errors. Hattie acknowledged that the common language effect size (CLE) values in the book were calculated incorrectly throughout, while the d values were correct (secondary account; Topphol, 2011 and Schulmeister and Loviscach, 2014 are cited as raising problems with study selection and statistics). Bergeron's critique (a statistician writing in the McGill Journal of Education) says some CLE values come out negative or above 100%.

Conceptual problems. Bergeron argues that Hattie averages unrelated studies, treats correlations as effect sizes, converts correlations to d without checking the conditions, and uses unweighted averages. A correlation of about .196 converts to d above 0.4 yet explains under 4% of variance. The sign of an effect depends on an arbitrary choice of baseline, and effects on different outcomes, populations and durations are not directly comparable. In a simulated example Bergeron shows three effect-size formulas ranking the same three teaching methods differently. The 0.40 cutoff, he says, has no principled justification. Wecker, Vogel and Hetmanek (2016/2017) tested Hattie's data base and procedure against the methodological demands of meta-analysis; their verdict, in the abstract, is that the goal of integrating educational research broadly is visionary but cannot currently be carried out in a scientifically sound way.

A further problem is use: a rank list invites teachers to drop low-ranked practices and adopt high-ranked ones without regard for context.

Evidence strength: low for any single ranking position.

### Implications for LTF

Do not use Hattie's league table or the 0.40 line as an authority for LTF design decisions, and never quote a Hattie effect size as a fact in a design rationale. Prefer primary meta-analyses and experiments, as with Alfieri, Kulik and Shute in this lane. Use Hattie as a prompt, not a proof: he is one of many sources saying that clear learning intentions, feedback and visible success criteria matter, which the better-evidenced sources also support. If an LTF decision cites Hattie, it should also cite a primary study that makes the same point. Self-reported grades ranked near the top of Hattie's list, but Bergeron notes that is a correlation between reported and actual grades, not a gain from self-assessment; LTF can still compare self-rated confidence with measured performance.

## rosenshine2012principles

Rosenshine (2012), "Principles of instruction: research-based strategies that all teachers should know", American Educator 36(1), 12-19, 39. Adapted by the author from his 2010 report for the International Academy of Education.

### Core claims

Rosenshine distills ten instructional principles from three bodies of work that, he says, supplement and complement each other: (A) cognitive science on how working memory limits learning, (B) classroom studies of "master teachers" (those whose classes made the highest achievement-test gains), and (C) research on cognitive supports such as thinking aloud, scaffolds and models for complex tasks. The unifying idea is that novices need strong, readily accessible background knowledge, which comes from teaching in manageable amounts, modeling, guided practice, error correction and review. The most effective teachers also used hands-on activities, but always after, not before, the basic material was learned.

### Specifics

The ten principles as the article lists them: (1) begin a lesson with a short review of previous learning; (2) present new material in small steps with student practice after each step; (3) ask a large number of questions and check the responses of all students; (4) provide models; (5) guide student practice; (6) check for student understanding; (7) obtain a high success rate; (8) provide scaffolds for difficult tasks; (9) require and monitor independent practice; (10) engage students in weekly and monthly review.

Concrete findings reported in the text. Review: effective teachers spent five to eight minutes at the start on review; in one elementary math experiment, teachers were taught to spend eight minutes a day on review and their students scored higher than those in other classrooms. Questions: the most successful teachers spent more than half of class time lecturing, demonstrating and asking questions, and asked more process questions ("explain how you found the answer"); a classroom experiment that trained teachers to ask more factual and process questions in guided practice raised scores. Models: worked examples, studied alternately with problems to solve, reduce cognitive load. Success rate: in a fourth-grade math study, 82% of answers were correct in the most successful teachers' classes versus 73% in the least successful ones, and the article says the optimal success rate "appears to be about 80 percent," showing that students are learning and still challenged. Scaffolds include modeling and thinking aloud, prompts, and tools such as cue cards or checklists, withdrawn gradually as competence grows. Independent practice should use the same material as guided practice, and long explanations at students' desks signaled that guided practice had been insufficient. Review should include overlearning, practice beyond initial mastery to reach automaticity.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: a practitioner-oriented synthesis by an eminent researcher. The master-teacher strand is correlational process-product research from the 1970s and 1980s (classrooms observed, behaviors coded, achievement gains compared), mostly in basic skills such as math and reading, so it shows what effective teachers do but cannot by itself prove cause. Some principles do have experimental support within the article (the review and questioning training studies). The cognitive-science strand rests on limited working memory, the same architecture Kirschner, Sweller and Clark use. The "80 percent" figure is described by Rosenshine as what research "suggests"; it is an observational optimum for classroom answers, not a universal rule. The article cites Miller's 1956 "seven plus or minus two" paper as suggested reading for working memory limits, a claim that should not be turned into a rule of seven items for interface design.

### Implications for LTF

The ten principles map well onto an LTF lesson template: a short review of the previous lesson's key facts at the top; new material in small steps with a check question after each; models and worked examples (annotated example flights, checklists done as examples) before the learner practices; guided practice in a first challenge, then independent practice in a second; and spaced weekly or monthly review quizzes pulling items from earlier modules. The 80% target is a useful tuning heuristic for quiz and challenge difficulty: if learners pass nearly everything, raise difficulty; if they fail often, the preceding explanation or guided practice was insufficient. Use it as a design aim, and measure real pass rates after launch. Process questions ("why did you pick that speed?") suit quizzes. Hands-on activity belongs after the basics are taught, which argues against dropping a beginner into the sim before they have the vocabulary. Checklists and cue cards are named as scaffolds, supporting their use in fly mode, with the plan to fade them.

## clark1983reconsidering

Clark (1983), "Reconsidering research on learning from media", Review of Educational Research 53(4), 445-459.

### Core claims

Clark reviews meta-analyses and other studies of whether particular media (television, film, computers, audiotutorials) improve learning, and argues that the consistent finding is that there are no learning benefits to be gained from employing any specific medium to deliver instruction. His analogy is that media are mere vehicles that deliver instruction and do not influence achievement any more than the truck that delivers groceries changes our nutrition. Apparent gains from one medium are, he argues, vulnerable to rival hypotheses: uncontrolled differences in instructional method or content, and novelty effects. He also critiques "media attribute" and "symbol system" theories, and ends with suggestions for more promising research directions (studying methods, aptitudes and tasks rather than media).

### Specifics

Box scores versus effect sizes. In a review of 42 adequate audiotutorial studies, 29 favored audiotutorial and 13 conventional; of 15 with significant differences, 11 favored audiotutorial. The effect size analysis, however, found only about 0.2 standard deviations, equal to roughly 1.6 points on a 100-point examination (Kulik, Kulik and Cohen 1979, as reported by Clark).

Method confounding. In meta-analyses of college computerized versus conventional courses, the effect was 0.51 when different faculty taught the compared courses but dropped to 0.13 when one instructor planned and taught both (C. Kulik, Kulik and Cohen, 1980). Computer groups often saved 30 to 50 percent of lesson time, which Clark attributes to greater design effort, comparing it to racing a precision-engineered racer against the family car. Programmed instruction delivered by text or by computer gave similar effects of about 0.2, and Keller's personalized system of instruction gave about 0.5, so method explains more than medium.

Novelty. In secondary-school computer studies (J. Kulik, Bangert and Williams, 1983), an average effect of 0.32 was 0.56 for studies lasting four weeks or less, 0.3 for five to eight weeks and about 0.2 beyond eight weeks. Published studies averaged larger effects (0.47) than unpublished (0.21) or dissertations (0.3), suggesting editorial bias toward newer media. Clark advises strongly against further media comparison research, because medium will inevitably be confounded with method.

Media attributes. Studies of attributes such as zooming or animated arrows found they could cultivate skills for some learners; but a partial replication (Bovy, 1983) found a static isolation of cues worked even better than zooming or irising, suggesting that only the efficient isolation of relevant cues was needed. Attributes are sometimes sufficient but not necessary conditions.

### Replication and criticism

This is a review and argument, using the meta-analyses available by 1983 (all dating from earlier studies of film, television, early CAI). It is not itself a meta-analysis. Kozma (1994, Educational Technology Research and Development 42(2), 7-19, "Will media influence learning? Reframing the debate") answered, as his title says, by reframing the debate. Nathan and Robinson (2001, Journal of Interactive Learning Research 12(1), 69-88), which I read, argue that Clark and Kozma may agree about instructional design but rest on different views of learning and knowing, and propose treating media and method as mutually constitutive. Clark's later position (secondary account) is that apparent media benefits remain confounded with method. Clark's claim is strongest as a methodological warning about confounded comparisons, weaker as a statement that medium never matters; simulations may enable methods that were previously impossible.

### Implications for LTF

Do not justify design decisions by medium ("video is more engaging than text," "interactive widgets work better"). Ask what instructional method the medium enables and test the method. For LTF, a widget, an annotated diagram and a paragraph of text are vehicles, and the method (worked example, prediction prompt, feedback) is what teaches. Compare designs on method-matched controls: for example, test an interactive airspeed widget against a static diagram with the same prediction prompt and feedback. Expect any novelty gain from a flashy simulation to fade, so judge after repeated use. At the same time, take Kozma's point: a simulator such as MSFS 2024 enables practice methods that no text can, so keep the sim at the center of challenges, while checking that the instructional method around it is sound.

## kirschner2008ten

Kirschner and van Merriënboer (2008), "Ten steps to complex learning: a new approach to instruction and instructional design", chapter 26 in T. L. Good (Ed.), 21st Century Education: A Reference Handbook, Vol. 1, SAGE, pp. 244-253. A short statement of the four-component instructional design model (4C/ID) of van Merriënboer (1997), later developed in the book Ten Steps to Complex Learning (van Merriënboer and Kirschner, 2007, with later editions).

### Core claims

The chapter presents 4C/ID, a prescriptive design approach for programs that teach complex cognitive skills over weeks to years. Complex learning is the integration of knowledge, skills and attitudes, the coordination of qualitatively different constituent skills, and often transfer to daily life and work. The authors argue that atomistic design (small objectives taught separately) causes compartmentalization, fragmentation into part-task sequences, and the "transfer paradox." The remedy is holistic design driven by authentic whole tasks, with supporting information added as needed.

### Specifics

The four blueprint components. (1) Learning tasks: authentic whole-task experiences, organized in easy-to-difficult task classes, with diminishing support within each class (scaffolding) and high variability of practice. (2) Supportive information: helps with nonrecurrent aspects (problem solving and reasoning); explains how the domain is organized (mental models) and how to approach problems in it (cognitive strategies); specified per task class and kept available to learners. (3) Procedural information: how to perform recurrent, routine aspects; step-by-step, presented just in time and quickly fading as expertise grows. (4) Part-task practice: extra repetition on selected recurrent aspects to reach a very high level of automaticity, begun only after the aspect has been met in a meaningful whole task.

The ten steps map to those components: design learning tasks (1), sequence task classes (2), set performance objectives (3), design supportive information (4), analyze cognitive strategies (5), analyze mental models (6), design procedural information (7), analyze cognitive rules (8), analyze prerequisite knowledge (9), design part-task practice (10). The authors link the process to Merrill's pebble-in-the-pond approach (see the Merrill source).

The transfer paradox is explained with an example: diagnosing three error types e1, e2, e3. Teaching them blocked (e1, e1, e1, e2, e2, e2, e3, e3, e3) is efficient but yields low transfer, because learners build knowledge specific to each error; a random-order sequence takes more time and effort but promotes general knowledge and better diagnosis of unseen errors. The authors cite evidence that variability of practice benefits transfer for both simple and complex tasks.

Support versus guidance. Support is product-oriented (givens, goals, solutions) and guidance is process-oriented (how to approach solving). Both diminish with expertise, along a continuum from fully reasoned case studies through partially worked examples (the completion strategy) to conventional tasks. Each task class begins with high-support tasks, continues with lower support and ends with conventional tasks. Learners move to the next task class when they reach prespecified performance standards. Job aids and electronic performance support systems can supply procedural information.

### Replication and criticism

Evidence type: a design model built on cognitive load theory and skill acquisition theory. The chapter is by the model's authors, and I did not read independent evidence on whole 4C/ID programs. A 2021 meta-analysis (Costa, Miranda and Melo, Learning Environments Research) of use and effect is reported in a search summary as concluding that 4C/ID should be prioritized in college and university settings and that higher education seems more suitable; I did not read it, so I make no claims about effect sizes. Kirschner is also an author of the 2006 minimal-guidance paper in this lane, so the model shares that tradition and its critics.

### Implications for LTF

4C/ID is the best fit in this lane for LTF's structure. Learning tasks: each in-sim challenge is a whole flight task (a full pattern, a full approach), not an isolated skill. Task classes: group challenges from easy (calm wind, simple airport, high support) to harder (crosswind, distractions, no hints). Within a class, begin with a demonstration or heavily supported attempt, then lighter support, then none; the sim assessment is the unsupported attempt. Variability: change airport, wind and weather across tasks so the learner abstracts the skill rather than memorizing one scenario. Supportive information: concept pages belong before a task class and stay reachable during it. Procedural information: checklists and step-by-step prompts shown just in time in fly mode and faded as the learner improves. Part-task practice: single-skill drills (reading airspeed, calling out altitudes) only after the learner has met the skill inside a whole flight. Performance standards per task class should drive gating (see the mastery source).
