## Fitts1947

**Core claims.** Fitts and Jones analyzed incidents that the Army Air Forces had filed as "pilot error" and showed that most were predictable consequences of how cockpits were designed. The decisive move was to treat the error as evidence about the machine. This is the founding document of the idea "design out the error rather than blame the person", and it is the ancestor of Dekker's "new view" and Reason's system model.

**Specifics.** Method: the authors collected 460 reported experiences (critical incidents) from pilots through interviews and written reports (the title's number is confirmed; any split by type is not confirmed here). They classified them into types: substitution errors (reaching for the wrong control, such as retracting the landing gear instead of the flaps), adjustment errors (right control, wrong setting), forgetting, errors of reversibility, unintentional activation, and errors of the "cannot reach" kind. Substitution errors were a large class (exact share not confirmed). Typical culprits were controls that looked and felt alike and sat side by side (gear and flap handles in the B-17 and similar types), and controls whose location changed between aircraft types. The remedies they advocated were shape coding (controls in the shape of the thing they affect: wheel-shaped gear handle, wedge-shaped flap handle), location standardization, and separation of similar controls. These became the shape-coded knobs used in aircraft after the war and the basis of the later Chapanis and Roscoe work on control coding. The sister report, "Psychological Aspects of Instrument Display" (TSEAA-694-12A, 1947), documented instrument reading errors, notably the three-pointer altimeter that pilots misread by 1,000 or 10,000 feet; Grether's 1949 laboratory work on instrument reading compared altimeter designs; the commonly quoted misreading percentage for the three-pointer type could not be traced this session and is not confirmed, but the finding that counter or window designs reduced such errors is the accepted lesson. Altimeter misreading is the standard example of a display that is legible but not readable.

**Quotation.** None given; wording not confirmed.

**Criticism and status.** The classification is old and loosely defined; the sample is self-reported and biased toward memorable events. The central insight has held for 75 years and is the base of every later error taxonomy. The 1989 paper "Fitts and Jones' Analysis of Pilot Error: 40 Years Later" (Wright State) applied the taxonomy to cars and computers.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Treat learner mistakes in challenges as data on the design, not on the learner: if many learners put the flaps and the gear handle in the wrong place, the screen is wrong.
2. Code controls by shape, not color alone, in widgets (flap lever, mixture, throttle, carb heat), echoing the real C172 knob shapes. This is also a strong identity device.
3. Separate similar controls in the "fly mode" panel and never put two same-looking buttons with different consequences side by side.
4. Show an altimeter widget that teaches three-pointer misreading explicitly, with a window readout as the safe pattern.
5. Log common wrong answers per challenge and treat clusters as design bugs.

## Degani1992

**Core claims.** A literature review of typography research aimed at the cockpit: typefaces, character size, letter case, line length, spacing, layout, color and contrast, and the reading conditions of cockpits (glare, vibration, poor light, paper quality). It ends with a list of graphical recommendations (19 in the Smithsonian's summary of the report).

**Specifics.** The recommendations, as reported by secondary coverage and the report abstract, include: use a sans-serif face for cockpit documents; set long text in lowercase (mixed case) rather than all capitals, reserving capitals for short warnings and labels; use black type on a white or light ground for most documents (high contrast); avoid italics and heavy use of underlining; avoid justified text where it produces uneven word spacing; keep lines to moderate length; leave generous leading and white space; use bold sparingly and a limited set of typographic cues; separate sections with layout rather than ornament. The report discusses why capitals read more slowly in running text (loss of word shape) and why checklist headings and critical items still benefit from capitals or bold. Color: restrict the palette, use red only for critical items, and never rely on color alone, since 1992 printing and cockpit lighting alter colors (night lighting, red lights, glare). Reading conditions: manuals must work in turbulence, at dim light levels and when held on a knee. Many exact numeric recommendations (point sizes, line lengths) exist in the report and should be read from the PDF (NTRS 19930010781) before quoting.

**Criticism and status.** The evidence base is the 1960s to 1980s legibility literature (Tinker, Poulton, Rehe, etc.), partly superseded by modern screen research; sans serif advantage in print is weak in the wider legibility literature (see lane 02). A 2024 PACDEFF paper (Schmid, Thompson and Sargent) revisits it for digital documentation. Treat it as the aviation-specific justification for restrained typography, not as proof that sans serif is intrinsically more legible.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Checklists and procedure text: mixed case for steps, capitals only for short challenge words (the "CHECK", "SET" verbs) and warnings.
2. Black on white (Day) and the equivalent high-contrast inversion (Night) are the baseline; decorative color is secondary.
3. Never use color as the only cue, which also meets accessibility goals.
4. Generous leading and short lines for in-sim use, where the learner reads at arm's length under a second screen's glare.
5. The report's own look (plain, dense, ruled) is part of the aviation documentation culture LTF can borrow from.

## Degani1993

**Core claims.** Checklists are one of several tools for crew standardization and error trapping, and a good one is designed around human limits, the cockpit's task flow and the airline's culture, not just the list of items. The paper (with Degani and Wiener's 1990 NASA report on the normal checklist) frames checklists by function and by use.

**Specifics.** Functions: provide a standard sequence; serve as a memory aid for items that can be forgotten; provide a back-up (a second line of defense) to catch errors; enable cross-checking and shared mental models between crew; support quality control. Formats: challenge-do-verify (a person reads, another responds), do-verify (the crew do the items from memory or by flow, then verify with the list), and "flow" patterns tied to panel position. They examined length (long checklists degrade attention), item order (follow the physical sequence of the cockpit and the task, not a logical but unrelated order), item wording (short, unambiguous, with the response stated), and the human issues: the checklist can be rushed, skipped, run on autopilot (the response becomes a habit and is spoken without looking), or abandoned under interruption. The accident record that motivated the work includes the 1987 Northwest Flight 255 and 1988 Delta Flight 1141 takeoffs without flaps and slats set, where the before-takeoff checklist was missed or run without the checking being done. Design guidelines they give include: limit the checklist to critical items, make it as short as safety allows, put the checks in the order of the physical layout, have an unambiguous indicator of where the crew is in the list (and what is outstanding after interruption), minimize items with indirect responses ("set" versus "checked"), and design for the airline's real cockpit practices. They also argue that checklist philosophy (who runs it, when, whether automated) needs to be explicit.

**Criticism and status.** Field studies, not controlled trials. Their principles have been reused in CAP 676 (UK CAA) and in Burian's work, and cited in the medical checklist literature (Haynes 2009). Not specific to single-pilot VFR flying, though the C172 normal checklists are the same type.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Checklist UI should show the current step, completed steps and outstanding steps at a glance, so an interruption does not lose place.
2. Teach the difference between a "flow" (do from memory in panel order) and a challenge-response checklist; build both into lessons.
3. Order steps in the physical sweep of the panel, matching how the learner's eyes travel in the simulator.
4. Each item states the expected response, not just the control name.
5. Teach complacency: add a challenge that includes a deliberately wrong state to catch "responding without looking".

## Burian2006

**Core claims.** Emergency and abnormal checklists differ from normal ones: they are used under stress and time pressure, are infrequently practiced, and must support decision-making as well as execution. The paper offers design guidance from NASA's Emergency and Abnormal Situations (EAS) project, which separated internal factors (format, layout, wording) from external factors (time criticality, workload, crew experience).

**Specifics.** The guidance (exact wording not confirmed; read the PDF) covers: titles that match what the pilot recognizes, not an engineer's name for the fault; a clear first action, with immediate "memory" actions distinguished from list actions; consistent verb use and a consistent response format; conditional steps ("if ... then") structured so the reader never has to hold a condition in mind; separate cautions and notes placed before the step they apply to (reading a warning after the action is useless); explicit indications of the end of the checklist and of any further checklists required; avoiding scrolling or paging where steps can be lost; minimal text with unambiguous abbreviations; typography that signals priority with a restricted set of devices; and attention to physical use (print size under poor light). The EAS work found that real checklists in airline use deviate from these principles, with long wordy steps, branching that is hard to follow and inconsistent formats between manufacturers. The Burian text also refers to UK CAA CAP 676 (paper checklists) and CAP 708 (electronic checklists) as earlier guidance that did not fully cover abnormal-situation design.

**Criticism and status.** A conference paper summarizing a project, with guidelines grounded in expert judgment and observed use more than controlled experiments. The FAA connection could not be confirmed in search; treat it as NASA work.

**Further detail.** The Emergency and Abnormal Situations project at NASA Ames reviewed the checklists of several manufacturers and operators and observed real use. A recurring theme is that emergency and abnormal checklists are the ones pilots practice least and need most. The guidance addresses a distinction between items that must be done at once from memory and those done by reading, and recommends that the first step tell the pilot whether to act immediately. It addresses the distinction between a checklist that is entirely linear and one with branches, and recommends that branching be made visible and kept to a minimum, since each branch asks the pilot to hold a condition in memory under stress. It deals with language: use consistent verbs, avoid ambiguous abbreviations and avoid negative phrasing that can be misread. It addresses the placement of notes and cautions so that they come before, not after, the actions they affect, and that the reader can tell which text is an action and which is information. For electronic checklists, it raises concerns about screen space, scrolling, and indicating which items are complete, skipped or inapplicable. It also notes the role of crew coordination: who reads, who acts and who flies the aircraft, since the checklist is a tool within the crew's task management, not a replacement for flying the aircraft first. Details here are from the project's general findings and should be checked in the paper.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Emergency-procedure lessons (engine failure, fire) should use a distinct, simpler format than normal checklists, with the first action unmistakable.
2. Put cautions before the step they apply to, not after.
3. Conditional steps appear as visual branches with one decision per screen block.
4. Name procedures by the symptom the learner sees ("Engine roughness"), not the system name.
5. Make the end state explicit ("Checklist complete. Next: land as soon as practical").

## Haynes2009

**Core claims.** Introducing a 19-item WHO Surgical Safety Checklist at eight hospitals in eight cities reduced major complications and deaths. The study is the main evidence that the aviation checklist idea transfers to another high-risk field, and Gawande's 2009 book The Checklist Manifesto brought it to a wide public.

**Specifics.** Prospective before-and-after study, October 2007 to September 2008. Patient counts per phase are not confirmed here (look them up in the abstract). Search confirmed the death rate fell from 1.5 percent to 0.8 percent and inpatient complications from 11.0 percent to 7.0 percent. The checklist has three stages (before anesthesia induction, before skin incision, before the patient leaves the operating room); the often-quoted run time is not confirmed; this staged "pause points" structure came from aviation, as did the insistence on a short list of critical items and on spoken, team-based verification. Gawande's text draws heavily on Degani and Wiener and on the Boeing Model 299 story (the 1935 crash, after which pilots used a checklist).

**Criticism and status.** No concurrent control group; hospitals were aware of being studied (Hawthorne effect); subsequent evidence is mixed. Urbach et al. (NEJM 2014) found no significant reduction in Ontario after mandated adoption, and later work suggests benefits depend on engagement and implementation. The aviation analogy is real for short, well-designed lists but is not proof that every checklist works.

**Further detail and context.** The study was run for the World Health Organization's Safe Surgery Saves Lives programme, led from Harvard by Atul Gawande. Eight hospitals in eight cities were chosen to span income levels and regions, so the result is not limited to rich-country operating rooms. Each site first collected baseline data on complications and deaths, then introduced the checklist and collected data again. The checklist divides an operation into three pauses: before anesthesia is induced (sign in), before the skin incision (time out), and before the patient leaves the room (sign out). At each pause the team confirms a short set of items aloud, for example patient identity and site, known allergies, antibiotic timing, equipment and counts, and each team member introducing themselves by name and role. The introduction step is deliberate: it flattens hierarchy so that anyone feels able to speak up, which is the aviation crew resource management lesson carried over. The design follows Degani and Wiener's advice: short, critical items only, with a pause structure, and a version meant to be adapted locally rather than imposed unchanged. Gawande's 2009 book The Checklist Manifesto explains the lineage, including the 1935 Boeing Model 299 crash that led to pilots' checklists, and interviews with airline checklist designers.

The headline figures (death rate roughly halved, complications down by about a third) are widely quoted, but the exact patient counts are not confirmed here. The study is an uncontrolled before-and-after comparison, so secular trends and the attention of being studied could contribute. Later large observational studies reached different conclusions. A population study in Ontario (Urbach and colleagues, 2014) found no significant improvement after mandatory adoption, and commentators argue that checklists work when staff engage with them and fail when ticked as paperwork. For LTF the safe reading is that a well-designed, short, team-owned checklist can help, and that how it is used matters at least as much as its content.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use this as the authority for "why checklists" copy: short, critical items, pause points, team-style verification.
2. Teach pause points in a flight: before start, before takeoff, before landing.
3. Be honest in copy: checklists help when done attentively, not when ticked by rote.
4. Keep any "run through the checklist" interaction time-light so learners do not skip it.
5. Use the three-pause structure for lesson structure too (brief, do, debrief).

## Endsley1995

**Core claims.** Situation awareness (SA) is "the perception of elements in the environment within a volume of time and space, the comprehension of their meaning, and the projection of their status in the near future" (paraphrase of the standard definition; exact wording not confirmed). Three levels: Level 1 perception, Level 2 comprehension, Level 3 projection. SA is distinct from decision-making and performance; poor SA is a primary source of human-error accidents, and SA is limited by attention and working memory, mitigated by long-term memory structures (mental models and schemata).

**Specifics.** The model places SA in a loop with the task and system factors (interface design, complexity, automation), and individual factors (goals, preconceptions, abilities, experience, training). Related work (Endsley, Designing for Situation Awareness, 2003, with Bolte and Jones) derived design principles and listed "SA demons": attentional tunneling, requisite memory trap, workload/anxiety/fatigue/other stressors, data overload, misplaced salience, complexity creep, errant mental models and out-of-the-loop syndrome. Design guidance includes: organize information around goals not sensors; support Level 2 and 3 by presenting comprehension and projection (trends, predictions, "what happens next") rather than raw data; make critical cues salient without overloading; support global SA at all times; avoid system complexity and hide mode logic. Measurement: SAGAT (freeze the simulation, query the operator) and SPAM/SART.

**Quotation.** None.

**Criticism and status.** Flach (1995, "Situation awareness: proceed with caution") and Dekker argue it is circular (good performance implies good SA) and risks becoming a catch-all explanation; the representationalist model (a mental picture in the head) conflicts with ecological and distributed-cognition views (Hutchins). SAGAT freezes the task, and criterion validity is debated. Nonetheless the three levels are a widely used vocabulary and FAA training uses SA explicitly.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Structure instrument-scan lessons by SA level: read the value (L1), understand what it means (L2), predict what it will do (L3, with trend arrows in widgets).
2. Challenge feedback should ask "what will happen next?" prompts, not only "what does it read?".
3. Teach tunneling: a challenge in which the learner focuses on the altimeter while the heading drifts.
4. Dashboards that put the key state (phase, next step, current limit) in one fixed place support SA in fly mode.
5. Avoid complexity creep in the UI itself.

## Wickens1995

**Core claims.** The proximity compatibility principle (PCP): the perceptual proximity of display elements should match their task proximity (the degree to which information must be mentally integrated). Elements that must be combined for one judgment should be displayed close together in perceptual space (nearby, same color, within an object, linked by shape); elements that are used separately in focused attention should be separated, because close proximity causes interference and clutter. Proximity can be spatial, by color, by code, or by object.

**Specifics.** Task proximity ranges from low (focused attention on one variable) through integration to a single judgment, to "dependence" in the same computation. Display proximity forms can be position, linkage (lines, shapes), object formation (a single object with emergent features), color, and common motion. Findings the paper reviews: integrating dimensions into an object display (such as a polygon or an artificial horizon with pitch and roll in one symbol) speeds integrated judgments but can hurt reading individual values; separating displays hurts integration; color coding is a cheaper proximity cue than spatial grouping, but color can attract attention to things that are not related; a mixed "proximity compatible" design only works if tasks are known. Wickens elsewhere (Wickens and Hollands 2000, Engineering Psychology and Human Performance) lists 13 principles of display design in four groups: perceptual (make displays legible, avoid absolute judgment limits, support top-down processing, redundancy gain, discriminability); mental model (pictorial realism, the principle of the moving part); attention (minimize information access cost, proximity compatibility, multiple resources); and memory (replace memory with visual information, predictive aids, consistency with other displays). Wickens's cost of "information access" (the effort to move eyes or scroll) is the quantity to minimize.

**Criticism and status.** Many experiments support it; some studies found that integration benefits fail to appear when integrating is not required, and that color proximity helped while spatial proximity did not (follow-up study). The principle is general enough to be hard to falsify. It does not say how close "close" is.

**Use for LTF.**
1. In widgets, group by the decision, not by the instrument: put airspeed and flap limits (V-speeds) together when the learner compares them.
2. Separate unrelated instruments with space and a rule, not a shared card background.
3. Use color families to bind related values, and a different family to separate warnings.
4. Keep the info access cost low in fly mode: no scrolling, no hidden tabs, the next step always in the same place.
5. Don't pack a "panel" into one object if learners must read each value separately.

## Sarter1995

**Core claims.** Mode errors in supervisory control come from automation with multiple modes whose status is not made salient. Pilots lose "mode awareness" and are surprised ("automation surprises"). The core questions the pilot needs answered: What is it doing? Why is it doing that? What will it do next? (a formulation widely attributed to Woods and Sarter; exact wording not confirmed).

**Specifics.** The paper synthesizes a program of studies: field surveys and simulator studies of pilots of glass-cockpit aircraft (Boeing 737-300 and 757/767, Airbus A-320) flying with the flight management system. Findings: modes interact with each other and change automatically (indirect mode changes, not commanded by the pilot); mode annunciations are small and similar; the same input produces different effects in different modes; pilots with high experience still confuse modes in unusual situations; and monitoring is weakest when the situation is not in the expected path, a "knowledge calibration" failure. Recommendations: make the mode display salient and spatial (not one line of abbreviations), use trend and prediction displays that show what the automation will do next, minimize the number of modes and indirect transitions, support direct manipulation, and train for the unusual cases. The NASA Technical Memorandum series (NASA CR-177617) hosts earlier versions.

**Criticism and status.** Findings have been reproduced across later accident analyses (e.g., NASA analyses of the 737 MAX and Asiana 214); the research drove FAA 2013 flight-path management guidance. Some critique says the question frame blames design where training is also at fault.

**Further detail.** The paper builds on a set of studies of pilots using cockpit automation, mainly the flight management system and autopilot modes in modern airliners. The researchers combined surveys and interviews of line pilots with simulator scenarios, and found that pilots coped well in the normal paths but showed gaps in their understanding of the system in unusual combinations of modes. A key finding was that mode awareness failures were not limited to novices: experienced pilots also had incomplete mental models of how modes interact, because the system has many modes, some of which engage or change automatically depending on conditions. The paper defines mode error as an action appropriate for one mode applied in another, and ties it to three conditions: multiple modes, a mismatch between the pilot's model and the system's behavior, and weak feedback about current mode. It argues that the usual blame on pilot error obscures a design problem: the information about the active mode was presented as small text on a flight mode annunciator, easy to miss and hard to monitor. The remedies the authors propose center on making system state and future behavior observable, reducing the number of modes, and supporting pilots in tracking the automation's intent. They link this to the idea of observability and to the broader concept of automation surprise, where the system does something the pilot did not expect. Subsequent studies of accidents have repeatedly found mode confusion among the contributing factors.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Any widget with modes (autopilot, "fly mode") needs an always-visible mode label and a "what next" cue.
2. Ensure a single control does one thing: a button must not have different effects in different states.
3. When LTF's own UI changes state (checklist complete, challenge mode), announce it with more than a small text change.
4. Teach autopilot modes in lessons as an "automation surprises" case study.
5. Use the three questions as a literal teaching prompt after each simulator lesson.

## Bainbridge1983

**Core claims.** The "ironies" of automation: automating a process leaves the operator with the tasks the designer could not automate, and with a monitoring job that people do badly. The more advanced the automation, the more crucial the human contribution. Designers are a source of error, yet the operator is asked to be the backup.

**Specifics.** Five or six main ironies. (1) Designer error: the human is left to cover designer failings. (2) Skill degradation: manual skills deteriorate when unused, so the operator is least capable at the moment of need. (3) Monitoring: humans cannot maintain vigilance for long on an unchanging source; citing Mackworth-type findings, effective vigilance declines in around half an hour, so a human is not a reliable monitor of rarely failing automation. (4) Takeover: operators must act fastest when the process is most abnormal and least understood, using a mental model that has not been updated. (5) Training: the skills for rare takeovers must come from simulation, since there is no real practice. (6) Alarm and display design: displays must support intervention, not just normal operation. Suggested remedies: give the operator interesting work, maintain skills through simulated practice, give the automation to the operator as a tool not a replacement, and use alarms with care (a set of alarm and attentional guidelines).

**Quotation.** None given; the often-quoted line that the more advanced the control system, the more crucial the human operator's contribution could not be checked verbatim.

**Criticism and status.** Conceptual, from industrial process control, not aviation, but cited extensively in aviation and in the 2023 Ergonomics special editorial commemorating it. A later paper (2018, Strauch) tracks it to aviation; automation research has confirmed manual flying skill erosion.

**Further detail.** The paper is short, about five pages, and is written from the perspective of process control, where an operator oversees a plant. Bainbridge argues that the common view that automation reduces human error because humans are unreliable has an irony at its core: the designer is human too, and design errors are a main source of system problems. Second, the automatic system is given the tasks the designer can specify, leaving the operator the rest, which tends to be the hardest tasks. Third, the operator is assigned a monitoring role, but humans are not good at monitoring something that rarely needs action, since studies from the 1940s onward show that vigilance falls off after a fairly short time. The remedy usually proposed is an alarm, but alarms themselves need the operator to keep a good model of the process. Fourth, manual skills deteriorate with disuse, and cognitive skills need to be practiced, so the operator is less able to take over precisely when they must. Fifth, when something goes wrong the operator must diagnose an unusual situation with a process model that has been unused, and may have little time. She discusses training: simulators can help but cannot train for faults that the designers did not imagine. She suggests that operators should have meaningful work, that displays should support the operator in understanding the process state, and that computers might support the human rather than the reverse. The paper has been called one of the most cited in human factors; as a conceptual argument it is not experimentally validated, but later research on out-of-the-loop performance supports its main claims. Specific figures for vigilance decline are not confirmed here.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Teach hand-flying before autopilot: the learner needs the manual skill that the automation does not replace.
2. Include "automation fails" scenarios in challenges.
3. Don't automate the learner's attention: auto-advancing screens and auto-ticking checklists teach passivity.
4. Practice rare events (engine failure) repeatedly in short drills, since real exposure is rare.
5. Keep the learner as the actor in fly mode: it prompts, never acts.

## Wiener1980

**Core claims.** The first systematic human factors critique of flight-deck automation: automation was introduced for engineering and economic reasons, and its human consequences (workload shifts, boredom, complacency, loss of skill, communication changes) were not considered. It suggests design guidelines such as keeping the pilot informed and in the loop.

**Specifics.** Areas they cover: human-machine interface, monitoring and vigilance, workload (automation redistributes, not simply reduces, workload and can increase it in high-workload phases), skill degradation, job satisfaction, and crew communication and command (who is in charge). They anticipate "clumsy automation" (Wiener 1989: automation that makes the easy parts easier and the hard parts harder, as it reduces workload when it is already low and increases it when it is high). Examples drawn from incidents of autopilot and autothrottle misuse. Guidelines proposed include: automation should be designed to be human-centered, with the pilot able to see what it is doing; use the automation fail-soft; keep humans doing meaningful tasks; and avoid automation for its own sake. A key suggestion is that a system with the pilot as monitor requires the pilot to be given a good reason and ability to monitor.

**Criticism and status.** Predates glass cockpits; later work by the same lead author and by Billings (Aviation Automation: The Search for a Human-Centered Approach, 1997) expanded it. NASA TM 81206 is the earlier version of the paper; compare wording to the journal version.

**Further detail.** The paper is a review rather than an experiment. It examines why automation entered the cockpit: advances in computers, fuel economy and air traffic demands, together with the engineering idea that removing the human from routine tasks removes error. The authors question this. They discuss how automation changes the pilot from active controller to monitor, and how research on vigilance suggests that humans are poor at long watchful monitoring of reliable systems. They raise the issue of which functions to automate and which to leave to the pilot, and warn against allocating to the human only what the designer could not automate, which leaves a fragmented, unsatisfying role. They discuss workload: automation can reduce workload in cruise yet raise it in approach and when the system must be reprogrammed, a pattern later called clumsy automation by Wiener. They raise skill loss, the effect on crew communication when attention goes to the machine, and whether the pilot or the machine is in command. They also argue for error-tolerant design and for pilots understanding what automation will do. The paper appeared as a NASA memorandum before its journal version and shaped the NASA and industry workshops of the 1980s, which led to Billings's 1991 principles of human-centered automation: the pilot must be in command, must be involved, and must be informed, and the automation must be predictable and able to monitor the human. The paper's concerns have been borne out by later incidents, though the cockpits it addressed are earlier than today's glass cockpits and the exact structure of its recommendations should be checked in the original.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Use as a source for copy on autopilot lessons.
2. LTF's own automation (auto-grading, auto-progress) should follow the "informed, in the loop" rule.
3. Workload redistribution: do not lower workload in easy phases and raise it in hard ones; use the same short checklist all the time.
4. Show the learner what the system infers (for example, which phase of flight it thinks they are in), so they can correct it.
5. Avoid replacing meaningful tasks with button taps.

## Hutchins1995

**Core claims.** Cognition is distributed across people and artifacts. The cockpit as a whole remembers and computes landing speeds; the pilots are parts of that system. Speed bugs and cards transform a memory task into a perceptual one.

**Specifics.** Ethnography of an MD-80 cockpit (airline transport jet). The speed to fly on approach depends on aircraft weight and flap configuration. Pilots look up the target speeds in a speed card (a table), and set movable markers (speed bugs) on the edge of the airspeed indicator. The task of remembering speeds becomes the task of seeing where the airspeed pointer is relative to the bug. Specific processes: the flaps position is read from the panel; speeds are announced by one pilot to the other; the card is read by the pilot not flying; the bugs are set, each as an external memory; the crew coordinate with the check of the numbers. Hutchins shows that errors are detected by comparisons across representations (card, bug, FMS). A widely quoted line says speed bugs are part of the process by which the cockpit system remembers speeds (secondary source reports it from p. 283; verbatim wording not confirmed). He argues that the properties of the system (reliability, error trapping) are not properties of any single pilot's mind.

**Criticism and status.** Influential in HCI and cognitive science; later (2014) work on "how a cockpit forgets speeds" shows the system can fail when the artifacts change (FMS in place of bugs). It is a case study, not an experiment, and distributed cognition is criticized as hard to test.

**Further detail.** The study comes from Hutchins's fieldwork with airline crews, which became the book Cognition in the Wild (1995), where he analyzed ship navigation. Here he examines a cockpit as a system that performs a computation: determining the correct approach speeds for a landing. The landing speeds depend on the aircraft's weight and the flap setting. The crew uses a table of speeds, often in a booklet, to find the numbers for the current weight and then sets markers (speed bugs) on the airspeed indicator. Hutchins traces how information is transformed through a sequence of representations: a number in the table, a spoken call, a marker position on the dial, and finally the relation between a needle and a marker, which is perceived rather than remembered. He argues that this chain reduces the demand on individual memory and spreads error detection across people and artifacts, since a wrong number would be noticed when the pilots compare sources or when a marker looks out of place. He also shows that the crew's activity is coordinated through talk and the shared view of the instruments, so that the pilots' roles are part of the cognitive system. The theoretical point is that the unit of analysis for cognition can be the whole sociotechnical system, not only the individual head. The work is influential in HCI, design and cognitive science; critics note that it is an interpretive case study and that its claims about system-level properties are hard to test. Specific details of the airline procedures are from memory and the verbatim quotation is not confirmed.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Design widgets as external memory: put V-speeds as colored arcs and bugs on the airspeed widget, mirroring real airspeed indicator markings (white, green, yellow arcs, red line).
2. Replace "remember 65 knots" with "see the pointer relative to the mark".
3. Teach that cards and markers are the cockpit's memory.
4. Fly mode should show the targets for the current phase, so the learner does not memorize.
5. This is a rich identity source: the speed bug, the speed card.

## Reason1990

**Core claims.** Human error is a normal by-product of human cognition and not a moral failing; errors are shaped by task conditions and system defenses. Reason separates slips and lapses (execution and memory failures with correct intention), mistakes (rule-based or knowledge-based planning failures) and violations (deliberate deviations). He builds on Rasmussen's skill, rule and knowledge levels and proposes the Generic Error-Modelling System (GEMS).

**Specifics.** Slips arise from attentional capture (a strong habit takes over: "capture errors") and omissions; the defenses are cues and forcing functions. Rule-based mistakes occur from applying a good rule in the wrong situation or a bad rule. Knowledge-based mistakes occur when the situation is novel and reasoning in working memory is limited by bounded rationality, confirmation bias and "cognitive underspecification". The book introduces the notion of active failures (unsafe acts at the sharp end) and latent conditions (decisions by designers and managers that lie dormant), and the defenses-in-depth image that later became the "Swiss cheese model" (1997 book; 2000 BMJ). He advises on "error management" strategies: focus on conditions, not on persons; use defenses; design for detection and recovery. The distinction of error types is a central tool of airline training (threat and error management, Helmreich).

**Criticism and status.** The Swiss cheese picture is criticized for suggesting linear causation and for its vagueness in practice (Dekker, Hollnagel's FRAM, Leveson's STAMP). The slips/mistakes taxonomy is robust. The book is from memory in this lane; confirm editions before citing page numbers.

**Further detail.** The book is structured in three parts: the nature of error, the cognitive mechanisms and the management of risk. Reason distinguishes error from violation by whether the action was intended to deviate. Slips and lapses are failures of execution or storage in a correctly formed plan, and are most likely in routine, automatic activity where attention is elsewhere. He explains slips by the interplay of automatic control (schemas, running on stored routines) and attentional monitoring: attention checks are intermittent, so a strong habit can capture an action. Classic examples are doing the usual thing when the situation has changed. Mistakes are failures in planning: rule-based mistakes apply a rule wrongly or apply a bad rule, and knowledge-based mistakes arise in unfamiliar problems when people reason in limited working memory with biases such as overconfidence and fixation on a first hypothesis. The Generic Error-Modelling System (GEMS) describes how people try rule-based solutions first and drop to the more laborious knowledge-based level only when rules fail. Reason then turns to organizations: accidents emerge when the active failures of operators combine with latent conditions created earlier by management and design, and the defenses meant to catch errors have holes. His later image of layers of cheese was popularized in later writing. Critics say the layered model suggests simple linear causation and is hard to apply predictively, and the taxonomy of error types is sometimes applied loosely. Even so, the distinction between slips, mistakes and violations remains the standard vocabulary of aviation threat-and-error management training. Details here are from memory.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Classify learner errors as slip, mistake or violation to decide the fix (cue, instruction, or motivation).
2. Teach the "error chain" as an accident story, which is also a rich narrative device.
3. Add forcing functions in challenges, such as "you cannot start the engine until the mixture is set".
4. Make recovery visible: show what the learner did to catch an error, rewarding detection.
5. Language of non-blame in feedback: "the setup invited this error".

## FAA2010AC251322

**Core claims.** 14 CFR 25.1322 (amended 2010, harmonized with EASA CS 25.1322) requires flightcrew alerts to be prioritized by urgency: warnings (immediate awareness and immediate action), cautions (immediate awareness, subsequent action) and advisories (awareness, possible action). AC 25.1322-1 (December 2010) is the FAA's guidance on compliance.

**Specifics.** Color rules in the regulation: red is for warnings only, amber (or yellow) for cautions, and advisories may be any color except red or green. Colors for alerts must be distinct, and red, amber and yellow are not to be used for non-alerting functions on the flight deck in a way that interferes with alerting. Alerts must have an aural or tactile component for warnings and cautions as appropriate, and must be removed when the condition ends. The same hierarchy is used by integrated glass displays such as the Garmin G1000 (not confirmed; no Garmin document was read), with red for warning, amber for caution, white for advisory. Also noted: color alone is not sufficient (a redundant cue like text or shape is required); color presentation is verified under expected lighting. The FAA Human Factors Design Standard (HF-STD-001) and Part 23 guidance (AC 23.1311-1C for electronic displays in light aircraft, which includes the Cessna 172 with glass panel) give related rules on display color and the use of red/amber/green. The "quiet, dark" cockpit philosophy (no light, no alert when all is normal), originally from Airbus and Boeing, is the aim of the alerting hierarchy.

**Criticism and status.** An authoritative regulatory source, but the text I could verify was the rule; AC section wording was not read. The color convention follows a long tradition (red warning, amber caution, green normal) that is itself an arbitrary but learned convention; the legibility of red on dark backgrounds is poor (see lane 05).

**Further detail.** The rule and its advisory circular define alert levels by the combination of urgency and required action. A warning demands immediate crew awareness and immediate action; a caution demands immediate awareness and subsequent action; an advisory demands awareness and may require action. The alerts should be prioritized so that the most critical is most conspicuous, avoid nuisance alerts that desensitize the crew, and be removed when the condition no longer applies. The regulation reserves red for warnings and amber for cautions, and prohibits using those colors in other flight deck functions in ways that interfere with their alerting role. The text also expects that crew can distinguish colors under all expected lighting, and that alerts are accompanied by more than color alone. The related philosophy of the quiet, dark cockpit means that in normal flight no alert lights are on, so any illuminated alert is meaningful. These conventions are mirrored in light-aircraft glass cockpit displays, though this entry did not read the Part 23 advisory circular text, and details specific to the Cessna 172 glass panel were not confirmed. The rule's color use matches the common aviation and engineering pattern of red for stop or danger, amber for caution, green for safe or normal, and cyan or white for information, a pattern that is conventional rather than natural and must be learned, which is why consistent use matters.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Make red mean "stop, act now" only; amber means "caution, attend"; white/cyan advisory; green normal. Never use these colors for decoration or brand.
2. Reserve red for the one or two real-danger moments, so it keeps meaning.
3. Always pair color with text or an icon.
4. The "quiet" philosophy: LTF's normal state should be calm, with alerts rare.
5. This gives LTF a ready semantic palette that learners will meet in the sim.

## Roscoe1968

**Core claims.** Aircraft displays should be designed on principles derived from human perception and from how pilots think of the aircraft's motion: pictorial realism (the display should look like the world it represents, with a spatial layout and orientation that match it), the principle of the moving part (the element that moves on the display should be the element that moves in the pilot's mental model, and in the same direction), and others including frequency separation.

**Specifics.** The moving part: in an attitude indicator an "inside-out" display moves the horizon bar against a fixed aircraft symbol (the view of a pilot looking out), whereas "outside-in" shows a moving aircraft over a fixed horizon. Roscoe argued that pilots think of the aircraft as moving, so the aircraft symbol should move, but that the real world appears to be moving relative to the pilot, creating a conflict that favoured the horizon-moving display (the "inside-out" convention has become standard and is the one used on the standard attitude indicator) but proposed a "frequency-separated" display in which the aircraft symbol moves with fast changes and the horizon moves with slow changes. Another principle: the direction of motion should be compatible with the direction of control input and consequence (up for increasing altitude, clockwise for increasing value). He advocated moving the altimeter tape for a vertical scale where up equals higher. His related findings: tapes with a moving scale and fixed pointer versus fixed scale with moving pointer, and the combination of pictorial and numeric cues. The 1980 book Aviation Psychology (Iowa State) and support the main themes. The "basic T" instrument layout (attitude indicator top center, airspeed to its left, altimeter to its right, heading indicator below) descends from the RAF "blind flying panel" and the US Army Air Forces standardization in the 1940s and is a case of control-display standardization rather than a principle of Roscoe's; origin not confirmed this session.

**Criticism and status.** Citation here is from memory, not confirmed by search. The moving-part principle has been tested with mixed results, depends on the task and on pilot training; the "inside-out" display won by convention more than by evidence.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Widgets should move as the real thing does: the airspeed pointer rotates clockwise as speed increases; the altitude pointers increase clockwise.
2. Use the basic T layout for the instrument panel widgets, which also trains the scan.
3. Make the animation direction match the cause: pulling back on the yoke moves the horizon down on screen, and show why.
4. Offer a "view from outside" and "view from inside" toggle to teach this tension.
5. Sims show the layout learners will meet; keep widget geometry real.

## Dekker2002

**Core claims.** The "new view" of human error: error is a symptom of trouble deeper in the system, not a cause. To understand an event, reconstruct why people's actions made sense to them at the time (local rationality), and avoid hindsight bias, counterfactual reasoning and outcome bias. The book is a practical field guide for investigators, with an old-view versus new-view contrast.

**Specifics.** Old view: people are a problem to control, error is the cause, bad apples. New view: people are part of the solution, error is the effect, systems drive behavior. Key tools: tracing the "local rationality principle", the "Samurai" problem (we judge decisions by what we now know), the distinction between "what happened" and "why it made sense", the sequence-of-events diagrams, and the argument that "human error" labels end investigations instead of starting them. Includes discussion of drift into failure (the slow normalization of deviance), of automation, and of procedural compliance (checklists are not followed because they do not match the work, so the gap between rules and practice should be studied). Dekker's critique of situation awareness (it as an after-the-fact label) is in the same line.

**Criticism and status.** The "new view" is itself an advocacy position and has been criticized for under-weighting individual responsibility, which Dekker answers with "just culture". The book is accessible but not experimental. Publication details (3rd ed. 2014 CRC Press) from memory.

**Further detail and structure.** The book is organized as a guide to a change of perspective. It begins with two views of human error. In the old view, human error is a cause of accidents, the system is basically safe and people are the unreliable element, so the remedy is more procedures, more discipline and more automation. In the new view, error is a symptom, a consequence of trouble deeper inside the system, and people create safety by adapting to conflicting goals and incomplete information. Dekker builds this into practical advice. First, reconstruct the situation from the point of view of the people inside it, using the data they had at the time, rather than from the investigator's later knowledge. This is the local rationality principle: what people did made sense to them given their goals, knowledge and attention. Second, beware of hindsight bias, where knowing the outcome makes the path to it look obvious and the missed cues look glaring. Third, treat labels such as loss of situation awareness, complacency or non-compliance as conclusions that need explaining, not explanations. Fourth, look at how work is really done versus how it is described in procedures: practical drift, where small adaptations become normal, is common and often efficient until it is not.

The book also treats the relationship between procedures and practice honestly. Rules cannot cover every situation, so people adapt; blaming them for the adaptation after a failure misses that the same adaptation usually succeeds. This links to just culture, a framework Dekker later developed, which separates honest mistakes from reckless behavior without defaulting to blame. Criticism is that the new view can seem to under-weight individual accountability and that its advice is qualitative and hard to test experimentally. Dekker also criticizes the situation awareness construct as a label rather than a mechanism. The book is a position text by an experienced pilot and safety scientist; details in this entry are from memory, and the publisher and edition information has not been confirmed online.

**Use for LTF.**
1. Feedback language: describe why the answer was reasonable, then show the better cue.
2. Never label a learner's miss as "careless".
3. Design post-challenge debriefs as reconstruction ("here is what the instruments showed at that time").
4. Show the gap between checklist and practice honestly: when the sim differs from the real aircraft, say so.
5. Track patterns of drift, as with a learner who skips the checklist after success.

## Lane synthesis

**Where the sources agree.** (1) Errors are produced by design: Fitts and Jones (1947), Reason (1990), Dekker (2002) and Degani and Wiener (1993) all place the cause in cockpit, display, checklist and organization rather than in the individual. (2) Displays should match task structure and the mental model: Roscoe's pictorial realism and moving part, Wickens and Carswell's proximity compatibility, Hutchins's speed bugs turning memory into perception. (3) Automation without transparency is dangerous (Wiener and Curry 1980, Bainbridge 1983, Sarter and Woods 1995): the human needs to know what it is doing, why and what next, and must remain skilled. (4) Short, critical-item checklists in physical order, designed for use under interruption, work best (Degani and Wiener; Burian; Haynes). (5) Color carries fixed meaning (red warning, amber caution) and must be redundant with text or shape (FAA AC 25.1322-1; Degani 1992).

**Where they conflict.** Representational models of SA (Endsley) versus distributed cognition (Hutchins) and ecological critics (Flach, Dekker). Closeness: proximity helps integration but hurts focused reading (Wickens and Carswell). Checklists help, but implementation matters (Urbach 2014 against Haynes 2009). Swiss cheese linear causation versus systemic models. Typography: sans-serif and lowercase advice in Degani rests on older print evidence and is contested in the legibility lane.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Reserve red for real danger and amber for caution; use no other decoration in those hues; always pair color with text or an icon.
2. Code controls by shape and position, borrowing the real C172 knob shapes, and separate look-alike controls.
3. Build instrument widgets as real instruments: standard basic T layout, movement directions as in the real instrument, V-speed arcs and bugs as external memory.
4. Group display elements by the decision they serve; separate those used alone.
5. Mode and state are always visible, with a "what next" cue and no hidden state changes.
6. Checklist UI: current, done and outstanding steps visible; order by physical panel sweep; response stated; cautions before steps; clear end state.
7. Typography for documentation: restrained, mixed case for steps, capitals for short warnings, high contrast, generous leading.
8. Teach SA in three levels (see, understand, project) and add trend or prediction cues to widgets.
9. Teach hand-flying first and include automation-failure practice; keep the learner as the actor.
10. Non-blame feedback: treat learner error as evidence about the design and explain why the wrong choice was reasonable.

**Identity opportunity.** Aviation's own visual culture (checklists on kneeboards, speed cards, placards, basic T layout, bug markers, red-amber-green alert hierarchy, plain functional documentation) is a ready identity grammar for LTF that is credible to pilots and unlike generic SaaS.
