## Playfair1786
**Core claims.** William Playfair (1759-1823), a Scottish engineer and political economist, argued that representing quantities as geometric magnitudes lets the eye take in a series of numbers at once, and that this is faster and more memorable than reading a table. In The Commercial and Political Atlas (London, 1786) he drew the trade of England with other countries over time, and in doing so published the first bar chart and the first widely used line chart of economic time series. The pie (circle) chart and the circle-and-line comparison appeared later, in his Statistical Breviary (1801). His central design idea was that a chart should be a picture of a relation, not a decoration of a number: money on the vertical axis, time on the horizontal, with the shape of the line carrying the argument.

**Specifics.** The Atlas plots imports and exports for individual trading partners as two lines on a shared time axis, and the area between the lines is shaded in a different colour to show the balance of trade (one colour where exports exceed imports, another where imports exceed exports). This is an early use of the "gap between two series" device, so the viewer reads the balance as a shape rather than computing it. Charts sit on a fine grid with labelled axes, a title, and a prose explanation on the facing page, because Playfair expected that readers had never seen this kind of picture. He used bars where he had a single measurement per category and no continuous time series (his well-known bar chart of Scotland's trade by country is the standard example, and he said he drew bars because he lacked yearly data for Scotland). That choice anticipates the modern rule that discrete data suits bars and continuous change suits lines (see Zacks1999). He also engraved the charts on copper, so their lettering and line weight were controlled by the engraver, and he compared the graphic to a map of statistics, which is why he calls the book an atlas. The number of plates in the Atlas and exact dates of editions (1786, 1787, 1801) were not re-checked this session.

**Criticism and status.** Playfair's claims about speed and retention were assertions, not experiments; the first experimental support came almost two centuries later (see Cleveland1984). Some of his charts were also persuasive pieces with a political conclusion, an early example of how a well-drawn graphic can argue as much as inform, which later authors such as Tufte and Cairo treat as a question of graphical integrity. Historians credit him with invention, not with the later conventions of scale and labelling. He is the right citation for the origin of the forms, not for how to design them.

**Use for LTF.** (1) A learner's progress over time is a line, a comparison of categories (lessons, systems) is a bar; do not invent new forms for data that have a 240-year-old standard. (2) Shade the gap that matters, for example the difference between target and actual altitude, so the learner reads the error as an area rather than doing subtraction. (3) Every chart gets a title and one sentence of explanation, as Playfair knew his readers needed. (4) When data are missing for some categories or dates, choose bars over a line instead of interpolating, as he did.

## Cleveland1984
**Core claims.** William Cleveland and Robert McGill, statisticians at Bell Laboratories, proposed that a graph is read through "elementary perceptual tasks": the judgments a viewer performs to decode the encoded values. They argued that these tasks can be ordered by how accurately people carry them out, and that good graph design chooses encodings that use the more accurate tasks. The ordering they proposed, from most to least accurate: position along a common scale; position along identical, non-aligned scales; length, direction and angle; area; volume, curvature; shading and colour saturation. The paper has three parts: a theory of graphical perception, a set of experiments that tested parts of the theory, and an application, showing how common charts can be redesigned to use the better tasks (dot plots in place of pie charts and divided bars, grouped bar designs that put the compared values on a common baseline).

**Specifics.** The experiments asked people to judge what percentage one marked value was of another (a ratio judgment), on charts such as bar charts, pie charts and framed rectangles. Accuracy was scored as the log absolute error, log2(|judged percent - true percent| + 1/8), which stops very small errors from dominating. The position-angle experiment compared pie charts against bar charts and found that bar judgments, which use position along a common scale, were more accurate than pie judgments, which use angle. The position-length experiment compared five arrangements of bars (including adjacent bars, bars separated by other bars and a divided bar) and found that comparing values on a common baseline was more accurate than comparing values that are not aligned, and that segments in the middle of a stacked bar are harder to compare than segments sharing a baseline. The authors also reported systematic biases in angle judgments (the exact size of these effects is not confirmed here). The sample sizes of individual experiments are not confirmed in this session and should be taken from the paper before quoting. The paper's application section recommends the dot plot, which Cleveland developed further in his later books The Elements of Graphing Data (1985) and Visualizing Data (1993), where he also covers scales, labeling and the principle of making data stand out. The idea of banking a curve to about 45 degrees to make slopes comparable is not in this paper; it is from a separate 1988 paper by Cleveland, McGill and McGill on the shape of a two-variable graph, which I could not verify this session and have not added as a source.

**Criticism and status.** The ranking was replicated by Heer and Bostock (2010) with a much larger crowd sample, and it is the foundation of the channel rankings in Mackinlay (1986) and Munzner (2014). Criticisms: the tasks are ratio judgments of precise values, so the ranking says little about gist, trend or memory; some later work (for example Spence and Lewandowsky on pie charts, and Talbot, Setlur and Anand 2014 on bar charts) finds that angle and area judgments are not as poor as the ranking implies for part-to-whole judgments, and that the ordering depends on the task. Treat the ranking as strong for quantitative comparison and as a default, not as a prohibition.

**Use for LTF.** (1) For anything a learner must read precisely (airspeed against a target, altitude error, score against a pass mark) use position on a common scale, as a dot, line or aligned bar. (2) Avoid stacked bars and donut charts for comparing segments across rows. (3) Put compared values on one baseline and one scale. (4) Reserve colour intensity and area for impressions, never for exact values. (5) Print the number when exactness matters.

## Heer2010
**Core claims.** Jeffrey Heer and Michael Bostock tested whether graphical perception experiments can be run on Amazon Mechanical Turk with results comparable to laboratory studies. They replicated earlier experiments on spatial encoding (the Cleveland and McGill position, length and angle tasks) and on luminance contrast, and then ran new experiments on the perception of rectangular area (as used in treemaps and cartograms) and on chart size and gridline spacing. The paper received an honorable mention at CHI 2010 and appeared on pages 203-212 of the proceedings. The conclusion is that crowdsourced perception studies are viable, cheap and fast, and that they give new insight into design choices.

**Specifics.** Method: workers recruited through Mechanical Turk saw charts in the browser and made estimation judgments, typically what percentage a smaller marked element is of a larger one, as in Cleveland and McGill; accuracy was measured with a log error measure like the original. The authors compared their error rankings to the 1984 results and reported that the rank order of the encodings was similar, which supports both the original ranking and the crowdsourcing method. They also tested luminance contrast, comparing results against what would be expected for contrast sensitivity under uncontrolled monitors, to see whether crowd studies can survive display variation, and they report that they can, with caveats. The new experiments tested how accurately people judge the area of rectangles at several aspect ratios, how chart size affects the accuracy of simple charts, and how the spacing of gridlines affects value reading. The exact thresholds from those experiments (the smallest chart size that kept accuracy, the best gridline spacing, the error at each aspect ratio) are not confirmed in this session. Secondary summaries report large savings in time and cost against a lab study, but those figures come from the authors' own slides and are not confirmed here. Practical advice in the paper includes screening workers with check questions, being careful with payment and instructions, and detecting low-effort responses.

**Criticism and status.** Later work confirms the viability of online experiments but warns of display and demographic variability, and online graphical perception studies have since become standard (Harrison and colleagues, for example, ran large online studies on correlation perception). The area experiment tested rectangles only, so it does not tell us about circles or irregular shapes. The chart-size and gridline results concern simple charts and should not be generalised to dense multi-series charts.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Treat the position-length-angle-area ranking as an empirically robust rule for the design system. (2) Gridlines should be light and sparse, since the paper studies the tradeoff; check the paper for the figures before fixing a spacing. (3) For small-screen and compact progress strips, chart size was studied and small charts remained usable for simple data, which supports sparklines; again confirm numbers in the paper. (4) LTF can test its own widgets cheaply with the same remote method, rather than guessing. (5) For treemap-like skill maps, expect area comparisons to be imprecise, so print the values.

## Tufte1983
**Core claims.** Edward Tufte, a statistician and professor at Yale, wrote The Visual Display of Quantitative Information (Graphics Press, 1983; second edition 2001) as both a history and a theory. He defines graphical excellence as the well-designed presentation of interesting data, a matter of substance, of statistics and of design. It communicates complex ideas with clarity, precision and efficiency, gives the viewer the greatest number of ideas in the shortest time with the least ink in the smallest space, and nearly always involves several variables. Graphical integrity means telling the truth about the data. Part I covers graphical practice (history and examples), part II the theory: integrity, data-ink and chartjunk, multifunctioning elements, data density and small multiples, and aesthetics.

**Specifics.** Named ideas: the lie factor, the size of the effect shown in the graphic divided by the size of the effect in the data, which should be about 1 (the book gives examples where it is several times that; a tolerance band quoted in secondary sources was not confirmed against the book). Data-ink is the non-erasable core of the graphic; the data-ink ratio is data-ink divided by the total ink used, and the principles are to maximise it within reason, erase non-data-ink and redundant data-ink, and revise. Chartjunk is non-data ink and redundant decoration, including moire vibration, heavy grids and "ducks", graphics where the form overwhelms the data. Data density is the number of entries in the data matrix per unit area of the graphic, and Tufte favours high density. Small multiples are repeated frames of the same design, indexed by a variable, so that the viewer learns the design once and then compares. Other devices: the range frame (axis lines that show only the data range), the dot-dash plot, and the quartile plot. The worked examples include Minard's 1869 map of Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign, which carries about six variables (army size as band width, position on the map in two dimensions, direction of movement, the date, and temperature on the retreat). The five principles of graphical integrity include that the representation of numbers should be directly proportional to the numeric quantities, labelling should be clear and on the graphic, and that time series of money should be deflated. A frequently quoted line in the book is "Above all else show the data."

**Criticism and status.** The book is the most widely cited design statement in the field, but its rules were not tested in experiments at the time. Bateman and colleagues (2010) found that embellished charts were as accurately read and better remembered after a delay; Borkin and colleagues (2013) found that pictorial and distinctive visualizations are more memorable than minimal ones. Few and others have defended much of the clutter-reduction agenda on perceptual grounds, but not the maximal data-ink ratio as such. Tufte also argued from taste, and his dislike of ornament is partly an aesthetic stance. Some of his fiercest examples (the "ducks") are subjective.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Label lines and bars directly and drop the legend wherever the series count allows. (2) Strip chart frames, heavy gridlines and duplicated tick labels from widgets. (3) Use small multiples to compare flights, approaches or maneuvers on identical axes. (4) Keep bars at zero and size every encoding in proportion to the value. (5) Apply chartjunk rules to the data region only, and let the identity of LTF live in the frame, titles and type around it (see Bateman2010).

## Bertin1967
**Core claims.** Jacques Bertin (1918-2010), a French cartographer, published Semiologie graphique in 1967 (English translation by William J. Berg, University of Wisconsin Press, 1983) as a theory of graphics as a sign system. A graphic is monosemic: it is built so that, once its legend is known, it has one reading, unlike a picture or a poem. He distinguishes the graphic image, which is visible at a glance, from the data table that stands behind it. The image must reveal the principal groupings and the exceptions, and a good construction is the one that makes the structure visible in a single moment of perception.

**Specifics.** Bertin defined the "visual variables" that a mark can vary. The two dimensions of the plane (position) are the strongest; on top of the plane he defined six retinal variables: size, value (lightness), texture (grain), colour (hue), orientation and shape. For each variable he gave perceptual properties: whether it is associative (marks can be grouped despite variation), selective (marks can be singled out at a glance), ordered, and quantitative (differences can be read as ratios). In his scheme position and size carry all of these to varying degrees; value is selective and ordered but not directly quantitative; colour hue is selective and associative but not ordered; shape is only associative; texture is associative, selective and ordered. He also gave the number of steps a viewer can reliably distinguish for the variables that allow ordering, which are small (the specific counts are not confirmed here and should be taken from the text). Data are classified by level of organisation: nominal (qualitative), ordered, and quantitative. The matching rule is that a variable should be chosen whose properties are at least as strong as the level of the data, and that using a variable that is weaker than the data loses information while a stronger one implies information that is not there. His "image" concept led to the reorderable matrix: a table with rows and columns that can be permuted until the structure appears (he built physical card and strip versions), the ancestor of seriation, clustered heatmaps and many modern matrix views. He also separated three construction types: diagrams (correspondence between sets), networks (relations within a set), and maps (geographic order).

**Criticism and status.** Bertin's work was theoretical, built from cartographic experience rather than experiments, and some of his rankings were later revised. Cleveland and McGill (1984) and Mackinlay (1986) reordered the variables for quantitative data on the basis of accuracy experiments, putting position first, then length, and so on, and Mackinlay explicitly credits Bertin as the source of the framework. Bertin's translation is dense and his terminology idiosyncratic, so most designers know his ideas through Tufte, MacEachren, Munzner and the ggplot2 tradition. His rule that hue should not be used for ordered data remains standard.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Use hue only for nominal distinctions (a system, a phase of flight) and lightness or length for ordered or quantitative values (competence level, score). (2) Do not ask a learner to tell apart more than a handful of lightness or size steps; keep scales coarse. (3) The one-glance image is a good test for the progress overview: can the learner see where they stand without reading? (4) A skills-by-lessons matrix whose rows and columns can be sorted is a direct use of the reorderable matrix, useful to reveal gaps. (5) Choose each visual variable from the data level, and document the choice in the design system.

## Tukey1977
**Core claims.** John Tukey (1915-2000), statistician at Princeton and Bell Laboratories, argued in Exploratory Data Analysis (Addison-Wesley, 1977) that data analysis begins with looking: "detective work" with simple, hand-made displays and resistant summaries, before any confirmatory test. A picture's value is its power to show what one did not expect. He separated exploratory from confirmatory analysis and argued that statisticians had neglected the former. Methods should be robust to outliers, simple enough to do with pencil and paper, and organised around checking what is left after fitting a description to the data (residuals).

**Specifics.** Named techniques in the book: the stem-and-leaf display, which is a histogram that retains the digits of the data, so the shape and the values are both visible; the box-and-whisker plot (called a schematic plot), built on the five-number summary of the extremes, the median and the two hinges (roughly the quartiles); fences set at a multiple of the spread between the hinges (the 1.5 multiple is the commonly quoted convention, and is a rule of thumb, not a derived value) beyond which values are flagged as outside; resistant lines fitted by medians; median polish for two-way tables; and re-expression through a "ladder of powers" (reciprocal, log, square root, raw, square) to make distributions symmetric or relations straight. He taught plotting residuals against fitted values and against other variables, and tools for smoothing a series with running medians (the "3RSSH" and similar smoothers). The quotation commonly attributed to him, that the greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see, is from this book (the page number is not confirmed here). The book is written in a deliberately informal voice with invented terminology (hinges, hanging rootograms), which partly explains why much of it was never adopted while the box plot was.

**Criticism and status.** Tukey's exploratory outlook is mainstream, and the box plot is in every plotting library. Critics say that the box plot hides distribution shape (bimodality is invisible), and studies of lay viewers find that boxplots are misread without instruction; more recent "dot strip plus summary" displays address this. Exploratory work can also produce false discoveries without a confirmatory step, which is why later work stresses preregistration. The book assumes interactive analysis by an expert, not mass communication, and it predates computer graphics.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Show each learner's raw attempts as dots (for example the landing touchdown points relative to the aim point) with a median tick, rather than a mean alone. (2) Medians and ranges describe a few flights better than a mean and standard deviation. (3) Plot residuals, such as "how far from the target speed" for each approach, to show the pattern of error. (4) Avoid box plots for beginners; they need training to read. (5) Treat a single outlier flight honestly instead of letting it distort the summary.

## Mackinlay1986
**Core claims.** Jock Mackinlay's Stanford doctoral work, published in ACM Transactions on Graphics 5(2), 110-141 (April 1986), described APT (A Presentation Tool), a system that automatically designs static two-dimensional graphics (bar charts, scatter plots and connected graphs) for relational data. It treats a graphical presentation as a sentence in a graphical language and separates two criteria: expressiveness, meaning that the graphic expresses all the facts in the data and only those facts, and effectiveness, meaning that the graphic exploits the capabilities of the output medium and the human visual system as well as possible. A design is first checked for expressiveness, then the most effective one among the expressive designs is selected.

**Specifics.** The paper builds primitive graphical languages (for example horizontal and vertical axes, a bar language, a line language, a map language, a connected-line language), each with an expressiveness test, and a composition algebra that combines them (aligned axes, merged and double-axis combinations) into compound charts, with a search procedure that tries compositions and ranks them. A key example of an expressiveness failure: connecting bars of a nominal variable with a line implies an ordering and a trend that the data do not contain, so the language is inexpressive for that data. A related failure is using a graphical language that implies a functional relationship (a single value per x) for data that do not have it. For effectiveness, Mackinlay proposed a ranking of visual encodings per data type (quantitative, ordinal, nominal), extending Cleveland and McGill's ranking for quantitative data and borrowing from Bertin for the others. For quantitative data position is first, followed by length, angle and slope, area, volume, then density and colour saturation, with colour hue near the end. For ordinal data position again leads, then density, colour saturation, colour hue and texture. For nominal data position leads, then colour hue, texture and connection, with shape and containment among the middle choices, and length, angle and area last. The exact order of the middle entries in the ordinal and nominal lists is not confirmed here and should be copied from the paper's figure before quoting. The ranking was later adopted in Tableau's "Show Me" and by Munzner, whose "expressiveness and effectiveness" principles descend from this paper.

**Criticism and status.** The quantitative ranking was supported by experiments (Cleveland and McGill 1984; Heer and Bostock 2010), but the nominal and ordinal rankings are theoretical extrapolations that were not tested directly. APT also assumed a rather narrow set of chart types. Later work, such as Show Me and the Draco constraint system, builds on it, and recommendation engines are still sensitive to the choice of ranking. Expressiveness is a logical test and holds regardless of the empirical ordering.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Add an expressiveness check to chart review: does the chart imply a trend, ordering or total that the data lack? (2) Never connect separate lessons or categories with a line. (3) Encode ordered values by lightness or length, and nominal values by hue or position. (4) Codify the encoding priority in the design system as a short table, so new widgets choose from it. (5) When a chart type is missing from the system, derive it from a language and a composition rule, not from imitation.

## Shneiderman1996
**Core claims.** Ben Shneiderman, a human-computer interaction researcher at the University of Maryland, presented "The eyes have it: a task by data type taxonomy for information visualizations" at the IEEE Symposium on Visual Languages in 1996 (pages 336-343). The paper proposes the Visual Information Seeking Mantra, "overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand", and organises designs along two dimensions: seven data types and seven tasks. The aim is to help designers match interface capabilities to what a user wants to do with a collection.

**Specifics.** The seven data types are one-dimensional (linear, such as text or lists), two-dimensional (planar, such as maps), three-dimensional (such as molecules or buildings), temporal (items with start and end times), multi-dimensional (items with many attributes, handled as points in n-space), tree (hierarchies) and network (items linked to arbitrary others). The seven tasks are overview (gain an overview of the whole collection), zoom (zoom in on items of interest), filter (remove uninteresting items), details-on-demand (select an item or group and get details), relate (view relationships among items), history (keep a history of actions to support undo, replay and refinement) and extract (allow extraction of sub-collections and of the query parameters). The paper links these to the earlier dynamic-queries work of Shneiderman's group, in which sliders and toggles update the displayed set continuously with immediate feedback, and to direct manipulation. It reviews example systems (such as starfield displays and treemaps) to show how each type and task is supported, and notes that the three-dimensional and temporal types raise their own problems. The exact response-time figure often cited from the dynamic-queries literature (on the order of 100 milliseconds for continuous feedback) is not asserted in this paper as far as I can confirm, so it should be attributed to the response-time literature (see lane 24), not here.

**Criticism and status.** The paper is a taxonomy built from design experience, not an experiment. Craft and Cairns (2005) argued that the mantra lacks empirical support as a general model and that it fits some tasks and not others, and many modern interfaces start with a search or a personalised summary rather than a full overview. It remains the standard organising principle for exploratory interfaces, and Munzner restates it as a design principle. The mantra describes exploration of large collections, so for a short linear course it needs adaptation.

**Use for LTF.** (1) A progress dashboard follows the mantra literally: a course map as the overview, tap a module to zoom, tap a lesson for the details. (2) Filtering flights by maneuver, date or outcome should update instantly. (3) Keep a history of attempts, and let learners extract a summary to share or print. (4) Never hide the overview inside a drilled view; keep it one tap away. (5) For beginners, begin with a single recommended next step, then offer the overview, since the mantra assumes a user who knows what to look for.

## Zacks1999
**Core claims.** Jeff Zacks and Barbara Tversky, cognitive psychologists then at Stanford, published "Bars and lines: A study of graphic communication" in Memory & Cognition 27(6), 1073-1079 (1999). Their claim is that graph forms carry conventional meanings that viewers apply automatically. Bars are read as discrete comparisons between categories ("A is higher than B"), while lines are read as trends or functional relations ("as X goes up, Y goes up"). The mapping holds even when the data would make the other reading more natural, so a mismatch between form and data produces a misreading. The paper situates this in a broader view that graphs are communication systems whose formats have meaning, and that people draw on spatial and conceptual metaphors such as "up is more" and "a line connects things that are related".

**Specifics.** The paper reports several experiments in which participants either described graphs or chose graphs for a description. In the description studies, people shown a graph with a categorical x axis (for example, two groups) and a numerical y axis produced discrete comparisons when it was drawn with bars, and tended to produce trend statements when it was drawn with lines, even when the line connected only two categorical points. When a graph had an x axis that was continuous (for example, time or a numeric dose), lines led to trend descriptions and bars less often. In the construction or choice tasks, participants preferred bars for descriptions that compared two discrete values and lines for descriptions of trends, and the mismatched cases were rated as less appropriate. The paper also discusses the idea that a bar is an object (a "container" with a size) whereas a line is a path (a trajectory). Participant numbers and effect sizes for the individual experiments are not confirmed in this session and should be read from the paper. A later and separate study (Newman and Scholl 2012) found a "within-the-bar bias": viewers judge points inside a bar as more likely to belong to the distribution than points outside, even when the bar shows a mean; that is a different finding from this paper and is mentioned only as a related warning about bar charts.

**Criticism and status.** The finding has been echoed widely (reviews by Shah and Hoeffner 2002 and by Franconeri and colleagues 2021 cite it) and fits common design advice. It is a tendency, not a law: viewers with more graph literacy are less swayed by the format, and the experiments used short descriptions by students, which limits generalisation to complex dashboards or to non-US audiences. The paper does not show that one form is more accurate, only that it suggests a different reading.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Use lines for continuous series such as a flight trace or progress by day, and bars for comparisons among categories such as score by lesson. (2) Do not connect discrete, unrelated items with a line, because learners will read a trend into it. (3) Name the intended reading in the chart title ("Your approach speed was steadier this week"), so the form and the message agree. (4) Beginners may over-read lines as trends, so annotate with the actual change. (5) Where a series is both categorical and ordered (lessons in a fixed order), choose bars, and add a separate trend marker only if there is one.

## Bateman2010
**Core claims.** Scott Bateman and colleagues at the University of Saskatchewan ("Useful junk? The effects of visual embellishment on comprehension and memorability of charts", CHI 2010, pages 2573-2582) tested Tufte's claim that decoration in charts ("chartjunk") harms communication. Their result: charts with rich pictorial embellishment, of the kind used by the designer Nigel Holmes, were not read less accurately than plain versions of the same data, and they were remembered better after a delay of weeks. The authors do not recommend adding embellishment to every chart; they say the results suggest that visual imagery can help fix a chart in memory, which matters when long-term memorability is a goal.

**Specifics.** Design: the same data were drawn as a plain chart and as an embellished chart, using fourteen charts in a within-subject design in which each participant saw one version of each chart (this design and a sample of twenty university students come from a critical secondary description by Stephen Few, and were not confirmed against the paper). Participants answered comprehension questions about the charts shortly after viewing and were later asked to recall the charts. Reported findings (from summaries and the abstract): there was no significant difference in the accuracy of interpreting the main message between plain and embellished versions; there was no difference in recall after a short gap of a few minutes; after a delay that summaries describe as two to three weeks (one copy of the paper's introduction mentions more than 12 days; the exact interval is not confirmed here), recall of the topic, the trend and the categories was significantly better for the embellished charts. Participants also preferred the embellished charts and described them as more enjoyable and more memorable, and some noted that the embellishment helped them find the chart's topic. The analysis proposes that "chartjunk" should be assessed by what it contributes to a task (comprehension, memory, engagement), instead of declared harmful in general.

**Criticism and status.** The study is small, uses one style of embellishment (Holmes-like illustration, which is meaningful and tied to the topic, not random ornament), and has not been replicated at scale. Few's critique argues that the embellishments in the study were not the kind Tufte attacks, that the study measured memory of the chart topic rather than of the data, and that the data values may not have been recalled better. Borkin and colleagues (2013) found a consistent result in a much bigger memorability study, but also reported that memorability is not the same as comprehension. The honest reading is: decoration does not seem to hurt basic comprehension in this setting, and meaningful imagery may help memory.

**Use for LTF.** (1) A meaningful illustration tied to the topic (an aircraft silhouette beside the altitude chart) is defensible where long-term memory of a result matters, and it can carry LTF's identity. (2) Random ornament has no support from this study. (3) Keep embellishment away from the data marks themselves, so accuracy is protected. (4) Offer a plain view for exact reading, such as a table below the chart. (5) Test any embellished widget with a one-week recall check with learners, since the authors' own evidence is thin.

## Borkin2013
**Core claims.** Michelle Borkin, Aude Oliva, Hanspeter Pfister and colleagues (IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics 19(12), 2306-2315, from InfoVis 2013) asked what makes a visualization memorable. They found that observers agree with each other about which visualizations are memorable and which are forgettable, so memorability is a property of the visualization, not only of the viewer. Attributes that increase memorability include colour and human-recognizable objects such as pictograms and photographs; common graphs (bar charts, line graphs, scatterplots) are less memorable than unique types such as trees, networks and diagrams. The authors state that a memorable visualization is not necessarily a good one.

**Specifics.** The team assembled a large set of visualizations from news media, government reports, scientific journals and infographic sources (a Harvard press release gives a collection of more than 5,000), and labelled a set of 2,070 single-panel visualizations by type, attributes and source (the 2,070 figure is confirmed from the paper's description). They then ran memory experiments on Amazon Mechanical Turk using a repeat-detection game: workers saw a sequence of visualizations, each shown briefly (one second each, per the press release), and pressed a key when they saw one again. Memorability scores were based on the hit rate (correctly recognised repeats) and the false-alarm rate. The number of visualizations used in the memory experiment, the number of workers, and the size of the eye-tracking study (a figure of 33 participants sometimes quoted) are not confirmed in this session and should be read from the paper. Findings reported in the abstract and summaries: colour and human-recognizable objects increased memorability; common graphs were less memorable than unique types; visual density and a high data-ink ratio were associated with lower memorability, in contrast with Tufte; and in the eye-tracking part, observers fixated on titles and on pictorial elements, and the text of titles and captions helped later recall of the message. Source types differed, with infographics the most memorable and scientific figures the least (this ordering is from my memory of the paper and is not confirmed here).

**Criticism and status.** Memorability here means recognition after a very short exposure, not understanding, accuracy or learning, and the authors say so explicitly; a follow-up (Borkin and colleagues 2016) studied recall of the message and found that titles and text mattered. Confounds between chart type and source are possible, since infographics differ from scientific figures in many ways at once. The findings parallel Bateman 2010 and conflict with a strict reading of Tufte, but both sets of authors stress the difference between being memorable and being clear.

**Use for LTF.** (1) A distinctive, pictorial chart for the key progress moment ("your first solo landing") may be more memorable than a generic bar. (2) Keep generic bars and lines for routine data, and spend distinctiveness where memory matters. (3) A title that states the message does real work, since viewers look at it. (4) Add a human or aircraft object to the frame, not the data. (5) Do not mistake memorability for comprehension; test both.

## Few2006
**Core claims.** Stephen Few, a business-intelligence consultant and educator, defined a dashboard as a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives, consolidated and arranged on a single screen so the information can be monitored at a glance (Information Dashboard Design: The Effective Visual Communication of Data, O'Reilly, 2006; second edition from Analytics Press, 2013). The book argues that dashboards fail because designers spend space and attention on decoration, on gauges and on too many variants, rather than on communication. Good dashboard design is a matter of understanding perception, choosing the right display medium for each measure, and removing everything that does not inform.

**Specifics.** The book lists common design mistakes that include exceeding the boundaries of a single screen, supplying inadequate context for the measures, displaying excessive detail or precision, choosing a deficient measure, choosing inappropriate display media, introducing meaningless variety, using poorly designed display media, encoding quantitative data inaccurately, arranging the data poorly, highlighting important data ineffectively or not at all, cluttering with useless decoration, misusing or overusing colour, and designing an unattractive display (list reproduced from memory; the order and exact wording are not confirmed). Few advocates the bullet graph, a linear replacement for the radial gauge that he designed: a featured measure drawn as a bar, a comparative measure (the target) drawn as a short mark, and qualitative ranges (poor, satisfactory, good) shown as bands of graded grey or a single hue of different lightness. He argues that a bullet graph shows the same information as a gauge in much less space and uses length, which is read more accurately than angle. He also promotes sparklines (Tufte's word-sized trend lines) for context, small multiples, and sorted bar graphs. On colour: use muted colours for the bulk of the display and a saturated colour only for what needs attention; do not rely on red and green alone because of colour blindness (about 8 percent of men have a red-green deficiency, a figure from the colour-accessibility literature in lane 06, not from this book). He recommends arranging by importance, with the most significant measures at the top left, and grouping related measures with whitespace.

**Criticism and status.** The advice is practitioner knowledge, consistent with the perceptual ranking but not tested as a whole. Defenders of gauges point out that people read dials quickly because they match familiar instruments, and in aviation the round gauge is a convention with its own research base (lane 17). The advice is for dense business dashboards, not for learning. The bullet graph has been widely adopted, though user studies of it against gauges are limited and not reviewed here.

**Use for LTF.** (1) The learner dashboard fits one screen with three to five measures, each with context (target and past performance). (2) Use bullet graphs for "where you are relative to the standard." (3) Use sparklines for the trend beside each number. (4) Keep saturated colour for what needs attention and mute the rest, in both Day and Night themes. (5) LTF's cockpit instrument widgets are teaching objects that copy real dials and are a justified exception, but they should have a text readout beside them for accuracy and accessibility.

## Munzner2014
**Core claims.** Tamara Munzner (University of British Columbia) wrote Visualization Analysis and Design (A K Peters/CRC Press, 2014) as a textbook that organises the field around three questions: what data are shown, why the user is looking (the task), and how the visualization is built (the idiom). A design is analysed at four nested levels: the domain situation, the data and task abstraction, the visual encoding and interaction idiom, and the algorithm. Because each level feeds the next, a mistake at an outer level (solving the wrong problem) cannot be fixed by polishing an inner level, and each level has its own validation method and its own threats.

**Specifics.** The book's how-framework divides idioms into encoding (marks and channels), manipulating (select, navigate, change), facet (juxtapose, partition, superimpose), and reduce (filter, aggregate, embed). Marks (points, lines, areas) are the geometric items and channels (position, colour, size, shape, tilt, motion) control their appearance. Channels are divided into magnitude channels, which show how much, and identity channels, which show what or where. The effectiveness ranking of magnitude channels follows the Cleveland, McGill and Mackinlay tradition: position on a common scale, position on unaligned scales, length (one-dimensional size), tilt or angle, area (two-dimensional size), depth, luminance, saturation, curvature and volume (three-dimensional size). Identity channels are spatial region, colour hue, motion and shape. She names principles of channel use: expressiveness (the visual encoding should express all of, and only, the information in the dataset), effectiveness (more important attributes get more effective channels), the notion of "popout" (a single distinct item among distractors is found in parallel by a pre-attentive channel), discriminability (how many steps of a channel can be told apart), and separability (channels interact, so colour and size are not fully independent). On colour she recommends limiting categorical hue to a small number of bins (a few to about a dozen distinct hues) and avoiding the rainbow for ordered data. She also covers the "eyes beat memory" principle (showing views side by side beats switching between views, because viewers' working memory is limited), "resolution beats immersion" (avoid 3D unless it is essential), "overview first, zoom and filter, details on demand", "function first, form next", and response-time classes of roughly a tenth of a second for apparent immediacy, one second for fluid flow of thought and ten seconds for keeping attention, which she takes from the human-factors literature.

**Criticism and status.** This is a synthesis textbook, widely adopted in university courses. Its channel rankings rest on the experimental papers above, and its claims about nominal and ordinal rankings share the limits noted for Mackinlay. Critics note that the nested model is a validation framework, not a design recipe, and that the book focuses on data-analysis visualization rather than communication to the public.

**Use for LTF.** (1) State the task before choosing the chart: compare, locate, trend or summarise. (2) Use the nested model as a design-review checklist, asking first whether the dashboard solves the learner's actual problem. (3) Place comparisons side by side rather than behind tabs. (4) Keep widget interaction under a tenth of a second where it should feel immediate. (5) Treat 3D and rainbow colour as exceptions that need a reason.

## Wilkinson1999
**Core claims.** Leland Wilkinson, a statistician who built the SYSTAT and SPSS graphics systems, published The Grammar of Graphics (Springer, 1999; second edition 2005) to give statistical graphics a formal structure. His thesis is that charts are not a list of types (bar, pie, scatter) but compositions of a small number of independent components: data, the algebra that builds variables from the data, scales, statistics, geometry, coordinates, and aesthetics. A pie chart is, in this view, a stacked bar chart drawn in polar coordinates, and a bar chart and a histogram differ in the statistic applied before the geometry. This makes the space of possible charts a space of choices, and it allows a system to check whether a specification is meaningful.

**Specifics.** The book defines a pipeline in which variables are extracted from data, transformed by an algebra (with operators for crossing, nesting and blending variables, from which facets and small multiples follow), mapped through scales onto aesthetic attributes, and drawn by geometric elements (point, line, area, interval, polygon, schema) in a coordinate system (rectangular, polar, parallel, and transformations of them), with guides (axes and legends) added. Statistical transformations (binning, smoothing, summaries) belong to the specification, so a histogram is an interval element applied to binned counts. Hadley Wickham's layered grammar of graphics (2010, Journal of Computational and Graphical Statistics) adapted the idea for ggplot2: a plot is data, aesthetic mappings, layers of geometric objects with statistical transformations and position adjustments, scales, a coordinate system, and facets. Wilkinson's book also includes chapters on the perception issues of graphics and on the history of the form, and it argues for an object-oriented system. The grammar is the ancestor of ggplot2, Vega and Vega-Lite, Observable Plot and Tableau's visual specification.

**Criticism and status.** The grammar is a specification theory, not a perceptual theory: it tells you which charts can be built but not which are good, so it needs to be paired with Cleveland and Mackinlay on effectiveness. It is also complex, and the second edition is difficult reading. It has been extremely influential in practice, and its descendants are the working standard for declarative charts. Critics note that it fits statistical charts better than, for instance, cockpit dials or custom diagrams.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Specify each chart declaratively in content or code (data, mapping, geometry, scale, facet) so the design system controls encoding. (2) Facet by default for comparisons across flights or maneuvers. (3) Keep one scale for one quantity across the product so altitude always looks like altitude. (4) Build small, composable chart parts (scales, axes, marks) instead of one-off widgets. (5) For custom instrument widgets, state which parts are grammar-based charts and which are skeuomorphic.

## Franconeri2021
**Core claims.** Steven Franconeri, Lace Padilla, Priti Shah, Jeffrey Zacks and Jessica Hullman reviewed what is known about how people extract meaning from charts and what design choices follow (Psychological Science in the Public Interest 22(3), 110-161, December 2021, DOI 10.1177/15291006211051956). The review draws on research in visual perception, graph comprehension, uncertainty communication and health-risk communication. Its guidelines fall into three themes: align the graphic with the message so the intended comparison is the easy one, keep demands on working memory and attention low, and use or respect familiar conventions. A commentary by Jonathan Schwabish in the same issue proposes alternative practices.

**Specifics.** The review stresses that viewers extract global statistics (averages, trends, clusters, outliers) from a graph quickly and in parallel, but that comparing individual values or subsets is slow and serial, so a design should make the comparison it wants easy through grouping cues such as colour, proximity, connection and enclosure. How data are arranged (which variable is on the x axis and which is colour or panel) decides which comparisons are easy: values that are adjacent on the axis are compared most easily. Legends force a serial look-up, so direct labels help; working memory holds only a handful of objects, so a viewer cannot track many categories at once; bar length encodes the value, so a bar axis should start at zero, whereas line and dot charts need not; conventions (up means more, red means warm or stop, time runs left to right) speed reading and violations slow it; titles and annotations that state the takeaway change what viewers remember; text and graphics should work together; and graph literacy varies widely, so complex or unfamiliar charts need explanation. For uncertainty, static intervals and error bars are widely misread, and frequency formats and sample-based displays can help (see Hullman2015). The authors point out that "always start at zero" is not a universal rule and that dual-axis charts mislead.

**Criticism and status.** The review is the current consensus of its authors, who are active researchers, but it is a review of studies of uneven quality, and the authors flag open questions and context dependence; many cited experiments use college students and brief tasks. Some recommendations (such as the effect of titles on memory) rest on a small number of studies. Because it reviews many results, the paper is a good map for what to look up next rather than a final authority on any one claim.

**Use for LTF.** (1) State the takeaway in each chart title and point to the key mark. (2) Choose the grouping variable so that the comparison the learner needs is the adjacent one. (3) Label lines and bars directly, and keep highlighted categories to about three or four. (4) Zero baselines for bars; for a flight trace, scale the axis to what the learner needs and say so. (5) Assume low graph literacy at first, and add a "how to read this" line that fades as the learner gains experience.

## Hullman2015
**Core claims.** Jessica Hullman, Paul Resnick and Eytan Adar published "Hypothetical outcome plots outperform error bars and violin plots for inferences about reliability of variable ordering" in PLOS ONE 10(11), e0142444 (2015). They argue that static displays of uncertainty, such as error bars and violin plots, encourage viewers to summarise a distribution as a fixed range and make it hard to infer joint outcomes, such as how likely it is that one variable is larger than another. Hypothetical outcome plots (HOPs) animate a sequence of equally likely draws from the distribution, so the viewer builds a frequency-style sense of the uncertainty. In their experiments HOPs gave more accurate inferences for the tasks tested.

**Specifics.** The experiments were online studies with crowd workers in which participants judged, from a visualization of uncertain quantities, how reliably one variable would be ordered above another and related probabilities. Conditions compared HOPs with error bars and violin plots (and other static displays). The reported result in the title and abstract is that HOPs outperformed the static displays for the ordering inference. Sample sizes, frame rates (an animation of draws shown one after another at a rate of roughly a few per second) and the exact accuracy differences are not confirmed in this session. The paper sits in a wider literature in which people misread intervals as certain bounds (the deterministic construal error studied by Joslyn and LeClerc), in which frequency formats such as "3 out of 10" are better understood than probabilities, and in which quantile dot plots (Kay and colleagues 2016) improve decisions. HOPs show the uncertainty without requiring any statistical idiom.

**Criticism and status.** The tasks are narrow, and results for ordering inference do not guarantee gains for other tasks. Animation makes the display harder to print, archive and compare, and it conflicts with accessibility needs for reduced motion, so a static alternative is needed. A small number of draws can mislead, and HOPs take time to watch. Replications and extensions (Kale and colleagues, 2018, on animation versus static samples) found cases where static displays do as well; I did not verify those details in this session.

**Use for LTF.** (1) Show variability in a learner's landings or approaches as individual dots, not an error bar or a plus-minus. (2) Phrase uncertainty as counts ("about 7 of your last 10 landings were within the zone"). (3) Avoid animation-only displays; offer a static view and respect reduced-motion settings. (4) Explain a band or range in plain words next to the chart. (5) Be honest when the sample is small: say that three flights is too few to call a trend.

## Lane synthesis
**Where the sources agree.** The experimental line (Cleveland and McGill 1984; Mackinlay 1986; Heer and Bostock 2010; Munzner 2014; Franconeri and colleagues 2021) agrees on a hierarchy of accuracy for reading quantities: position on a common aligned scale beats length, which beats angle and slope, which beats area, with colour and volume last. Bertin's earlier theory is consistent with this for the properties of variables, and Few applies it to dashboards. All the sources agree on honest scales, labels placed on the marks, a stated message, and matching form to data: lines for continuous change and bars for discrete categories (Playfair, Zacks and Tversky).

**Where they conflict.** Tufte's data-ink and chartjunk rules conflict with Bateman and colleagues (2010) and Borkin and colleagues (2013), who found that pictorial embellishment does not reduce comprehension in their tasks and raises memorability. The resolution that fits both: keep the data region plain and precise, and put meaningful, topical imagery in the frame and titles, where it supports memory and identity. Few's dislike of gauges conflicts with the legitimacy of cockpit-style dial widgets; LTF's dials are teaching objects that mimic the real instrument, so they stay, with a numeric readout. Shneiderman's overview-first mantra is a heuristic and not well tested, so a beginner flow should lead with a next step.

**Ten actionable takeaways.**
1. Use aligned position (dots, lines, bars on a common baseline) for anything the learner must read precisely.
2. Avoid pie, donut, 3D and stacked bars for comparisons across rows.
3. Lines for continuous change, bars for categories, and never connect unrelated categories.
4. Bars start at zero; line and dot charts need not.
5. Label directly, skip legends, and highlight no more than three or four items.
6. Titles state the takeaway, with a one-sentence reading note under each chart.
7. Progress screens follow overview, then zoom and filter, then details, but lead beginners to a next step.
8. Dashboards fit one screen, give context (target, past), and use saturated colour only for exceptions.
9. Show raw attempts as dots, and express uncertainty as counts, with static alternatives to animation.
10. Decoration belongs in the frame, never on the data marks; this is where LTF's identity can enter charts.
