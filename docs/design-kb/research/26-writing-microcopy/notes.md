# Lane 26: Writing for interfaces and learners

## morkes1997concise

Morkes, J. and Nielsen, J. (1997). Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web. Nielsen Norman Group.

### Core claims
Web readers scan rather than read, so text written for paper performs poorly on screen. Three writing changes were tested against a deliberately boastful control: cutting the word count in half (concise), reformatting for scanning with lists, headings and bold keywords (scannable), and removing promotional exaggeration (objective). Each helped on its own, and the three combined helped most. The paper is one of the earliest controlled measurements of how writing style, rather than layout or navigation, changes usability.

### Specifics
The article reports three studies with 81 users in total (11, 19 and 51). Studies 1 and 2 were exploratory. In Study 1, 11 participants (6 end users, 5 technical users) did directed tasks on existing sites, and navigation problems dominated what they said. In Study 2, 19 participants (ages 21 to 59, all with at least five months of web experience) were sent to three of 18 preselected sites and thought aloud. Most of them (15) scanned before reading. They preferred concise, simple, informal writing, summaries and the inverted pyramid.

Study 3 was the measurement study. Fifty-one experienced web users, recruited by Sun, averaged about two years of web experience and ranged from 22 to 69 years old. Web professionals, writers and programmers were excluded so that the sample stood for "normal users". It used a five-condition between-subjects design on a seven-page site called "Travel Nebraska", with identical hypertext structure and essentially the same information in every version. Nebraska was chosen to limit prior knowledge. The versions were promotional (control, "marketese"), concise (about half the control's word count), scannable (bulleted lists, bold keywords, captions, shorter sections, more headings), objective (no exaggeration) and combined.

Measures were task time in seconds, task errors, memory (recognition and recall), time to draw the site map, site map accuracy, and subjective satisfaction on a 1 to 10 scale. Mean task time was 359 seconds for the control and 149 for the combined version. Mean task errors fell from 0.82 to 0.10. Satisfaction rose from 5.7 to 7.0 for the combined version, with the scannable version highest at 7.4. Normalized overall usability, with the control at 100, was 158 for concise, 147 for scannable, 127 for objective and 224 for combined. The authors present these as improvements of 58%, 47%, 27% and 124%. Site map accuracy showed no significant differences, and one hypothesis (about memory) was only partly confirmed. The article also cites a separate project in which rewritten Sun pages scored 159% higher than the originals (all figures from nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/).

### Replication and criticism
The study is a small-sample, between-subjects usability experiment (about 10 users per cell), reported in an industry venue and not peer reviewed. The "improvement" percentages are composites that Nielsen built by normalizing and averaging heterogeneous measures, so they are better read as direction and rough size than as precise effects. The site was one fictional travel site with fact-finding tasks, so the findings may not carry to explanatory or instructional writing, where a learner needs reasoning and not only a fact. The "objective" condition changed tone and content together, so the effect of tone alone is hard to separate. Later eyetracking work by the same group (see nielsen2006fshaped) and a wider literature on scanning agree on the direction, but there is no formal replication of these exact conditions that I could confirm. Treat the sizes as indicative.

### Implications for LTF
The study supports a house rule that lesson text should lead with the point and keep paragraphs to one idea. It also tells us what not to infer. LTF is not a fact-finding site, since learners must understand why a procedure works, so "halve the words" should not be applied by cutting explanation. Cut padding, hype and throat-clearing instead. The "objective" result argues against encouraging copy ("You'll be flying in no time!") in lesson and challenge text, and for plain statements of what the learner will do. In fly mode, where the learner glances between the sim and the page, the scannable treatment (short numbered steps, bold action words, one idea per line) is the right default, because that reader is scanning by necessity. For quizzes and challenge briefings, put the task in the first line. Where we want deliberate reading, such as a warning about a real procedure, do not format it as skimmable fragments that the learner will skim past.

## nielsen1997how

Nielsen, J. (1997). How Users Read on the Web. Nielsen Norman Group, 30 September 1997.

### Core claims
Nielsen's short alert summarizes the early Sun usability work for practitioners: people do not read web pages word by word, they scan. The article reports that 79% of test users always scanned any new page they encountered and only 16% read word by word. From that, he derives guidelines for writing: highlight keywords, use meaningful subheadings, use bulleted lists, keep one idea per paragraph, use the inverted pyramid, and write half the text or less. He also argues that credibility is something users look for and that promotional language lowers it.

### Specifics
The page gives the figures 79% scanned and 16% read word by word (nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/, fetched this session). It cites the improvements that came from the companion measurement study (morkes1997concise): about 58% for concise text, 47% for scannable layout, 27% for objective language and 124% for the combination. It lists eight guidelines: highlight keywords (hypertext links, typeface variations, color); write meaningful subheadings and not clever ones; use bulleted lists; keep one idea per paragraph, because users skip any second idea if the first words of the paragraph do not catch them; use the inverted pyramid, with the conclusion first; use half the word count of conventional writing or less; avoid "marketese"; and support credibility with good writing, graphics and outbound links. It notes that newsletters are read even more abruptly than web pages. The article is a summary, not a paper, and does not describe the sample behind the 79% and 16% figures beyond naming the study it draws on.

The article belongs to a wider discussion about whether reading online is different in kind from reading on paper. Nielsen's position is that it is, because screens are tiring and users are task driven. A reasonable qualification is that scanning is a strategy readers adopt when they have a task and the text is dense, not a fixed trait of web users.

### Replication and criticism
The 79 and 16 percent figures come from a small usability sample and have been quoted for decades without the sample size attached. The broader finding, that users scan when a page looks dense and they have a goal, is consistent with eyetracking results from the same group (nielsen2006fshaped) and with what Krug reported from observation (krug2014dont). Two limits matter. First, the sample was of mostly experienced web users in 1997, and screens, habits and typography have all changed. Second, scanning is not evidence that people comprehend by scanning. People in a learning situation may read closely precisely the sentences they decide are relevant. A fair summary is: expect scanning, make scanning productive, and do not assume it is the only mode.

### Implications for LTF
For LTF this is the evidence behind the heading and list conventions in lesson pages. Headings should say what the section tells you ("Raise the nose slowly in the flare"), not tease it. First words of bullets and headings should carry the information, because the eye lands on them first. For fly mode, assume the learner reads in glances: every step starts with the verb, one action per line. For a learner reading a lesson at a desk, the same rules still help but should not stop longer explanatory paragraphs where the logic of a maneuver needs them. Credibility matters for an aviation product: avoid hype, cite sources for facts, and link outward to the real references (FAA handbooks) where appropriate.

Confirmed this session: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/ (title, date, 79% and 16%, eight guidelines).

## nielsen2006fshaped

Nielsen, J. (2006). F-Shaped Pattern For Reading Web Content (Original Study). Nielsen Norman Group, 16 April 2006.

### Core claims
Eyetracking of 232 users reading thousands of web pages showed a dominant reading pattern shaped roughly like the letter F. Users read horizontally across the top of the content, then move down and read a shorter horizontal stretch, then scan down the left side. Nielsen concludes that users do not read text thoroughly, word by word, and that the first lines and the first words of each line draw most attention. The practical message is to put the most important information first, in the first two paragraphs and in the first words of headings, bullets and paragraphs.

### Specifics
The study is an eyetracking study with 232 users who looked at thousands of pages, with fairly consistent behavior across sites and tasks (nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/). Nielsen describes the pattern as three parts: a top horizontal bar, a shorter lower horizontal bar, and a vertical stem down the left edge that is sometimes slow and systematic and sometimes faster and spottier. Variants are an E-like shape with a third horizontal read and an inverted L with a single bar. Heatmap examples are an about-us article (the expected F), an e-commerce product page (the second bar sits lower because of the image, and users also look at price and the add to cart area), and a search results page (the second bar is longer than the first because its headline is longer). The stated implications are: users will not read word by word; the first two paragraphs must carry the most important information; subheads, paragraphs and bullets should start with information-carrying words because users read the first two words on a line much more than the third. An 11-year follow-up is said to confirm the findings.

A later article by Kara Pernice of the same group (2017, last reviewed in 2026) refines how the pattern should be read (nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/). It describes F-shaped reading as a default that appears when there are no strong cues guiding the eye, most likely when text is unformatted, users want to be efficient and they are not deeply committed to the content. It says the pattern persists on mobile in recent studies, with a mirrored version for right-to-left languages, that it is rarely a perfect F, and that it is "a bad sign" because users skip important content. It lists other scanning patterns (layer-cake, spotted, marking, bypassing and commitment). Its recommendations are to put the key points in the first two paragraphs, use visible headings starting with the most informative words, group related content, bold key phrases, use descriptive link text, use bullets and numbers and cut unnecessary content.

### Replication and criticism
This is eyetracking data on a large sample, but it is a vendor-reported study; the full data and the sampling of pages and tasks have never been released in the form a journal would require. The F-pattern was widely over-read as a layout law ("put things in an F"), and the group itself later said this is a misunderstanding. Its follow-up frames the F as the symptom of unformatted, low-commitment reading, which designers should counteract with structure. Academic eyetracking work on reading (see the legibility lanes) supports the idea that attention decays down a page when text is dense, but also shows that readers adapt their pattern to the task, so a single pattern cannot be assumed for instructional pages where learners are motivated. The study was of general web pages and not of learning materials, so effect on lesson pages is an inference.

### Implications for LTF
Use the F-pattern as a diagnostic, not a template. On a lesson page, if a learner is reading in an F, the writing has failed to give them cues. The remedies are the ones listed: put the rule or the action in the first line of each section, start list items with the verb or the key term ("Hold the nose level" and not "It is important to keep the nose level"), and use descriptive link text such as "Read the stall recovery lesson". In fly mode, the learner glances at the left edge of a step list, so the number and the first word of each step must be enough to find your place again after looking at the sim. Quiz questions should put the question in the first line and answer options should differ in their first words, not their last. Because the pattern is weaker when headings and visual groups give strong cues, LTF's tokens for heading weight and spacing do real work here and should be applied consistently in both themes.

Confirmed this session: read https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/ (232 users, three components, implications) and https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/ (Pernice follow-up, caveats).

## krug2014dont

Krug, S. (2014). Don't Make Me Think, Revisited: A Common Sense Approach to Web Usability, 3rd edition. Berkeley: New Riders.

### Core claims
Krug's short book is a craft treatise based on his consulting experience and informal testing. Its argument is that a page should be self-evident or at least self-explanatory, so the user does not have to spend effort working out what things mean. Chapter 2 sets out three "facts of life" about how people use the web: they scan rather than read, they satisfice (take the first reasonable option) rather than optimize, and they muddle through rather than work out how things work. Chapter 3 draws the design consequence, which the chapter subtitle states as "Designing for scanning, not reading". Chapter 5 is the writing chapter: "Omit needless words".

### Specifics
The third edition (copyright 2014, published by New Riders, with a December 2013 release date on the publisher's catalog) has 13 chapters: Don't make me think!; How we really use the Web; Billboard Design 101; Animal, Vegetable, or Mineral?; Omit needless words; Street signs and Breadcrumbs; The Big Bang Theory of Web Design; "The Farmer and the Cowman Should Be Friends"; Usability testing on 10 cents a day; Mobile; Usability as common courtesy; Accessibility and you; Guide for the perplexed (Pearson catalog page, fetched this session). The edition added a mobile chapter and wove mobile through the book.

For writing, chapter 5 opens, according to an O'Reilly preview described in search results, with what Krug calls the third law of usability: get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what is left. The chapter's two named targets are "happy talk" (welcome text and other content-free sociability that nobody reads) and instructions. The chapter heading "Instructions must die" expresses his view that, if a page needs instructions, the page should be fixed, and any instructions that remain should be cut to the minimum. The chapter acknowledges E. B. White's "omit needless words", so the advice has a direct line to the style tradition (see strunk2000elements). Chapter 3 says that if the audience will act as if reading billboards, design good billboards; the chapter contents cover conventions, visual hierarchy, and clear areas.

### Replication and criticism
This is expert opinion and observation, not experiment; the book's own answer to uncertainty is to test with a few users (see the chapter titled "Usability testing on 10 cents a day"). It fits the experimental findings that readers scan (morkes1997concise, nielsen2006fshaped). Critics point out that "omit half the words" is a slogan: removing words in explanatory material can remove the reasoning a beginner needs, and minimalist documentation research (carroll1990nurnberg) shows that what to cut is a matter of design, not a percentage. The book was written for web sites and shopping and information tasks, not for learning, where the user's goal is understanding, not escaping the page quickly.

### Implications for LTF
Use Krug as the plain-language case for cutting happy talk. Lesson intros that say "Welcome to Lesson 3! In this lesson you will..." are the thing he calls out; start with the first thing the learner does or learns. Do not apply the "instructions must die" line to flight procedures: these are the content, and a checklist is an instruction by nature. It does apply to the interface. If a widget needs a paragraph explaining how to operate it, redesign the widget; if a button needs a tooltip to be understood, rename it. The "satisficing" and "muddling through" findings argue for forgiving flows: a beginner will take the first plausible option, so labels must be unambiguous and default choices safe. The mobile and accessibility chapters support our use of the same short copy in compact fly mode.

## strunk2000elements

Strunk, W., Jr. and White, E. B. (2000). The Elements of Style, 4th edition. New York: Longman. (First published by Strunk privately in 1918; White's revised edition first appeared in 1959.)

### Core claims
The Elements of Style is a short prescriptive handbook of usage and composition. Its best-known principle is brevity, the heading "Omit needless words" (numbered 13 in the original list), which sits among eighteen numbered rules: eight on usage and punctuation and ten on composition. It is a craft treatise in the American classroom tradition, and it has been cited more than it has been tested. Its authority comes from long use and the clarity of its slogans, not from evidence about readers.

### Specifics
The original text, as transcribed on Wikisource (en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Elements_of_Style), lists these rules. Elementary rules of usage: form the possessive singular with 's; put a comma after each term in a series of three or more except the last; set off parenthetical expressions with commas; put a comma before "and", "or" or "but" introducing an independent clause; do not join independent clauses with only a comma; do not split a sentence into two; make a participial phrase at the start of a sentence modify the grammatical subject; divide words at line ends by formation and pronunciation. Elementary principles of composition: make each paragraph cover one topic (9); usually open a paragraph with a topic sentence (10); "Use the active voice" (11); state things positively (12); omit needless words (13); avoid strings of loose sentences (14); express parallel ideas in parallel form (15); keep related words together (16); keep to one tense in summaries (17); place the emphatic words at the end of the sentence (18). Later chapters of the book treat matters of form, words and expressions commonly misused, and in the White edition a chapter on approach to style. The fourth edition (a Pearson/Longman paperback, ISBN 020530902X, catalogued with a 2000 copyright and a July 1999 release) revises the original's gendered pronoun advice, according to reference accounts I fetched through web search.

For microcopy the most portable rules are 9 and 10 (one topic per paragraph, topic sentence first), 11 (active voice), 12 (positive form), 13 (cut needless words) and 15 (parallel form), which is the rule behind consistent button labels and list items.

### Replication and criticism
There is no experimental base for the rules. The most visible criticism is by the linguist Geoffrey Pullum ("Fifty Years of Stupid Grammar Advice", The Chronicle Review, 17 April 2009, and the longer essay "The Land of the Free and The Elements of Style" at pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/LandOfTheFree.html). He argues that the book's passive-voice advice is inconsistent: Strunk's example "My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me" sounds bad for information-structure reasons (a first-person agent is old information), not because it is passive; the fourth edition's examples of passive-to-active fixes do not all begin as passives; and Strunk's own first sentence, and many in White's introduction, are passive. He also says the "that is restrictive, which is non-restrictive" ruling is not supported by actual usage, and calls the book a "hopeless guide to English usage". A defender could answer that the authors say the passive is "frequently convenient and sometimes necessary" (as Pullum himself notes) and that the book's value is as a prompt to revise, not as a grammar. Both can be true: the conciseness and active-voice rules are sound as defaults for writing about actions, but the grammar claims should not be taken as rules of English.

### Implications for LTF
Use the book as a source of habits, not as a style authority to cite in disputes. The useful ones for LTF are: one topic per paragraph with the claim first; positive form (tell learners what to do, "Keep the wings level", before what not to do); parallel form for any list of steps; active voice for instructions and for error messages, where the passive hides who must act ("The form could not be saved" versus "We could not save your form"). Do not turn "active voice always" into a lint rule. Procedures and aircraft facts sometimes read best passively ("The flaps are retracted after..."), and the writer should choose by what the sentence needs to emphasize. Because the grammar rules are contested, any LTF style guide should be grounded in readers' needs, and cite plain language research (see redish2007letting, dubay2004principles) when a rule needs defending.

## orwell1946politics

Orwell, G. (1946). Politics and the English Language. Horizon, April 1946.

### Core claims
Orwell argues that the English of his day was in decline and that the decline was both a symptom and a cause of muddled political thinking. Foolish thoughts produce sloppy language, and sloppy language makes it easier to have foolish thoughts. His remedy is a set of habits for the writer: pick the word that names the thing, resist ready-made phrases, and revise by asking what you are trying to say. The essay is a craft and polemical essay by a major writer, not research, but it is the canonical short statement of the plain language ideal in English.

### Specifics
The essay identifies four devices that make writing vague (summary of the Orwell Foundation text, fetched this session): dying metaphors (worn-out figures that no longer evoke an image and are used because they spare the writer the work of finding fresh phrasing), operators or "verbal false limbs" (stock phrases that replace a simple verb, often with passive constructions and noun forms), pretentious diction (grand Latinate or foreign words that make ordinary statements sound more impartial), and meaningless words (abstract terms, especially in criticism and politics, that point to nothing definite). It then gives a list of questions a careful writer should ask of every sentence: What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is the image fresh enough to have an effect? Could I say it more shortly? Have I said anything avoidably ugly?

The six rules at the end, as quoted by the Orwell Foundation (orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/): "Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print." "Never use a long word where a short one will do." "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out." "Never use the passive where you can use the active." "Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent." "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." The sixth rule tells the reader that the rules serve meaning and are not an end in themselves.

### Replication and criticism
This is an essay in the opinion and craft tradition. The claim that English is in decline has been criticized by linguists as the recurring complaint of every generation, and the supposed link between plain style and honest thought is plausible but untested. Orwell is also open to the charge that he breaks his own rules (Pullum makes the same charge against Strunk and White, see strunk2000elements). What has held up is the practical advice: prefer short, concrete words, cut padding, and treat jargon as a cost the reader pays. The readability research of the next decade (flesch1948new) supplied measures that agree with this advice on word length and sentence length, while also showing that length is only a proxy (redish2000readability).

### Implications for LTF
Aviation is full of jargon and acronyms, and beginners meet them cold. Rule five is the right default: use the everyday word first and introduce the technical term with a gloss ("the flare, the last gentle raising of the nose before the wheels touch"). Rule one warns against the stock phrases that creep into training copy ("Keep your eyes outside the cockpit", "build good habits") when they have lost their meaning; replace them with the observation the instructor would actually make. The questions list is a good review checklist for lesson authors: What am I trying to say? Could I say it more shortly? The sixth rule matters for aviation, where precise phraseology (a radio call, a checklist wording) must not be "plained" away; an exact required wording outranks plainness, and LTF's rule that such wording needs a source applies. Pair every use of a technical term with the exact wording that the sim or real procedure uses, and flag unsourced items in the content pipeline.
