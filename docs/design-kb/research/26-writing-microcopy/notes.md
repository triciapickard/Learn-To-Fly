# Lane 26: Writing for interfaces and learners

## morkes1997concise

Morkes, J. and Nielsen, J. (1997). Concise, SCANNABLE, and Objective: How to Write for the Web. Nielsen Norman Group.

### Core claims
Web readers scan rather than read, so text written for paper performs poorly on screen. Three writing changes were tested against a deliberately boastful control: cutting the word count in half (concise), reformatting for scanning with lists, headings and bold keywords (scannable), and removing promotional exaggeration (objective). Each helped on its own, and the three combined helped most. The paper is one of the earliest controlled measurements of how writing style, rather than layout or navigation, changes usability.

### Specifics
The article reports three studies with 81 users in total (11, 19 and 51). Studies 1 and 2 were exploratory. In Study 1, 11 participants (6 end users, 5 technical users) did directed tasks on existing sites, and navigation problems dominated what they said. In Study 2, 19 participants (ages 21 to 59, all with at least five months of web experience) were sent to three of 18 preselected sites and thought aloud. Most of them (15) scanned before reading. They preferred concise, simple, informal writing, summaries and the inverted pyramid.

Study 3 was the measurement study. Fifty-one experienced web users, recruited by Sun, averaged about two years of web experience and ranged from 22 to 69 years old. Web professionals, writers and programmers were excluded so that the sample stood for "normal users". It used a five-condition between-subjects design on a seven-page site called "Travel Nebraska", with identical hypertext structure and essentially the same information in every version. Nebraska was chosen to limit prior knowledge. The versions were promotional (control, "marketese"), concise (about half the control's word count), scannable (bulleted lists, bold keywords, captions, shorter sections, more headings), objective (no exaggeration) and combined.

Measures were task time in seconds, task errors, memory (recognition and recall), time to draw the site map, site map accuracy, and subjective satisfaction on a 1 to 10 scale. Mean task time was 359 seconds for the control and 149 for the combined version. Mean task errors fell from 0.82 to 0.10. Satisfaction rose from 5.7 to 7.0 for the combined version, with the scannable version highest at 7.4. Normalized overall usability, with the control at 100, was 158 for concise, 147 for scannable, 127 for objective and 224 for combined. The authors present these as improvements of 58%, 47%, 27% and 124%. Site map accuracy showed no significant differences, and one hypothesis (about memory) was only partly confirmed. The article also cites a separate project in which rewritten Sun pages scored 159% higher than the originals (all figures from nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/).

### Replication and criticism
The study is a small-sample, between-subjects usability experiment (about 10 users per cell), reported in an industry venue and not peer reviewed. The "improvement" percentages are composites that Nielsen built by normalizing and averaging heterogeneous measures, so they are better read as direction and rough size than as precise effects. The site was one fictional travel site with fact-finding tasks, so the findings may not carry to explanatory or instructional writing, where a learner needs reasoning and not only a fact. The "objective" condition changed tone and content together, so the effect of tone alone is hard to separate. Later eyetracking work by the same group (see nielsen2006fshaped) and a wider literature on scanning agree on the direction, but there is no formal replication of these exact conditions that I could confirm. Treat the sizes as indicative.

### Implications for LTF
The study supports a house rule that lesson text should lead with the point and keep paragraphs to one idea. It also tells us what not to infer. LTF is not a fact-finding site, since learners must understand why a procedure works, so "halve the words" should not be applied by cutting explanation. Cut padding, hype and throat-clearing instead. The "objective" result argues against encouraging copy ("You'll be flying in no time!") in lesson and challenge text, and for plain statements of what the learner will do. In fly mode, where the learner glances between the sim and the page, the scannable treatment (short numbered steps, bold action words, one idea per line) is the right default, because that reader is scanning by necessity. For quizzes and challenge briefings, put the task in the first line. Where we want deliberate reading, such as a warning about a real procedure, do not format it as skimmable fragments that the learner will skim past.

## nielsen1997how

Nielsen, J. (1997). How Users Read on the Web. Nielsen Norman Group, 30 September 1997.

### Core claims
Nielsen's short alert summarizes the early Sun usability work for practitioners: people do not read web pages word by word, they scan. The article reports that 79% of test users always scanned any new page they encountered and only 16% read word by word. From that, he derives guidelines for writing: highlight keywords, use meaningful subheadings, use bulleted lists, keep one idea per paragraph, use the inverted pyramid, and write half the text or less. He also argues that credibility is something users look for and that promotional language lowers it.

### Specifics
The page gives the figures 79% scanned and 16% read word by word (nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/, fetched this session). It cites the improvements that came from the companion measurement study (morkes1997concise): about 58% for concise text, 47% for scannable layout, 27% for objective language and 124% for the combination. It lists eight guidelines: highlight keywords (hypertext links, typeface variations, color); write meaningful subheadings and not clever ones; use bulleted lists; keep one idea per paragraph, because users skip any second idea if the first words of the paragraph do not catch them; use the inverted pyramid, with the conclusion first; use half the word count of conventional writing or less; avoid "marketese"; and support credibility with good writing, graphics and outbound links. It notes that newsletters are read even more abruptly than web pages. The article is a summary, not a paper, and does not describe the sample behind the 79% and 16% figures beyond naming the study it draws on.

The article belongs to a wider discussion about whether reading online is different in kind from reading on paper. Nielsen's position is that it is, because screens are tiring and users are task driven. A reasonable qualification is that scanning is a strategy readers adopt when they have a task and the text is dense, not a fixed trait of web users.

### Replication and criticism
The 79 and 16 percent figures come from a small usability sample and have been quoted for decades without the sample size attached. The broader finding, that users scan when a page looks dense and they have a goal, is consistent with eyetracking results from the same group (nielsen2006fshaped) and with what Krug reported from observation (krug2014dont). Two limits matter. First, the sample was of mostly experienced web users in 1997, and screens, habits and typography have all changed. Second, scanning is not evidence that people comprehend by scanning. People in a learning situation may read closely precisely the sentences they decide are relevant. A fair summary is: expect scanning, make scanning productive, and do not assume it is the only mode.

### Implications for LTF
For LTF this is the evidence behind the heading and list conventions in lesson pages. Headings should say what the section tells you ("Raise the nose slowly in the flare"), not tease it. First words of bullets and headings should carry the information, because the eye lands on them first. For fly mode, assume the learner reads in glances: every step starts with the verb, one action per line. For a learner reading a lesson at a desk, the same rules still help but should not stop longer explanatory paragraphs where the logic of a maneuver needs them. Credibility matters for an aviation product: avoid hype, cite sources for facts, and link outward to the real references (FAA handbooks) where appropriate.

Confirmed this session: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/ (title, date, 79% and 16%, eight guidelines).

## nielsen2006fshaped

Nielsen, J. (2006). F-Shaped Pattern For Reading Web Content (Original Study). Nielsen Norman Group, 16 April 2006.

### Core claims
Eyetracking of 232 users reading thousands of web pages showed a dominant reading pattern shaped roughly like the letter F. Users read horizontally across the top of the content, then move down and read a shorter horizontal stretch, then scan down the left side. Nielsen concludes that users do not read text thoroughly, word by word, and that the first lines and the first words of each line draw most attention. The practical message is to put the most important information first, in the first two paragraphs and in the first words of headings, bullets and paragraphs.

### Specifics
The study is an eyetracking study with 232 users who looked at thousands of pages, with fairly consistent behavior across sites and tasks (nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/). Nielsen describes the pattern as three parts: a top horizontal bar, a shorter lower horizontal bar, and a vertical stem down the left edge that is sometimes slow and systematic and sometimes faster and spottier. Variants are an E-like shape with a third horizontal read and an inverted L with a single bar. Heatmap examples are an about-us article (the expected F), an e-commerce product page (the second bar sits lower because of the image, and users also look at price and the add to cart area), and a search results page (the second bar is longer than the first because its headline is longer). The stated implications are: users will not read word by word; the first two paragraphs must carry the most important information; subheads, paragraphs and bullets should start with information-carrying words because users read the first two words on a line much more than the third. An 11-year follow-up is said to confirm the findings.

A later article by Kara Pernice of the same group (2017, last reviewed in 2026) refines how the pattern should be read (nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/). It describes F-shaped reading as a default that appears when there are no strong cues guiding the eye, most likely when text is unformatted, users want to be efficient and they are not deeply committed to the content. It says the pattern persists on mobile in recent studies, with a mirrored version for right-to-left languages, that it is rarely a perfect F, and that it is "a bad sign" because users skip important content. It lists other scanning patterns (layer-cake, spotted, marking, bypassing and commitment). Its recommendations are to put the key points in the first two paragraphs, use visible headings starting with the most informative words, group related content, bold key phrases, use descriptive link text, use bullets and numbers and cut unnecessary content.

### Replication and criticism
This is eyetracking data on a large sample, but it is a vendor-reported study; the full data and the sampling of pages and tasks have never been released in the form a journal would require. The F-pattern was widely over-read as a layout law ("put things in an F"), and the group itself later said this is a misunderstanding. Its follow-up frames the F as the symptom of unformatted, low-commitment reading, which designers should counteract with structure. Academic eyetracking work on reading (see the legibility lanes) supports the idea that attention decays down a page when text is dense, but also shows that readers adapt their pattern to the task, so a single pattern cannot be assumed for instructional pages where learners are motivated. The study was of general web pages and not of learning materials, so effect on lesson pages is an inference.

### Implications for LTF
Use the F-pattern as a diagnostic, not a template. On a lesson page, if a learner is reading in an F, the writing has failed to give them cues. The remedies are the ones listed: put the rule or the action in the first line of each section, start list items with the verb or the key term ("Hold the nose level" and not "It is important to keep the nose level"), and use descriptive link text such as "Read the stall recovery lesson". In fly mode, the learner glances at the left edge of a step list, so the number and the first word of each step must be enough to find your place again after looking at the sim. Quiz questions should put the question in the first line and answer options should differ in their first words, not their last. Because the pattern is weaker when headings and visual groups give strong cues, LTF's tokens for heading weight and spacing do real work here and should be applied consistently in both themes.

Confirmed this session: read https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/ (232 users, three components, implications) and https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/ (Pernice follow-up, caveats).

## krug2014dont

Krug, S. (2014). Don't Make Me Think, Revisited: A Common Sense Approach to Web Usability, 3rd edition. Berkeley: New Riders.

### Core claims
Krug's short book is a craft treatise based on his consulting experience and informal testing. Its argument is that a page should be self-evident or at least self-explanatory, so the user does not have to spend effort working out what things mean. Chapter 2 sets out three "facts of life" about how people use the web: they scan rather than read, they satisfice (take the first reasonable option) rather than optimize, and they muddle through rather than work out how things work. Chapter 3 draws the design consequence, which the chapter subtitle states as "Designing for scanning, not reading". Chapter 5 is the writing chapter: "Omit needless words".

### Specifics
The third edition (copyright 2014, published by New Riders, with a December 2013 release date on the publisher's catalog) has 13 chapters: Don't make me think!; How we really use the Web; Billboard Design 101; Animal, Vegetable, or Mineral?; Omit needless words; Street signs and Breadcrumbs; The Big Bang Theory of Web Design; "The Farmer and the Cowman Should Be Friends"; Usability testing on 10 cents a day; Mobile; Usability as common courtesy; Accessibility and you; Guide for the perplexed (Pearson catalog page, fetched this session). The edition added a mobile chapter and wove mobile through the book.

For writing, chapter 5 opens, according to an O'Reilly preview described in search results, with what Krug calls the third law of usability: get rid of half the words on each page, then get rid of half of what is left. The chapter's two named targets are "happy talk" (welcome text and other content-free sociability that nobody reads) and instructions. The chapter heading "Instructions must die" expresses his view that, if a page needs instructions, the page should be fixed, and any instructions that remain should be cut to the minimum. The chapter acknowledges E. B. White's "omit needless words", so the advice has a direct line to the style tradition (see strunk2000elements). Chapter 3 says that if the audience will act as if reading billboards, design good billboards; the chapter contents cover conventions, visual hierarchy, and clear areas.

### Replication and criticism
This is expert opinion and observation, not experiment; the book's own answer to uncertainty is to test with a few users (see the chapter titled "Usability testing on 10 cents a day"). It fits the experimental findings that readers scan (morkes1997concise, nielsen2006fshaped). Critics point out that "omit half the words" is a slogan: removing words in explanatory material can remove the reasoning a beginner needs, and minimalist documentation research (carroll1990nurnberg) shows that what to cut is a matter of design, not a percentage. The book was written for web sites and shopping and information tasks, not for learning, where the user's goal is understanding, not escaping the page quickly.

### Implications for LTF
Use Krug as the plain-language case for cutting happy talk. Lesson intros that say "Welcome to Lesson 3! In this lesson you will..." are the thing he calls out; start with the first thing the learner does or learns. Do not apply the "instructions must die" line to flight procedures: these are the content, and a checklist is an instruction by nature. It does apply to the interface. If a widget needs a paragraph explaining how to operate it, redesign the widget; if a button needs a tooltip to be understood, rename it. The "satisficing" and "muddling through" findings argue for forgiving flows: a beginner will take the first plausible option, so labels must be unambiguous and default choices safe. The mobile and accessibility chapters support our use of the same short copy in compact fly mode.

## strunk2000elements

Strunk, W., Jr. and White, E. B. (2000). The Elements of Style, 4th edition. New York: Longman. (First published by Strunk privately in 1918; White's revised edition first appeared in 1959.)

### Core claims
The Elements of Style is a short prescriptive handbook of usage and composition. Its best-known principle is brevity, the heading "Omit needless words" (numbered 13 in the original list), which sits among eighteen numbered rules: eight on usage and punctuation and ten on composition. It is a craft treatise in the American classroom tradition, and it has been cited more than it has been tested. Its authority comes from long use and the clarity of its slogans, not from evidence about readers.

### Specifics
The original text, as transcribed on Wikisource (en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Elements_of_Style), lists these rules. Elementary rules of usage: form the possessive singular with 's; put a comma after each term in a series of three or more except the last; set off parenthetical expressions with commas; put a comma before "and", "or" or "but" introducing an independent clause; do not join independent clauses with only a comma; do not split a sentence into two; make a participial phrase at the start of a sentence modify the grammatical subject; divide words at line ends by formation and pronunciation. Elementary principles of composition: make each paragraph cover one topic (9); usually open a paragraph with a topic sentence (10); "Use the active voice" (11); state things positively (12); omit needless words (13); avoid strings of loose sentences (14); express parallel ideas in parallel form (15); keep related words together (16); keep to one tense in summaries (17); place the emphatic words at the end of the sentence (18). Later chapters of the book treat matters of form, words and expressions commonly misused, and in the White edition a chapter on approach to style. The fourth edition (a Pearson/Longman paperback, ISBN 020530902X, catalogued with a 2000 copyright and a July 1999 release) revises the original's gendered pronoun advice, according to reference accounts I fetched through web search.

For microcopy the most portable rules are 9 and 10 (one topic per paragraph, topic sentence first), 11 (active voice), 12 (positive form), 13 (cut needless words) and 15 (parallel form), which is the rule behind consistent button labels and list items.

### Replication and criticism
There is no experimental base for the rules. The most visible criticism is by the linguist Geoffrey Pullum ("Fifty Years of Stupid Grammar Advice", The Chronicle Review, 17 April 2009, and the longer essay "The Land of the Free and The Elements of Style" at pullum.ppls.ed.ac.uk/LandOfTheFree.html). He argues that the book's passive-voice advice is inconsistent: Strunk's example "My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me" sounds bad for information-structure reasons (a first-person agent is old information), not because it is passive; the fourth edition's examples of passive-to-active fixes do not all begin as passives; and Strunk's own first sentence, and many in White's introduction, are passive. He also says the "that is restrictive, which is non-restrictive" ruling is not supported by actual usage, and calls the book a "hopeless guide to English usage". A defender could answer that the authors say the passive is "frequently convenient and sometimes necessary" (as Pullum himself notes) and that the book's value is as a prompt to revise, not as a grammar. Both can be true: the conciseness and active-voice rules are sound as defaults for writing about actions, but the grammar claims should not be taken as rules of English.

### Implications for LTF
Use the book as a source of habits, not as a style authority to cite in disputes. The useful ones for LTF are: one topic per paragraph with the claim first; positive form (tell learners what to do, "Keep the wings level", before what not to do); parallel form for any list of steps; active voice for instructions and for error messages, where the passive hides who must act ("The form could not be saved" versus "We could not save your form"). Do not turn "active voice always" into a lint rule. Procedures and aircraft facts sometimes read best passively ("The flaps are retracted after..."), and the writer should choose by what the sentence needs to emphasize. Because the grammar rules are contested, any LTF style guide should be grounded in readers' needs, and cite plain language research (see redish2012letting, dubay2004principles) when a rule needs defending.

## orwell1946politics

Orwell, G. (1946). Politics and the English Language. Horizon, April 1946.

### Core claims
Orwell argues that the English of his day was in decline and that the decline was both a symptom and a cause of muddled political thinking. Foolish thoughts produce sloppy language, and sloppy language makes it easier to have foolish thoughts. His remedy is a set of habits for the writer: pick the word that names the thing, resist ready-made phrases, and revise by asking what you are trying to say. The essay is a craft and polemical essay by a major writer, not research, but it is the canonical short statement of the plain language ideal in English.

### Specifics
The essay identifies four devices that make writing vague (summary of the Orwell Foundation text, fetched this session): dying metaphors (worn-out figures that no longer evoke an image and are used because they spare the writer the work of finding fresh phrasing), operators or "verbal false limbs" (stock phrases that replace a simple verb, often with passive constructions and noun forms), pretentious diction (grand Latinate or foreign words that make ordinary statements sound more impartial), and meaningless words (abstract terms, especially in criticism and politics, that point to nothing definite). It then gives a list of questions a careful writer should ask of every sentence: What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is the image fresh enough to have an effect? Could I say it more shortly? Have I said anything avoidably ugly?

The six rules at the end, as quoted by the Orwell Foundation (orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/): "Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print." "Never use a long word where a short one will do." "If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out." "Never use the passive where you can use the active." "Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent." "Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." The sixth rule tells the reader that the rules serve meaning and are not an end in themselves.

### Replication and criticism
This is an essay in the opinion and craft tradition. The claim that English is in decline has been criticized by linguists as the recurring complaint of every generation, and the supposed link between plain style and honest thought is plausible but untested. Orwell is also open to the charge that he breaks his own rules (Pullum makes the same charge against Strunk and White, see strunk2000elements). What has held up is the practical advice: prefer short, concrete words, cut padding, and treat jargon as a cost the reader pays. The readability research of the next decade (flesch1948new) supplied measures that agree with this advice on word length and sentence length, while also showing that length is only a proxy (redish2000readability).

### Implications for LTF
Aviation is full of jargon and acronyms, and beginners meet them cold. Rule five is the right default: use the everyday word first and introduce the technical term with a gloss ("the flare, the last gentle raising of the nose before the wheels touch"). Rule one warns against the stock phrases that creep into training copy ("Keep your eyes outside the cockpit", "build good habits") when they have lost their meaning; replace them with the observation the instructor would actually make. The questions list is a good review checklist for lesson authors: What am I trying to say? Could I say it more shortly? The sixth rule matters for aviation, where precise phraseology (a radio call, a checklist wording) must not be "plained" away; an exact required wording outranks plainness, and LTF's rule that such wording needs a source applies. Pair every use of a technical term with the exact wording that the sim or real procedure uses, and flag unsourced items in the content pipeline.

Volume, issue and page numbers appear only in a Wikipedia entry flagged for needing citations, so they are left out of the reference and of sources.json.

## flesch1948new

Flesch, R. (1948). A new readability yardstick. Journal of Applied Psychology, 32(3), 221-233. doi:10.1037/h0057532.

### Core claims
Flesch proposed a formula that predicts how hard a passage is to read from two countable features: average sentence length and average word length in syllables. Its output is a "reading ease" score on a scale where higher numbers mean easier text. It replaced earlier formulas that depended on vocabulary lists (such as Dale-Chall), because syllables and sentence lengths can be counted by clerks without word lists. The paper is the origin of the Flesch Reading Ease score now included in word processors. It is a methodological paper in applied psychology, validated against existing graded reading material, not an experiment on readers of web or instructional text.

### Specifics
Crossref and PubMed confirm the record: Journal of Applied Psychology, volume 32, issue 3, pages 221 to 233, 1948 (api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/h0057532; pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18867058, which lists no abstract). I could not retrieve the full text this session, so the details below come from the 1975 Navy report that reprints the formula (kincaid1975derivation), and from secondary accounts fetched through web search.

The formula as printed in the Navy report is Reading Ease = 206.835 minus 1.015 times words per sentence minus 0.836 times syllables per 100 words. (A common restatement in secondary sources writes the syllable term as 84.6 times syllables per word; the two are the same quantity expressed per word or per 100 words, with the report's coefficient printed in a slightly different form, so quote the form from a source you hold when precision matters.) Higher scores mean easier text. The report notes that a conversion table in Flesch (1948) maps reading ease to school grade level and that it is nearly linear between grades 8 and 16. Secondary accounts say the score is bounded in practice from 0 to 100, with 90 to 100 "very easy" and the lowest band "very confusing", that Flesch worked from 100-word samples so the method could be applied to books, and that he found short sentences and short words to be the best contributors to ease after analyzing newspapers and magazines. Treat these band labels as a convention of later software, not as findings of the paper.

### Replication and criticism
The formula has been validated many times against comprehension and cloze tests, and DuBay (dubay2004principles) reports that surface-feature formulas remain the best simple predictors of text difficulty on comprehension tests, with Flesch and Dale-Chall among the more valid. But three limits are well documented. First, the formula measures only surface features: it cannot see organization, content, coherence, layout or the reader's prior knowledge. Second, different formulas give different grade levels for the same text, because they were calibrated to different criteria (DuBay gives an example in which the scores for four paragraphs ranged from 8.9 to 12.3), and software counts of syllables differ. Third, rewriting to hit a score can make text worse, since the formula rewards breaking sentences and shortening words without regard for whether the connections between ideas survive (redish2000readability). The Navy study (kincaid1975derivation) re-derived the formula for a specific population of readers and a specific kind of text, which shows the constants are not universal.

### Implications for LTF
Treat the Flesch score as a smoke alarm for lesson text, not as a target. It is cheap to run in the content pipeline and can flag a paragraph whose score is far lower than the rest, which usually means long sentences stacked with technical terms. It should never be a pass or fail gate, for three reasons. Aviation vocabulary raises the syllable count of necessary words ("airspeed indicator", "altimeter setting") and a score would punish exactly the terms learners must learn. Short, fragmented sentences can score well while being incoherent. And checklists, radio calls and numeric limits are not prose, so the formula is meaningless for them. If LTF reports a number at all, report grade level ranges per lesson to authors and not to learners, and always review flagged passages by reading and by testing with real beginners.

Flesch's own paper was not read.

## kincaid1975derivation

Kincaid, J. P., Fishburne, R. P., Jr., Rogers, R. L., and Chissom, B. S. (1975). Derivation of New Readability Formulas (Automated Readability Index, Fog Count and Flesch Reading Ease Formula) for Navy Enlisted Personnel. Research Branch Report 8-75. Millington, TN: Naval Technical Training Command. doi:10.21236/ada006655.

### Core claims
The report recalculated three existing readability formulas on a single data set of Navy trainees, so that they would predict the grade level at which Navy personnel could actually comprehend Navy training manuals, and so that the three formulas would be directly comparable. The grade-level version of the Flesch formula that came out of it is the "Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level" used in word processors today. The authors state that readability formulas are only accurate to about one grade level, and warn against mechanical use.

### Specifics
Participants: 569 trainees at four technical schools at two Navy bases (Great Lakes Naval Training Center and Naval Air Station Memphis) were tested, and 38 were dropped for failing to follow instructions, illness or missing aptitude scores, leaving 531. They were mostly new enlistees with less than six months in the Navy and mostly recent high school graduates. Each took the comprehension section of the Gates-MacGinitie reading test, which gave their reading grade level, and were tested on 18 passages (3,067 words in all, mean length 170 words) from Navy Rate Training Manuals using a cloze procedure in which every fifth word was deleted and the trainee filled it in. Multiple regression of the passages' scaled grade levels on text features produced the new formulas (kincaid report, read in full this session as a PDF).

Table 3 of the report gives, for the Flesch family, the old Reading Ease formula (206.835 minus 1.015 times words per sentence minus 0.836 times syllables per 100 words) and the new grade-level formula: grade level = 0.39 (words per sentence) + 11.8 (syllables per word) - 15.59, and a simplified version 0.4 (words per sentence) + 12 (syllables per word) - 16. For the Automated Readability Index the old formula was 0.50 (words per sentence) + 4.71 (characters per word) - 21.43 and the recalculated one 0.37 (words per sentence) + 5.84 (characters per word) - 26.01. The Fog Count was recalculated by changing a constant. The authors report that all new formulas predict grade levels about one to one and a half grades below the originals on the test passages, and that the original Fog Count overestimated difficulty by about 1.5 grade levels. The report remarks that the original Flesch formula carries constants to three decimal places and that this extra precision adds nothing. It states that readability formulas "are only accurate to within one grade level", so an error of 0.1 of a grade from simplifying constants is trivial (paraphrasing the report, p. 19).

### Replication and criticism
The formula was derived on a narrow sample (new Navy enlistees, mostly recent high school graduates, reading technical training manuals), which limits generalization. It has nonetheless been widely adopted and validated against other criteria. The criticism is aimed at its use: the Flesch-Kincaid grade level is now often applied to web copy, consent forms, patient information, and error messages, populations and genres it was not built for, and the number gets read as a precise property of the text. Redish (redish2000readability) and others argue that this misleads; DuBay (dubay2004principles) argues that formulas remain useful predictors if used with other methods. The report itself is cautious, which is a helpful corrective to later use.

### Implications for LTF
If LTF's authoring checks include a grade level, document that it is a rough screen with a one-grade error band and that the constants come from a study of Navy trainees reading technical manuals, which is not far from LTF's adult technical learners but is not the same group. Do not display a grade level to learners, and do not set a pass threshold in `content:validate`. A sensible use is to compare lessons with each other: if one lesson is three grades above its neighbors, find out why. Also note that the report measured comprehension with cloze tests of running prose; the same method cannot be run on quiz stems, checklists, or micro-labels, where the formula's inputs (sentence length, syllables) do not describe the difficulty a beginner experiences, which is mostly about unfamiliar terms.

## redish2000readability

Redish, J. (2000). Readability formulas have even more limitations than Klare discusses. ACM Journal of Computer Documentation, 24(3), 132-137. doi:10.1145/344599.344637.

### Core claims
Redish argues, in a commentary on George Klare's review of readability measurement, that readability formulas are a poor way to judge or improve documents for real readers. Reviewing the literature, she finds technical weaknesses and stresses that direct usability testing with typical readers tells writers what they need to know. The formulas were built for children's school texts and not for adult technical documentation, ignore differences between readers, ignore the effects of content, layout and retrieval aids, and emphasize what can be counted over the subtler features that drive comprehension (summary of the abstract as listed in the JCD bibliography, ftp.math.utah.edu/pub/tex/bib/idx/jcd/24/3/132_137.html). It is the second of four commentaries on Klare's reprint.

### Specifics
This is a six-page commentary, so the argument is made by review and example, not new data. I could not retrieve the full text this session; the points that follow come from the bibliographic abstract and from DuBay's account of Redish's work (dubay2004principles), and should be read as that. The main objections are as follows. The formulas count words, syllables and sentences, and so they cannot say whether a text is organized the way readers need, whether it answers readers' questions, or whether it uses the readers' own words. The abstract's list of ignored factors includes layout and retrieval aids, which is where lists, tables, headings and step sequences do their work. Because the formulas ignore differences between readers, they can describe at most an average for a population and not the actual readers of a given document (this last step is my reading of the abstract, not a quotation). They also invite "writing to the formula", producing short choppy sentences or avoiding necessary technical terms, which can make texts harder to use. Her broader program, set out in the book Letting Go of the Words (redish2012letting) and in earlier work with Selzer, is that writers should test with representative users who do real tasks, using think-aloud protocols and performance measures, and should revise on that basis.

The 1981 paper by Redish in IEEE Transactions on Professional Communication, "Understanding the limitations of readability formulas" (Crossref lists it as vol. PC-24, issue 1, pp. 46-48), shows the same critique had been made two decades earlier.

### Replication and criticism
The position is an expert argument, not an experiment. DuBay (dubay2004principles) answers it directly: he agrees that formulas use only surface features but argues that these variables remain the best predictors of comprehension difficulty in the literature, that critics offer little or no evidence that "writing to the formula" is widespread, and that usability testing cannot by itself tell you whether a failure comes from the text or from something else. He also points to the Duffy and Kabance study (1981), which found that simplifying only words and sentences in Navy training passages helped lower-ability readers on some tasks (a 13% improvement in one experiment) and not on others, and argues that its rewrites were disjointed. The most defensible reading of the debate is that both sides agree on the main thing: formulas are an estimate and should be combined with testing.

### Implications for LTF
This is the main reason for not using a readability formula as a quality gate in the content pipeline. For a flight-training app, the real questions about a lesson page are: Can the learner find the next action? Does the sentence use the term the sim uses? Can they do the task after reading? These are questions for task-based testing with beginners, even if only five people, with a think-aloud protocol and a screen recording. The practical rule is to give every new lesson a small test with people who have not flown, and to record where they hesitate. Where a formula flags a paragraph, treat the flag as a prompt to read it aloud, not as a number to fix.

## redish2012letting

Redish, J. (2012). Letting Go of the Words: Writing Web Content that Works, 2nd edition. Waltham, MA: Morgan Kaufmann (Elsevier). First edition 2007.

### Core claims
Redish, a usability specialist and plain language practitioner, argues that web content works when it is written as one side of a conversation with a particular reader who has come to do something. Content should answer the questions that reader brings, in the reader's own words, and be organized so that the answer is easy to find. The book's recurring advice is to be concise and goal-oriented. A reviewer quotes the line "The key to successful writing on the web is to let go of the words without losing the essential messages" (page 98 of the first edition, per idratherbewriting.com's review; I could not check it against the book). The book is a practitioner's craft guide that draws on usability testing, information architecture, typography and copywriting. It is not a research report, although it sits on the author's earlier research on readability formulas and on testing documents with users (redish2000readability).

### Specifics
The publisher's description of the second edition (shop.elsevier.com, 15 August 2012) says it adds new material on content strategy, search engine optimization and social media, many new and updated examples, and more attention to tablets and smartphones. The topics listed on the publisher's page include planning content, designing for easy use, home pages, navigation, organizing information, focusing on key messages, headlines and headings, press releases, sentence-level writing, lists and tables, legal text, links, illustrations, the drafting and review process, style guides and usability testing. A second-edition chapter list from the booksite lists Chapter 1 "Content!", Chapter 2 "Planning: Purposes, Personas, Conversations" and a chapter on "Getting There: Pathway Pages" (page 5), according to search results; I did not retrieve the whole table of contents.

According to a detailed review of the first edition (idratherbewriting.com, 2011), the techniques include: think of the web as a conversation and anticipate the questions readers bring (the reviewer notes this does not mean every page must look like an FAQ); define users first, since you cannot know the questions people will ask without knowing who they are and in what context they come; address pain points so that content stays focused on why people came; lead with the most important questions using an inverted pyramid; and be concise and goal-oriented. The review also lists chapters on lists, tables, illustrations, links, headings, paragraphs and white space, and mentions one formatting point from the list chapter: wrapped text should align under the text, not under the bullet or number. The book's second-edition chapter titles ("Planning: Purposes, Personas, Conversations") and publisher topics (drafting and review process, style guides, usability testing) show that planning for a named reader and testing with real users frame the writing advice.

### Replication and criticism
This is a craft book with no experimental claims of its own. Where it relies on research, the evidence is the usability and eyetracking literature on scanning (morkes1997concise, nielsen2006fshaped) and Redish's own critique of readability formulas. The reviewer cited above judges the "web as conversation" model the strongest and most novel idea, but also says it gets lost as the book expands into a general web writing guide, that seasoned web writers may find the book too introductory, and that the first edition missed social media (the second edition addresses it). A deeper limit is that the "conversation" metaphor fits task-oriented informational content, such as help, services and product information. It fits learning content less well, where the reader does not arrive with a question; a beginner does not know what to ask. In that setting a teacher has to raise the questions the learner will need, and the metaphor needs adapting.

### Implications for LTF
Redish's method gives LTF a way to write lesson and reference pages. First, name the reader and the task for every page: "a beginner who has just finished the first sim flight and wants to hold altitude" and not "pilots". Second, consider writing headings as the question or action the reader would use ("How do I hold altitude?" or "Hold altitude with small power changes", where a procedure is the answer), so that a learner finding their way from a search result or a back-link can see at once where they are. Third, test: with five beginners per major lesson, sit them with a task and watch where they pause. Fourth, apply her list and table advice to quizzes and checklists. The caution is that "answer the reader's question" must not become an excuse to hide what a beginner does not know to ask; LTF's lesson order should still teach foundations first, and the conversation framing belongs in reference pages, glossary and challenge briefings, where the learner does arrive with a question.

## shneiderman1982designing

Shneiderman, B. (1982). Designing computer system messages. Communications of the ACM, 25(9), 610-611. doi:10.1145/358628.358639.

### Core claims
Novice users meet a computer system mainly as the messages on its screen, and poorly worded messages are a main cause of the negative image of computers. Shneiderman reports that five controlled experiments with revised messages improved users' performance and satisfaction, and from them he draws five recommendations for system developers and a one-figure summary of message design guidelines. The article is a two-page "Pracniques" (practitioner) piece, a short report of experimental results plus guidelines, in the early human-computer interaction literature, and a root of most later advice on error messages.

### Specifics
The opening gives examples of what novices face: violent messages such as "FATAL ERROR, RUN ABORTED", vague phrases such as "ILLEGAL CMD", and obscure codes such as "OC7" or "IEH2191", which leave them "shaken, confused, dismayed, and discouraged from continuing" (quoted from the article's first paragraph, read in full this session). The experiments, which are reported in a longer chapter the article cites, were these. Undergraduate novices repairing Cobol statements with modified compiler syntax error messages: messages with increased specificity produced 28 percent better repair scores. Users of a text editor whose only error message was a question mark made 10.7 errors, and 6.1 errors when they switched to an editor with brief messages. Students corrected 4.1 of 10 erroneous text editor commands with the standard system messages and 7.5 of 10 with improved ones. In a study of job control language error messages, students given messages from two popular contemporary systems scored 2.9 and 3.8 out of 6, while students given improved messages scored 4.8. Subjective preferences also favored the improved messages. The article does not report sample sizes or statistical tests.

The five recommendations are: increase attention to message design (consult copy writers or editors on wording); establish quality control (approval by a committee, with changes monitored and recorded); develop guidelines; carry out an acceptance test with the target user community; and collect user performance data, such as the frequency of each error condition and an error rate per thousand commands as a metric of system quality. Figure 1 summarizes the guidelines. Messages should not be wordy, negative in tone, critical of errors, general or cryptic, or suggest system control over the user. They should be brief, positive, constructive, specific and comprehensible, and emphasize user control over the system. Other considerations: upper and lower case preferred to uppercase only, asterisks only in special circumstances, error numbers (if needed at all) at the end of the message, a user-modifiable message file, two or more levels of messages, and reduced use of terms like "ILLEGAL", "INVALID", and "ERROR". The text gives model rewrites: instead of "ILLEGAL PASSWORD", "Your password did not match the stored password. Please try again."; instead of "INVALID DATA" in an inventory application, "Dress sizes range from 5 to 16."; instead of "INCORRECT COMMAND", "Permissible commands are: SAVE, LOAD, or EXPLAIN." The text adds that writing good messages "requires experience, practice, and a sensitivity to how the reader will react" and, like writing poems or essays, is a skill that can be refined.

### Replication and criticism
The experiments were small and early, and they used programming and editing tasks with students and command-line systems from the 1970s. Later work on enhanced compiler error messages for novice programmers is mixed. Becker (2016, SIGCSE) is reported to have found fewer errors with enhanced messages, while Pettit, Homer and Gee (2017) published a study titled "Do Enhanced Compiler Error Messages Help Students? Results Inconclusive" and a think-aloud study of 31 students found no substantial learning gain over a control, though students did read the enhanced messages and mostly acted on them (all from search-result snippets on core.ac.uk, not read in full). So the 1982 effects should not be assumed to carry over to every setting. The core guidance (be specific, say what to do, avoid blame, avoid codes) has nonetheless been repeated in later guidelines such as Nielsen's usability heuristic on helping users recognize and recover from errors (a different lane covers that). Some of the figure's items are dated: tone guidance that told designers to avoid "ERROR" altogether has been softened in modern practice, and the "two levels of messages" idea became expandable detail and help links. The article gives no inferential statistics, so the effect sizes should not be treated as precise. It is also a general computing context; the effect on learners of a modern web app's microcopy has not, to my knowledge, been measured in the same terms.

### Implications for LTF
This is the best-evidenced anchor for LTF's error and validation messages. For a quiz, a form, a sim connection check or a challenge result, write messages that say what happened in the learner's terms, what to do next, and give no blame ("That doesn't match yet. Check the altitude you read from the altimeter" rather than "Wrong answer" or "Invalid input"). The model rewrites translate directly: "Altitude must be 0 to 12,500 feet MSL" in place of "Invalid altitude". Keep codes out of the learner's view and put them at the end or in a details panel for support. Make the acceptance-test recommendation concrete: before releasing a challenge, run the failure messages past two or three beginners and ask "what would you do next?" Record how often each failure message is shown, and treat a frequent one as a defect in the lesson or the task and not only in the wording. In fly mode, where a learner is half attending, keep messages to one short line plus one action.

## carroll1987minimal

Carroll, J. M., Smith-Kerker, P. L., Ford, J. R., and Mazur-Rimetz, S. A. (1987). The Minimal Manual. Human-Computer Interaction, 3(2), 123-153. doi:10.1207/s15327051hci0302_2.

### Core claims
Learners of computer systems are active: they want to start on real tasks, they skip reading, they make errors and they improvise. Standard self-instruction manuals fight those habits with step-by-step drills, long introductions and previews. The authors built a "minimal manual" that accepts the habits, with less text, real tasks from the start, explicit support for error recognition and recovery, and a structure that helps learners coordinate the manual with the system. In two experiments with word-processing novices it was learned from faster and produced better task performance than a commercial manual. The paper is the empirical core of the "minimalist instruction" approach that Carroll later set out in his book The Nurnberg Funnel (1990), the standard source for writing procedures and help for software learners.

### Specifics
The authors set out a four-part minimalist training model: focus on real tasks and activities; slash the verbiage; support error recognition and recovery; and guide exploration. The motivating observation is that learners "want to do something, not learn how to do everything" (a learner's words, quoted in the paper), and that new users are using a tool and not learning for its own sake. Their earlier work on guided exploration had replaced a commercial manual with cards containing about an eighth as much text, each procedure broken into goals, enabling hints, checkpoints and results, and error recoveries; those learners spent less than half the time learning, spent less than a third as much time reading, and made half as many errors.

For the Minimal Manual, the authors cut the commercial training manual to 45 pages, less than a quarter of its length, by removing repetition, previews, reviews, practice exercises, the index, the "welcome to word processing" introduction and the troubleshooting appendix. Chapters averaged less than three pages, and the learner reached a first document within seven pages. Headings used the learner's goals, and topic chapters included open-ended exercises.

Experiment 1 was a between-subjects comparison of the Minimal Manual (MM) and a standard self-instruction manual (SS) with 19 novice subjects (10 in MM), run in groups of two or three in a simulated office. The MM group needed 40 percent less learning time, t(17) = 3.06, p < .01, accomplished 2.7 times as many performance subtasks, t(16) = 3.63, p < .01, and was more than twice as efficient on the basic tasks (accomplishment divided by time). On basic tasks alone the MM advantage in performance was 50 percent, t(16) = 2.11, p = .05. Experiment 2 was a 2 x 2 design (manual by instructions "learn while doing" or "learn by the book") with 32 office workers who had little computer experience, eight per cell. MM learners completed 58 percent more subtasks, F(1, 28) = 5.31, p < .05, and achieved 93 percent more per unit time on tasks 1 to 3, F(1, 28) = 6.13, p < .05. They started the system up almost twice as fast and spent 29 percent less time reading the manual (nonsignificant). They made 20 percent fewer errors, spent 10 percent less time recovering, and used the recommended recovery methods 60 percent more often, though these error differences were not statistically significant (all figures read in the full text this session).

### Replication and criticism
I did not retrieve later replications this session, so I cannot state whether the effects held in other settings or how large they were. Limits that appear in the paper itself are small samples (19 and 32), a 1980s word processor, and a commercial comparison manual that differed from the Minimal Manual in many ways at once, so the paper cannot say which design feature (shorter text, task focus, error recovery) caused the gain. Some error measures did not reach significance. Carroll himself has said (in the edutechwiki summary) that there is no deductive theory of minimalist instruction, so design depends on iterative testing.

### Implications for LTF
This is the most directly relevant empirical support for LTF's lesson design. Four consequences follow. First, lessons should put the learner on a real action (fly the pattern, hold the altitude) as early as possible and put explanation next to the action and not before it; previews and recap sections are what the authors removed. Second, every procedure in fly mode should carry its checkpoint (what should you see on the screen now) and its recovery (what to do if it did not happen), so the learner can coordinate the page with the sim, which is exactly the authors' "coordinate attention between the system and the manual" problem. Third, plan for errors: assume the learner will skip a step, press the wrong key or lose their place, and write recoveries. Fourth, delete welcome sections, "how to use this lesson" chapters and troubleshooting appendices that sit away from the step they concern. The caution is that flying involves concepts (lift, trim, the effect of controls) that cannot be learned only by doing a click-through; keep concept text short and tied to a task, but do not remove it.

## dubay2004principles

DuBay, W. H. (2004). The Principles of Readability. Costa Mesa, CA: Impact Information. ERIC document ED490073.

### Core claims
DuBay's review is a long, single-author synthesis of a century of research on readability, written in defense of the classic formulas against criticism from technical communicators. It has three parts: the history of adult literacy studies in the United States, the classic readability studies before 1950 (vocabulary lists and the first formulas), and the newer studies of the last fifty years. The ERIC abstract says "the principles of readability are in every style manual" and "readability formulas are in every writing aid", but "what is missing is the research and theory on which they stand". His main conclusions are that the surface features the formulas count (word difficulty and sentence length) remain the best simple predictors of how difficult a text is for readers, that critics are right that formulas cannot see content and organization, that usability testing alone cannot replace them, and that the formulas should be used with other methods.

### Specifics
The paper's contents are: an introduction on guidelines and the controversy; adult literacy studies (military surveys, civilian surveys, and challenges for technical communicators); the classic readability studies (Sherman's statistics on literature, Thorndike's word-frequency lists, the classic formulas of Dale and Tyler, Gray and Leary, Flesch, Dale-Chall and others); and the new readability studies (the cloze test, reader variables such as prior knowledge, interest and motivation, text leveling, and the new formulas of the Bormuth era, Kintsch's work on coherence, and formula applications). The full text was read this session (ERIC full text, 30 pages).

Material that matters for writers: Gray and Leary's 1935 study, "What makes a book readable", sorted the causes of difficulty into four categories (content, style, format and features of organization) and concentrated on 80 style variables, of which average sentence length had a correlation of -0.52 with difficulty, with "percentage of easy words" at 0.52 (DuBay's list). The cloze test, introduced by Wilson Taylor in 1953, deletes every fifth word and counts the words readers restore; DuBay reports that a cloze score of 35 percent or more is the level for assisted reading (his Table 7 compares cloze and multiple-choice scores). Bormuth's studies used the whole enrollment of students in grades 4 through 8 of one school district (675 students) with 20 passages, and, in DuBay's account, showed that the correlations between the formula variables and comprehension do not change with the reader's reading ability. DuBay also reports that the different formulas can disagree: for one set of four paragraphs he lists grade levels from 8.9 (Flesch) to 12.3 (Fog), and explains that formulas were developed with different criterion scores (for example 75 percent correct for Flesch and 50 percent for Dale-Chall) and that software counts syllables differently.

On the criticism, he discusses the Duffy and Kabance study (1981), in which 1,169 male Navy trainees aged 17 to 20 read passages whose words and sentences had been simplified, and the effect on comprehension was inconsistent: three experiments showed no significant improvement and the fourth showed a 13 percent gain only for the low-ability group on the vocabulary-simplified text. He argues that the rewritten passages in that study read as disjointed, which may explain the inconsistent results.

### Replication and criticism
This is a review with an advocate's position. It is useful for the history and for pointers to hundreds of studies, but its treatment of critics is partisan: he says critics "offer little or no evidence" of misuse of the formulas, while the critics he names (Redish, Selzer, Schriver and others) argue that the formulas distract writers from the organization and content problems that matter most. For that side, see redish2000readability. On usability testing, he says it and reading protocols leave problems hard to locate and can be expensive to repeat, while Redish argues for exactly those methods. The studies he cites on formula validity are mostly of school children and of military and adult basic education populations, so generalization to web and software copy is an inference. His claim that the formulas have survived "80 years of intensive application, investigation, and controversy" is a statement of popularity as much as of validity.

### Implications for LTF
DuBay gives LTF two things. First, the history is a reminder that the evidence for readability formulas is real but narrow: the formulas predict difficulty of continuous prose across a range of texts, and they have little to say about a single paragraph or a checklist line. Second, it sets a sound audience principle: the more critical the information is for safety, the greater the need for readability (his summary of the lessons for technical communicators, which applies directly to a flight training product). Practically, LTF should write explanatory prose at a level that a reader with a modest education can follow on the first reading, and should treat a formula result as one of several checks along with a read-aloud test and a beginner test. The cloze test is also a cheap method that LTF could adopt for pre-release checks of key passages: remove every fifth word from a paragraph, ask several non-pilots to fill it in, and see which sentences fail. This tests whether the text is predictable and understandable to people who lack the background, and it does so without any formula.

## plain2011federal

Plain Language Action and Information Network (PLAIN). (2011). Federal Plain Language Guidelines, March 2011, Revision 1, May 2011. Washington, DC: U.S. federal government.

### Core claims
The guidelines are the practical handbook of the U.S. federal plain language movement. They were first written in the mid-1990s by a community of federal employees and revised every few years. The goal, in their own words, is that users can find what they need, understand what they find and use it to meet their needs. They divide the work into five topics: think about your audience, organize, write your document (words, sentences, paragraphs and other aids), write for the web, and test. The guidelines are professional consensus built from legal writing and document design sources, each section ending with a list of sources, not original experiments, but they are the most specific written standard of plain language practice that a U.S. reader will meet. The Plain Writing Act of 2010 allowed agencies to follow these guidelines until the Office of Management and Budget issued its own (see uscongress2010plain).

### Specifics
The first rule is to write for your audience: use language the audience knows and is comfortable with, and take their current knowledge into account. The text calls it a myth that plain language means "dumbing down", and says to write for an eighth grade class only if the audience is one. It lists questions to answer before writing: who is my audience, what do they know, what do they need to know, what questions will they have, and what is the best outcome for the agency and for the audience.

The word-level guidance (Section III) includes using the active voice, using the simplest form of a verb, avoiding hidden verbs (nominalizations), using "must" for requirements and dropping "shall" (must for an obligation, must not for a prohibition, may for discretion, should for a recommendation), using contractions when they sound natural, using pronouns to speak directly to readers ("More than any other single technique, using 'you' pulls users into your document and makes it relevant to them"), minimizing abbreviations, using short simple words, omitting unnecessary words, using the same term consistently for a specific thought or object, avoiding legal, foreign and technical jargon, and not using slashes. Sentence guidance includes writing short sentences (express only one idea in each sentence, and break "if" conditions into separate sentences while keeping the logical relationship), keeping subject, verb and object close together, avoiding double negatives, and placing the main idea before exceptions and conditions. Paragraph guidance covers a topic sentence, transition words, short paragraphs and one topic per paragraph. Other aids are examples, lists, tables, illustrations, emphasis, and minimizing cross-references. A worked example rewrites a regulation on dropping objects from an aircraft, turning a long passive sentence about a "pilot in command of a civil aircraft" into a second-person instruction.

The testing section names three methods. Paraphrase testing uses individual interviews, suited to short documents and web pages: the guidelines advise 6 to 9 interviews per document, ask the participant to read to a stopping point and say in their own words what it means, and tell the tester not to correct the participant and not to use yes or no questions. Usability testing suits longer documents and web sites where finding information matters, and the guidelines advise finding three people matching simple criteria, run in rounds. Controlled comparative studies collect statistics at scale after the smaller methods. Focus groups are said to be more useful before writing than for testing.

### Replication and criticism
The document is a consensus standard of practice. Most individual rules lean on legal-writing authorities (Garner, Kimble, Charrow and others) and on reading research, but a rule like "use contractions" is supported in the text by a single cited reading study, and others only by the authority of the cited writers. Reading research does support the general direction on sentence length and word familiarity (see dubay2004principles), but the authors themselves note that the document still bears the stamp of its origin in regulations and tell the writer to define "you" clearly. The concrete numbers on testing (6 to 9 paraphrase interviews, three usability participants) are practical rules of thumb, in line with small-sample usability practice, and not statistical power calculations.

### Implications for LTF
The guidelines are a ready-made style spine for LTF copy. The rules that LTF's own conventions already follow (second person, active voice, short sentences, one topic per paragraph) have an official source here, and the examples show the pattern for a pilot audience. Three more are worth adopting explicitly: use the same term consistently for a specific thing (do not alternate between "airspeed", "speed" and "IAS" in one lesson, and define each once), put the main idea before exceptions and conditions (say what to do, then when it does not apply), and avoid slashes, which hide whether two things are alternatives or one thing. The "must, must not, may, should" scale is useful for the content pipeline: a lesson can distinguish requirement from recommendation using those four words only, and flight-training facts that are regulatory limits should say "must" only where a source says so. Finally, adopt paraphrase testing for any lesson with a high-stakes explanation: six beginners read a section, stop at each cue and say it back in their own words; every misunderstanding is a defect in the text.

## uscongress2010plain

United States Congress. (2010). Plain Writing Act of 2010, Public Law 111-274, 124 Stat. 2861. Enacted 13 October 2010.

### Core claims
The Act is U.S. law, not research. It requires federal agencies to use "plain writing" in covered documents, which are documents that people need to obtain a federal benefit or service or to file taxes, documents that give information about a benefit or service, and documents that explain how to comply with a federal requirement. It is the clearest legal statement in the United States of what plain writing means, and it set the expectation that public-facing content is written for its audience. It matters to a design knowledge base for two reasons: it gives a short authoritative definition that is useful for style rules, and it shows that plain language is about outcomes for the reader, not about a score.

### Specifics
The definition in the Act (section 3, as given in the GovInfo text I fetched this session) is that plain writing is writing that is clear, concise, well-organized, and follows other best practices appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience. The purpose section says the Act aims to make agencies more effective and accountable to the public by promoting government communication that the public can understand and use. Covered documents include letters, publications, forms, notices and instructions in paper or electronic form; regulations are excluded. Section 4 requires each agency to designate senior officials to oversee implementation, communicate and train employees in plain writing, set up a process for oversight of compliance, maintain a plain writing section on its website linked from the home page, designate points of contact to receive and respond to public input, and use plain writing in every covered document it issues or substantially revises. The Director of the Office of Management and Budget was required to issue guidance, and until then agencies could use the guidelines of the Plain Language Action and Information Network (see plain2011federal). Section 5 required an initial compliance plan and annual compliance reports published on the agency's plain writing page. Section 6 says the Act is not subject to judicial review and creates no enforceable rights, so compliance rests on reporting and public pressure and not on lawsuits.

The current implementation advice, hosted by the General Services Administration at digital.gov/guides/plain-language, adds statements useful for writers. It describes plain language as content that is "clear and easy to understand" and organizes the advice into principles, writing, design and testing. Its principles page repeats that "The first rule of plain language is: write for your audience" and that plain language does not mean dumbing down. Its writing page advises shorter words, short sections, active voice and present tense, and gives the example "Not 'It must be done,' but 'You must do it.'" It lists hidden verbs as a cause of wordiness ("We analyze data" against "We conduct an analysis of the data"). Its design page says to emphasize only what matters, "otherwise you'll dilute its impact," and to avoid underlining (readers expect it on links) and all capital letters. Its testing page names paraphrase testing, usability testing and controlled comparative studies, and says "Plan to test at least twice".

### Replication and criticism
Because it is a statute, there is nothing to replicate. The Act has no judicial enforcement by design (section 6), and agencies publish their own compliance reports (section 5), so its effect depends on how seriously each agency takes it; I did not find an independent evaluation this session. The Act's own definition is deliberately open ("best practices appropriate to the subject or field and intended audience"), which is a strength for flexibility and a weakness for measurement: it cannot be verified with a formula, and agencies are left to decide what clear means. This agrees with the research view in redish2000readability and dubay2004principles that no single score captures it.

### Implications for LTF
LTF is not covered by the Act, but the definition works as a one-sentence test for any lesson, quiz or message: is it clear, concise, well organized and appropriate to this audience and subject? The audience clause matters: aviation has legitimate technical vocabulary, and "plain" does not mean removing "airspeed indicator" or the precise phrase a radio call requires. The Act's emphasis on documents needed to do something is also a useful frame for challenge briefings and checklists, which are the LTF documents closest to a form or an instruction. The testing guidance (test at least twice, with real readers, then revise) should be built into the content workflow before a lesson is published.

## govuk2026writing

Government Digital Service. (2026). Writing to GOV.UK standards: "Use clear language" guideline and the A to Z style guide. GOV.UK Content and publishing guidance (guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk). Read November 2026; the pages carry no edition date.

### Core claims
GOV.UK's content guidance is the most developed public writing standard for a large transactional website. It makes plain English mandatory across the site so that content is "accessible and understandable to everyone who needs it", states that its rules are based on research into how people read online and use GOV.UK, and claims that the guidance applies to specialist audiences too. It is a standard of practice and an evidence-informed style guide, not a study. For LTF it offers concrete, checkable rules and an unusually transparent rationale for each rule.

### Specifics
The "Use clear language" guideline (guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/) gives the following. Plain English is mandatory for all of GOV.UK. Specialist readers still prefer it: the page cites research on legal language reporting that 80% of people preferred sentences written in clear English and that the preference grew as issues became more complex, and states that more educated and more specialist readers prefer plain English more. It cites Literacy Trust figures showing that 1 in 6 adults in England have very poor literacy (with comparable figures for Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland and a note that definitions vary by nation). Specialist terms are allowed but must be explained the first time they appear. Jargon and buzzwords are to be avoided: describe what you are actually doing. Prefer short familiar words ("buy" for "purchase", "help" for "assist", "about" for "approximately"), note that words ending in "-ion" or "-ment" tend to lengthen sentences, and use the words users know and search for. Requirements are to be expressed as: "must" for legal requirements, "need" for administrative steps without criminal consequences, "can" for optional actions, and not "may be able to". Active voice is preferred because it is direct and keeps sentences short, but passive is acceptable when the outcome matters more than who acts. Paragraphs should be no more than five sentences and sentences longer than 25 words should be split (the page gives these as rules, without a stated experiment behind them).

The A to Z style guide (guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/style-guides/a-to-z-style-guide/) adds entry-level rules. Do not use "click" for user interface actions "because not all users click" (use "select"). Avoid negative contractions such as "can't" and "don't" (the clear-language page also marks "should've", "could've", "would've" and "they've" as hard to read, while allowing others such as "you'll"). Bulleted lists need a lead-in line, steps use numbers, and the first time an abbreviation is used on a page it is explained in full, except for well-known ones such as BBC, NHS and UK. "There's usually no need to say 'please' or 'please note'." Metaphors are discouraged because they slow comprehension. Spelling and conventions are UK English.

### Replication and criticism
The rules are partly grounded in published research (readability, legal language comprehension) and partly in GOV.UK's analytics and testing, which are not published in full on these pages, so individual numbers cannot be audited from them. The 25-word and five-sentence limits are house rules and should not be read as research findings. Some rules conflict with other plain language guidance: the U.S. Federal Plain Language Guidelines recommend contractions where they sound natural (plain2011federal), while GOV.UK warns against negative contractions as harder to read. The two bodies base their advice on different readers and, in each case, on their own testing, so neither is a general law. Evidence on contractions in reading research is mixed in the sources I read this session, so LTF should choose a rule and test it with its own learners. The guide is written for UK English and an average UK adult whose first language is English, so it does not directly address non-native readers (a UK Home Office design page on limited English exists, which I did not read).

### Implications for LTF
Several rules transfer cleanly. The "click" rule matters because LTF learners will use a mouse, keyboard, joystick or touch; write "select" or "press" for interface verbs, and name the physical control only where it matters (in the sim, "press" is the verb for keys and "move" for a stick). Lead-in lines for lists, numbered steps for procedures and explaining abbreviations on first use per page are directly applicable to lessons and checklists; because each LTF page may be opened from a link, the abbreviation rule should apply per page, not per course. The modal-verb scale (must, need, can) maps well to training copy: say "must" only for regulatory or hard limits, and use "should" or "can" for guidance, so that a learner is not led to think a technique is a legal requirement. The explain-specialist-terms rule is the right model for aviation vocabulary: define a term on first use, keep it, do not avoid it. LTF should decide its own contraction policy in a style guide and test it; for a conversational second-person product, positive contractions such as "you'll" are low-risk and negative ones in warnings or limits ("don't exceed") are the case to test, since misreading a negation in a safety statement costs more than stiffness does.

## kimble2016proof

Kimble, J. (2016). The Proof Is in the Reading: Solid Evidence That Plain Language Works Best. Michigan Bar Journal, October 2016, pp. 52-54 ("Plain Language" column).

### Core claims
Kimble, a long-time plain language scholar and drafter of the federal court rules restyling, summarizes four studies from the 50 he collects in his 2012 book Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please (Carolina Academic Press). Of the 50, 18 involve legal documents and readers include judges, lawyers, administrators and the general public. His claim, stated in the column's opening, is that "readers strongly prefer plain language to legalese, understand it better and faster, are more likely to comply with it, and are more likely to read it to begin with". The column is advocacy by an expert, but it reports method and numbers, so each study can be examined.

### Specifics
Study 1 is Kimble's own survey from the mid-1990s: an original and a revised version of a short appellate opinion were sent to a random selection of 700 Michigan lawyers, half seeing the original first and half the revision first. The revision opened with a summary of the crucial facts, the issue and the answer, used short sections with informative headings and topic sentences, shortened average sentence length from 25 words to 19, and omitted unnecessary cases, detail and words. Of 251 lawyers who responded, 153 (61 percent) preferred the revised opinion. They rated the original at an average of 6 and the revision at 7 on a 1-to-10 scale, and the top two reasons were that the revision left out a lot of unnecessary detail and had a summary at the beginning.

Study 2 is Flammer's survey of judges (published 2010). It sent 800 surveys, 200 to each of four groups (federal trial, federal appellate, state trial and state appellate judges), and 292 judges responded. Each judge saw the original 3.25-page motion response and either a plain-English version or a more informal plain-English version, in either order, without labels. The plain version was shorter (2.5 pages), dropped underlining and all-caps headings, put the four reasons in a vertical list, used topic sentences and averaged 17.8 words per sentence against 25.2. Judges preferred it to the original 66 percent to 34 percent, with no differences by court level, state or federal, rural or urban, gender or experience. The informal version, which added contractions, first person and a conversational tone, was preferred by 58 percent, and Kimble reports the author's suggestion, from judges' comments, that the contractions were the main reason for the 8 point drop.

Study 3 is a 2005 California study by Mindlin of two court forms (a proof of service and a subpoena): 60 volunteers were divided into two groups, with each group seeing one original and one plain-language form, and answered 10 questions read aloud with 20 seconds each. Accuracy was 81 percent on the plain-language proof of service against 61 percent on the original, and 95 percent against 65 percent on the subpoena. Study 4 is a Document Design Center test for the Federal Communications Commission's marine radio rules for recreational boats from the early 1980s: readers of the old rules got an average of 10.66 of 20 questions right and readers of the new rules 16.85; mean response time fell from 2.97 to 1.62 minutes; and difficulty ratings (1 very easy to 5 very hard) fell from 4.59 to 1.88. Kimble notes that the hardest principle in the revision was restraint: "select only the content that the audience needs."

### Replication and criticism
These are four hand-picked studies from the author's own collection, selected to make the case, so the column cannot show how many studies had weaker or null results. Several measure preference and not comprehension (the opinion and motion studies), and preference by professionals for a briefer, better organized document may reflect convenience, not understanding. The comprehension studies (forms and FCC rules) are better tests but have modest samples and were run by people who favored the revision. In the studies Kimble describes, plain language was bundled with other changes (summaries at the start, headings, lists, cuts), so the effect cannot be assigned to word choice or sentence length alone. A review of the second edition of the book in the Michigan Bar Journal (November 2024) praises its updated evidence, while a separate secondary source says the second edition adds more summaries; I did not read either in full. DuBay (dubay2004principles) reports a more mixed picture for word-level and sentence-level simplification alone (the Duffy and Kabance experiments), which is consistent with Kimble's emphasis on structure, content selection and testing and not on formulas.

### Implications for LTF
The most transferable lesson is that the large gains come from structure and selection, not from synonym swaps: start with a summary of the point, use informative headings, put key reasons in a list, and cut content the reader does not need. The FCC example is directly relevant to a training product that wants to teach recreational flying, and it parallels LTF's decision to teach the Cessna 172 only: the revision removed rules for other audiences and edge cases. For LTF lessons, that suggests a summary of "what you will do and why" at the top of each lesson, and moving rare exceptions (weather minima, unusual configurations) into a clearly separate reference section. The contraction result is a reminder to test tone rather than assume it: a conversational style that some readers like cost some preference among judges, although the audience (judges) is not LTF's. Always pair a plain rewrite with a comprehension check, such as the 10-question read-aloud method in the court forms study, which LTF could adapt as a quiz placed after the explanation to see whether beginners understood.
