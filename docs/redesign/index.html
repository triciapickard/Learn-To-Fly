Each lesson has one job. You learn a single idea on the page, then go and prove it in the airplane while it’s fresh.

10 minutes at your desk

Understand it

  • Plain explanations of why the airplane does what it does.
  • Diagrams you can drag: turn the wind, bank the wings, move the needle.
  • Two or three questions to check it stuck.

20 minutes in the sim

Fly it

  • A flight card tells you exactly how to set up the sim.
  • Your phone sits beside the screen with the steps and a stopwatch in big type.
  • After you land, you mark the flight and it goes in your logbook.

Three things on your desk.

The simulator

Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024, any edition, on PC or Xbox. The Skyhawk and every airport in the course come with it. Older versions and other sims won’t match the lessons.

Something to steer with

A gamepad does the whole course, and lesson 0.2 sets it up with you. If you own a joystick, or a yoke and pedals, use them.

A second screen

A phone, tablet or laptop next to the sim, so the steps stay in view while your hands are busy.

One thing to be straight about: this teaches you to fly a simulator. It follows real training standards so the habits are good ones, but none of it counts toward a pilot’s licence. For that you need a real instructor in a real airplane.

Your first flight

Take off from Livermore, fly some gentle turns over the valley and come back. No scores, just the feel of flying.

Lesson 0.3. A 10 minute read. Checked in MSFS 2024, version 1.8.16.0, on 28 September 2026.

What to expect

This first flight is about getting a feel for the airplane. There’s no score to worry about. The flight passes as long as you take off and make a few turns. You’ll learn the proper technique for everything you do today in later modules.

Expect the airplane to feel twitchy at first. That’s normal. Everyone over-controls on their first flight.

From the right seat

Make a small input, wait, then make another small input. The airplane needs a moment to respond to each movement you make.

Five things to watch

  1. The horizon. Where it sits in the windshield tells you whether the nose is up, down or level, and whether the wings are level.
  2. Airspeed. The tape on the left of the PFD, the big screen in front of you, shows it in knots.
  3. Altitude. The tape on the right shows it in feet above sea level.
  4. Heading. The compass at the bottom of the PFD shows which way the nose points.
  5. The runway. On the way out and on the way back, know where it is.

Look outside most of the time. Glance at the instruments, then look back outside.

Step by step

From the right seat

Attitude and power work together. Leave the throttle at full and the airplane keeps climbing slowly, even with the nose level. Pull the power too far back and it sinks. At about 2,300 RPM, the level picture gives level flight.

Only in the sim In real life the Altamont Pass hills are lined with wind turbines. The sim’s scenery may not show them, so use the gap in the hills and the freeway instead.
At what airspeed do you raise the nose in the Cessna 172?
You raise the nose at 55 knots. Then you climb at about 74 knots, the speed that gains height fastest.

Coming back

When you’re done, you have two choices:

  • End the flight from the pause menu. That is fine for today.
  • Try to land. Fly back toward the airport, line up with a runway from a long way out, reduce power and descend gently. Don’t worry if it isn’t pretty. Module 4 teaches landing.

If things start to feel like too much at any point, pause. Take a breath, look at where you are, read the next step, then unpause.

What should you do if you feel overwhelmed during a flight?
Pause. It freezes everything so you can look around, re-read the steps and make a plan. You can’t pause a real airplane, so use it freely while you learn.
